## 1. What slides are for
In a live presentation the speaker is the show, and the slides are visual aids that keep it moving. The classic failure is the "visual recitation": dozens of dense bullet lists that the presenter reads aloud. To avoid it, separate what you **say** (full reasoning and transitions, which go in the speaker notes) from what you **show** (the point and its evidence).

There are two kinds of slide:
- **Text slides** show the structure (roadmap, summary) or emphasize key thought groups (conclusions, recommendations, next steps).
- **Exhibit slides** (charts, tables, diagrams) show relationships that words alone can't make clear.

The book's ideal mix is heavily weighted to exhibits: about 90% exhibits and 10% text. Treat that as a direction to lean in. Management decks with mostly qualitative content will carry more text slides. Even then, prefer a text slide designed as a diagram, showing how its ideas relate, over a plain bullet list.

## 2. Mapping the pyramid to slides
- **Introduction:** Situation point(s), then Complication point(s). Then the governing thought together with the Key Line as the roadmap or executive-summary slide.
- **For each Key Line point:** one slide stating the point with its support points, used as a section opener. Then one slide per support point, with the support point as the sentence title over a graphic or set of graphics.
- Go down only as far as the audience needs, usually the Key Line plus one level. Deeper evidence goes to the appendix.
- **Close:** restate the governing thought and Key Line, then the ask or next steps.

## 3. Text-slide rules
1. Present and support **one idea at a time**. The exception is an enumerating slide such as a summary or roadmap.
2. **Use statements, not captions.** "Sales outlook is favorable" beats "Sales outlook". A statement leaves no room for the audience to assume the wrong point.
3. **Keep it brief:** about 6 lines or 30 words at most. If you need more, use more slides.
4. **Use simple words and rounded numbers.** "$4.9M" is easier to take in than "$4,876,987".
5. **Make it readable from the back.** Rule of thumb: the viewing distance in feet divided by 32 gives the minimum letter height in inches. On a 16:9 slide, that usually means body text of at least 24pt.
6. **Make text slides interesting to look at.** Treat them as word-exhibits that show relationships through layout, such as a tree, a flow, or a 2×2.
7. **Use builds** to reveal a complex slide piece by piece. In pptx, either split the slide into sequential slides or note the build in the speaker notes.
8. Don't spend slides on transitions or introductions. Say those aloud. The consequence is that a live deck won't stand alone as a handout. If a handout is needed, pair each slide with outline-form notes, or make a separate read-ahead version.

## 4. Exhibit slides
Decide the **question** the exhibit answers. Put the **answer** as the title. Then pick the chart form that shows exactly that. Titles must be full sentences, or phrases with a verb: "Western Region accounts for almost half of profits", not "Profit by region". A message title also lets you check that the picture actually shows the claim.

| The exhibit answers… | Comparison type | Default chart |
|---|---|---|
| What are the elements? | Structure | Diagram: org chart, process flow, system map |
| How do amounts compare **to the whole**? | Component | Pie (≤5 slices) or 100% stacked bar |
| How do amounts compare **to each other**? | Item | Horizontal bar, sorted |
| What changed / how has it changed **over time**? | Time series | Column (few points) or line (many points) |
| How are items **distributed**? | Frequency | Histogram (column) |
| How do items **co-relate**? | Correlation | Scatter, or paired bars |

Keep exhibits simple enough to read in seconds. Use at most one or two complex, build-up diagrams per presentation. Use colour to highlight the one bar, slice, or line that the title talks about, and grey out the rest.

## 5. Storyboard and script procedure
1. **Write the introduction in full,** every word in the order it will be spoken. This also re-validates the Question for this audience.
2. **Lay out a blank storyboard.** Across the top of each slide box, write the point to be illustrated: the introduction points worth visualizing, every Key Line point, and every point one level below the Key Line.
3. **Rough out each visual.** Name the chart type and the data it needs, not the real numbers yet, plus a note on the relationship to show. Unknown data becomes `[DATA NEEDED: …]`.
4. **Script the words around each slide** so the set flows as a story. Transitions live in the script.
5. **Complete the slide design and build.**
6. **Rehearse, rehearse, rehearse.**

Storyboard row format (see `assets/storyline-template.md`):

`# | Type (text/exhibit) | Action title | Visual sketch / chart + data | Pyramid ref (e.g. 2.1)`

## 6. Showing structure and transitions
The same principles apply to written documents, read-ahead decks, and appendices.
- Make the hierarchy visible. Headings or dividers should mirror the divisions in the pyramid, and wording at the same level should be parallel.
- Introduce the Key Line up front with the roadmap. Give each section a one-line mini-introduction.
- Link sections verbally. Refer back to the previous point ("Having stabilized service, the next priority is cost"). Summarize long sections. End with the full conclusion and the concrete next steps.
