## 1. Why a pyramid
Readers and listeners automatically try to group incoming ideas and find the pattern that links them. They can hold only a handful of items at once. The traditional limit is about seven, and three or four is far more comfortable. If you hand them the governing idea first and then the supporting groups, they don't have to reconstruct the structure themselves. Major, abstract ideas should therefore arrive before the minor, supporting ones.

## 2. The three rules
1. **Ideas at any level summarize the ideas grouped below them.**
2. **Ideas in each grouping are always the same kind of idea.** You should be able to name them with a single plural noun: reasons, steps, problems, recommendations, and so on.
3. **Ideas in each grouping are always in logical order.** That order is time, structure, or degree (importance).

## 3. Vertical logic
Every box holds an *idea*: a statement that tells the audience something new and so raises a question in their mind (why? how? which? so what?). The level below answers exactly that question, and nothing else. The top box answers the Question that the introduction raised. When you present top-down, you are running a question-and-answer dialogue with the audience.

Test: read a point, predict the audience's question, and check that the points directly beneath it answer it. If a support point answers a *different* question, it belongs elsewhere or the parent is worded wrongly.

## 4. Horizontal logic
A grouping answers its parent's question in one of two ways, never both at once.

**Deductive** (a line of reasoning): a statement about the world, then a statement commenting on the first one's subject or predicate, then the implication of the two being true together. You summarize it by taking the final point and adding "because" to cover the first two. Deduction is heavier for audiences because they must hold each step in mind. Keep chains to four steps or fewer, and avoid it at the Key Line where you can.

**Inductive** (a grouping of like ideas): several ideas of the same kind, summarized by the inference their similarity implies. Prefer induction at the Key Line. Something you *discovered* deductively can usually be *presented* inductively. For example: "Buy the warehouse: it is the cheapest option, it is available now, it fits our expansion plans."

Check the inference: an inductive summary must not claim more than the grouping supports.

## 5. Logical order
The order comes from how the grouping was formed:

- **Time (step) order.** The ideas came from visualizing a process or a cause-and-effect sequence. Actions always go here. The first step comes first.
- **Structural order.** The ideas came from dividing a whole into parts, such as an org chart, a geography, a process map, or a value chain. Present the parts in the order the structure implies: the flow direction, or big to small.
- **Degree (importance) order.** The ideas came from forming a class of things that share a characteristic, such as three reasons or four problems. Put the most important or strongest first.

If you can't find any of these orders, either the ideas don't really belong together or the thinking is incomplete. Treat that as a signal to ask the user something.

## 6. MECE and summarizing
You can only summarize a grouping honestly if it is **MECE**: the ideas are Mutually Exclusive (no overlap) and Collectively Exhaustive (nothing missing relative to the parent).

**Avoid intellectually blank assertions.** "There are three problems" or "We have four recommendations" say nothing and hide unfinished thinking. Replace them with the specific insight.

**Action ideas** (steps, recommendations):
- Word each one so specifically that it implies an end result you could check. "Cut order processing time to 2 days" is better than "Improve order processing".
- Separate levels of abstraction. If one action must happen before another, or in order to achieve another, it belongs at a lower level.
- Group actions that produce the same effect, and summarize them by stating the *direct effect* of carrying them out.

**Situation ideas** (findings, facts, conclusions):
- Look for what they share: the same subject, the same action or verb, the same object, or the same implication.
- Name the narrow category that only these ideas fit into.
- Summarize with the *inference* the similarity implies. That is the "so what".

## 7. Building the pyramid
**Top-down** (try this first):
1. Name the subject.
2. Decide the Question you want answered in the audience's mind.
3. Write the Answer, or note that you can answer it.
4. State the Situation: the first uncontroversial thing you can say about the subject.
5. Develop the Complication: what happened in that situation that raises the question.
6. Recheck that the Complication really raises your Question and that your Answer answers it. Adjust whichever of them is wrong.
7. Find the new question the Answer raises. Decide whether you will answer it inductively or deductively, and name the plural noun if inductive.
8. Repeat the question-and-answer step at each lower level.

**Bottom-up** (when the governing thought is unclear, or you have a pile of material):
1. List every point you think you want to make.
2. Work out the relationships between them. Group like with like and spot causes and effects.
3. Draw a conclusion for each group, then a conclusion from those conclusions.
4. Work backwards to the introduction: what S and C make this Answer the response?

## 8. Caveats for beginners
- Always try top-down first. Don't draft slides to "get it all down" and structure them later.
- Start your thinking about the introduction from the Situation. It makes the right Complication and Question easier to find. On the slides you may then order S, C, Q, and A for effect.
- Don't skip the introduction because the main point feels obvious. Skipping it pushes introductory material down into the body and creates unwieldy deductive arguments.
- Historical chronology ("what happened") belongs in the introduction. The body holds only ideas, meaning claims, causes, and implications.
- Put nothing in the introduction that the audience won't accept as true. Put nothing in the body that the audience already knows. Either mistake distorts the Question.
- Prefer induction to deduction at the Key Line.
