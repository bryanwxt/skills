## 1. Decide what you want
Do you want to help people succeed in the world as it is, or to change the world? Be honest; many efforts want improvement without anything important changing. Teaching doesn't happen in isolation — what happens to learners outside class matters.

## 2. Communities of practice
- A **community of practice** is a group bound by shared interest in an activity. Newcomers enter through **legitimate peripheral participation**: small, low-risk tasks that still count as real contributions (proofreading, setting up rooms). Provide a ramp, not a cliff.
- Healthy communities: solve problems together, answer requests for information, share experience, reuse assets (templates, proposals), coordinate (shared purchases), build arguments for leadership, test plans, discuss new developments, document recurring solutions, visit each other, map who knows what and identify gaps.
- Four kinds: **action** (shared goal), **concern** (shared problem), **interest** (shared passion), **place** (shared location). Real ones mix them; pick a primary focus.

## 3. Join before you found
Consider joining an existing organization first (e.g. established coding-education groups). You start with colleagues, learn how things run, and avoid duplicating effort. Read organizing literature before starting.

## 4. Recruitment, retention, retirement
**Recruitment**
- Make it easy to find you and clear what you do and what volunteers get from it; tell stories.
- **Your best recruits are your own learners.** End every class with a sentence or two on how people can help and that help is welcome ("see one, do one, teach one"). Recent learners also counter the group's expert blind spot.
- **Start small** — people who've done you one small favour are likelier to do another (fix a typo, improve an exercise). Maintainable materials make this possible.
- Newcomers do **group tasks before solo tasks**, to learn norms and make friends.
- Assign **proactive mentors** who make introductions, explain the unwritten rules, and check in every few weeks.

**Retention**
- People should enjoy it ("if your people aren't having a ball, something is wrong") — enjoyment can be quiet satisfaction.
- **Ask** what people want to do, what they're comfortable doing, and how much time they have — "anything!" hides real preferences.
- Offer **many ways to contribute**; the more you do yourself, the fewer openings for others.
- **Recognize contributions** publicly and privately.
- **Make space:** don't micromanage or reply to everything first; let peer collaboration grow.
- **Train people** in transferable skills (meetings, budgets, grant writing, conflict resolution), ideally via partners who already do this.
- Spend an hour or two a month **finding and keeping allies** (ask for advice). Local government bodies can provide space, money, publicity, and connections.
- **"Soup, then hymns":** deliver useful things before writing manifestos.
- **Pay nothing or a real wage — never a token amount.**
- Help difficult personalities contribute (the know-it-all, the indecisive, the knowledge hoarder, the quiet one, the shirker, the last-minute one, the rude one) — each needs a different approach.

**Retirement**
- Ask people to make leaving explicit and make sure they don't feel ashamed.
- Give them a way to pass on knowledge (mentor a successor, be interviewed).
- Make sure they **hand over the keys** (accounts, bookings, contacts).
- Follow up after 2–3 months; thank them when they leave and at the next gathering.

## 5. Governance
- Every group has a power structure; the choice is between **formal and accountable** or **informal and unaccountable**. Write the rules down and publish them (who can use the name, who decides on charging for teaching, and so on).
- Legal forms vary by jurisdiction (company, non-profit, commons); get local advice.
- A **commons** — a resource managed by a community under rules it evolved — is one attractive model.
- Boards: a **service board** also does operational work; a **governance board** hires, monitors, and if necessary fires the director. Choose board members for **competence** (accounting, marketing) over passion.
- **Don't write a constitution at the start** — formalize what you already do, later. When you formalize, make it democratic; appointed boards drift from the community.
- Self-audit questions (answer individually, then compare): stated vs actual values; meeting and decision procedures; desired size; staffing and pay; money sources and priorities; membership rules.

## 6. Marketing
Marketing is understanding what others want and how you can meet it — the same skill as lesson design.
- **What are you offering, to whom?** The real "product" may differ from the obvious one (for a free workshop: grant proposals, alumni for sponsors to hire, a line in a civic report, volunteers' satisfaction).
- **Stakeholder personas**, then an **elevator pitch** for each: *For [audience] who [are dissatisfied with what's available], our [category] provides [key benefit]. Unlike [alternatives], our program [distinguishing feature].* Use it to shape your website, not as literal copy.
- If you don't know why a stakeholder would care, ask; if you think you know, ask anyway. People aren't purely economic — accomplishment, control, and community matter.
- **Brand** = first reaction when your name comes up. **Positioning** = what makes you different (the "unlike" clause).
- **People ask for data but believe stories:** props (a learner's project), short videos about learners, your founding story. Stories are free to share — important for groups with little money.
- **Findability:** search for yourself using the terms your audience would use; the website should lead with where, when, what, how to join, and how it changes lives — not the org chart. Use notice boards in libraries, schools, community centres, and shops for people who aren't online.
- **Referrals:** send people to better-suited groups; they'll return the favour.
- **Cold emails:** name a specific point of connection; mild urgency; how you'll make their life better; specific offer; credibility (track record, backers); terms (fees, travel, reserved seats); a plain, short subject line (no caps, no "free", no exclamation marks); keep it short. Roughly half reply, half of those talk, half of those book — so 10–15%, far above typical cold-call rates, but still expect rejection.
- If you can't be first in a category, create a new one — or join someone else's effort.
- Anticipate **passive resistance** (endless delay, endless objections) and plan responses.
- Adult learners manage time better, bring experience, learn by choice, and can tell productive struggle from plain struggle — pitch accordingly. Don't forget "conversational programmers" who need to talk with programmers, not program.

## 7. Partnerships with schools and others
- Most people would rather fail than change. Faculty resist new methods mainly for fear of looking foolish in front of students, worry about course evaluations during the transition, and wanting to teach like their own inspiring teachers. Address these directly.
- How CS faculty adopt practices: they hear about them while solving a problem, through institutional initiatives, colleagues, or conferences; they try them because of incentives, their own cost/benefit judgment, role models, or trusted peers' success; concerns include the initial slowdown, room layout, and scepticism of education research; they **keep** practices mainly because of positive student feedback.
- Evidence-based practice spreads slowly (medicine averages 17 years). **Implementation science** studies how to speed adoption.
- **Strategy:** find out what people believe they need (listen first); change incrementally within the institution's frameworks (e.g. shorter, targeted teacher certifications can multiply the teacher supply without lowering quality).
- **Change strategies** form a 2×2: individual vs system × prescribed (top-down) vs emergent (bottom-up). Outsiders usually start in individual/emergent — helping teachers reflect, and often live-coding *with* teachers so they learn to program. They may know more about teaching than you.
- **Bootcamps:** 1–6 month programmes for career changers, quality varies widely. Graduates face costs, stigma, unexplained rejections, jargon barriers, impostor syndrome, and not fitting in. Employers value soft skills and bootcamp grads' maturity. Approach bootcamps offering **teaching** expertise, since their staff are usually programmers; start with low-cost evidence-based practices and small wins.
- **Change patterns:** celebrate small successes; keep the idea visible; hand out tokens; recruit a "champion sceptic" whose critiques improve the plan.
- **How to ensure a practice is NOT adopted** (avoid these): make it optional, skimp on training, never use it on real work, never integrate it, use it sporadically, bury it in a quality initiative, marginalize its champion, seize on early missteps, invest little, exploit fear and inertia.
- **Be willing to change your goals** based on what the people you're helping need — a spreadsheet lesson may help them more than a programming one.

## 8. Meetings and post mortems
**Meeting rules:**
1. Decide whether a meeting is needed (information sharing → send it in writing).
2. Write an agenda — if nobody will, don't meet.
3. Time each item (and revise estimates upward).
4. Prioritize high-impact, low-time items.
5. One chair keeps things moving and talks less than most.
6. Require politeness; defer off-topic items.
7. No devices except for accessibility.
8. No interruptions — signal to speak; the chair keeps the queue.
9. Minutes by someone other than the chair: key points, **every decision and every task**.
10. Take personal notes.
11. End early (10:55 for an 11:00 end). Circulate minutes immediately; turn tasks into tickets and start the next meeting by reviewing them.
**Equalizers:** three sticky notes per person, surrendered each time they speak, reset when everyone has used one; "interruption bingo" tallying who interrupts whom.
**Online:** use a chat queue for speaking turns; in large or tense meetings, mute everyone and let the moderator unmute.

**Post mortem** at the end of every project (not for blame):
- a moderator with no stake;
- one hour exactly; attendance required;
- go around the room: each person adds one item to **Do Again** and one to **Do Differently**;
- comment on actions and decisions, not individuals;
- turn the lists into concrete recommendations.

## 9. Looking after yourself
Burnout is a real risk. Train successors, then move on and let the thing you built change.
