## 1. What motivates
- Intrinsic motivation drives the best learning. **Competence, autonomy, relatedness** (self-determination theory).
- **Success motivates more than praise.** Give early wins.
- **Authentic tasks** with **tangible artifacts** — no `foo`/`bar`; use real-looking data and problems.
- **Prioritize by time-to-master × usefulness** — teach quick, useful things first.
- Bring in former learners who struggled and succeeded — people believe people like themselves.
- Retention across learners is driven by meaningful assignments, instructor interaction, and collaboration on work (the last two need extra effort online).
- Grades exist; design with them in mind and reduce incentives to cheat.
- Teachers need motivation too — co-teaching helps.

## 2. What demotivates
The three biggest demotivators for adults: **unpredictability** (effort doesn't reliably lead to results), **indifference** (the teacher doesn't seem to care), **unfairness** (even when it benefits them). Taken far enough: learned helplessness.

Specific things to avoid:
- A contemptuous or holier-than-thou attitude (from teacher or peers).
- Dismissing learners' existing tools and skills (Windows, Excel, "outdated" frameworks).
- Deep technical tangents with the most advanced learners.
- Pretending to know more than you do — admitting limits builds trust.
- "Just", "obviously", "simply"; **feigned surprise** ("you don't know X?!").
- Installation and setup failures — each one confirms "this is too hard for me".
- Environments full of in-group signals (stereotypical decor, gendered wording) — neutral environments measurably increase interest from under-represented groups.
- "Fail fast, fail often" framing — celebrating failure is a privilege of people sure of a second chance. **Productive failure** (problems beyond current knowledge) can work, but only in a supportive culture.

Online help communities put off some learners (especially women) through unfamiliar site mechanics, feeling unqualified to answer, intimidating size, discomfort relying on strangers, and a sense that searching isn't "real work". Fixing these helps everyone.

## 3. Impostor syndrome and stereotype threat
- **Impostor syndrome:** believing success is luck and fearing exposure; common among high achievers and under-represented groups; fed by seeing only others' polished results.
  - Teachers can help by sharing their own mistakes (live-coding typos count) and explicitly inviting questions.
  - Individuals: talk about it with trusted people, avoid self-undermining phrases ("I'm not an expert, but…"), teach others, ask questions, build alliances, record accomplishments.
  - Communities: encourage, curb hostility, remove hidden barriers (document how to participate), leaders show their own learning, reward mentoring, critique work not people.
- **Stereotype threat:** reminders of negative stereotypes can lower performance. The evidence has complications, but unwelcoming climates clearly hurt. **Never imply some people are natural programmers.**
- **Mindset:** fixed-mindset beliefs hurt; growth-mindset interventions have small average effects, possibly larger for at-risk learners. Don't oversell.

## 4. Accessibility
Inaccessible material is profoundly demotivating and usually accidental. You can build a baseline without knowing learners' specific needs, and doing so signals care.
- **Involve people with disabilities** in decisions ("nothing about us without us").
- Use established do's-and-don'ts guides (e.g. for autism, screen readers, low vision, motor disabilities, hearing loss, dyslexia) and automated checkers (e.g. WebAIM).
- **Add one improvement per event;** start with easy wins: fonts, text size, step-free access to the room.
- **Visual design:** real headings and landmarks; never colour alone for meaning (use patterns or greyscale-distinct colours); remove unneeded elements rather than hiding them (screen readers read hidden content); allow self-pacing and repetition; narrate on-screen action in videos; provide code as text alongside video.
- **Curb-cut effect:** accessibility helps everyone (captions make video searchable).
- **Walk the route** from public transit to the room and washrooms, noting barriers.
- Allow **group sign-ups** so people can come with others they trust.
- **Spoon theory:** some learners have limited daily energy because of chronic illness or other burdens. Design for invisible obstacles and ask.

## 5. Inclusivity
Practical steps:
- Ask learners beforehand how the training can help them reach their goals.
- Check materials for gendered pronouns and include culturally diverse names.
- Emphasize **rate of learning**, not starting point.
- Encourage pair programming.
- Actively curb intimidating behaviour: jargon, show-off "questions".
- **Saving face:** identify topics learners might be embarrassed not to know, and give low-exposure ways to learn them.

Rethinking content:
- **Community representation** (role models and narratives from learners' communities) — risk: shallow projects that aren't real computing.
- **Computational integration** (ideas from the learners' culture built into the computing) — risk: cultural appropriation.
- When in doubt, ask learners and their community and give them control.

Move beyond the **deficit model**: under-representation (e.g. women's share of CS degrees in some countries is far below its 1980s peak and below other countries') has structural causes — marketing of home computers to boys, admissions restrictions, hiring for "cultural fit", preparatory privilege. Don't frame under-represented learners as lacking something. Learn to be an ally.

## 6. Code of conduct
- Every event (in person or online) should have one, with **reporting contacts** and **enforcement procedures**. The code's existence and enforcement matter more than its exact wording.
- A code of conduct is itself an accessibility measure for people who'd otherwise be marginalized.

## 7. Audit checklist
- [ ] Are examples and datasets authentic and culturally varied? Any `foo`/`bar`?
- [ ] Any "just", "simply", "obviously", or dismissive remarks about tools?
- [ ] Is the first win reachable within the first 15–30 minutes?
- [ ] Is setup tested on every OS, with a fallback?
- [ ] Are headings, contrast, font sizes, alt text, captions, and non-colour cues in place?
- [ ] Is code available as text, not only in images or video?
- [ ] Are pace and repetition under learner control where possible?
- [ ] Is the venue accessible (route, washrooms, seating, power)?
- [ ] Is there a code of conduct with a named contact and enforcement plan?
- [ ] Do marketing materials and names avoid gendered or in-group language?
- [ ] Are there ways to participate without public exposure (sticky notes, shared notes, anonymous feedback)?
- [ ] Is attention spread fairly (e.g. name sticky notes)?
- [ ] Are learners asked about their needs beforehand?
