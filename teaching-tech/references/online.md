## 1. What "online" means
Online isn't the same as automated. Live online teaching of a small group can feel like a seminar. Teaching hundreds at once forces standardized, automated assessment. Decide which you're doing.

## 2. MOOCs: promise and problems
- Two traditions: **connectivist** courses (learners build knowledge through networks) and hub-and-spoke, lecture-centred courses.
- Strengths: flexible timing, more courses available, lower costs.
- Weaknesses:
  - recorded content can't respond to an individual's misconception; there's only one explanation;
  - automated assessment works mainly at lower Bloom levels;
  - learners carry more of the burden of staying on track;
  - impersonality demotivates and invites incivility;
  - users skew affluent and already educated.
- Surveys of MOOCs and coding tutorials found decent presentation but weak design: bottom-up sequencing, shallow feedback, little explanation of when and why to use concepts, few common-error warnings, no personalization by goals.
- **Personalization** usually means adjusting pace (skipping after correct answers) — modest gains. Better: **multiple alternative explanations** for the hardest points.
- A good human teacher still adds measurable value that automation hasn't matched.

## 3. Practices that work
- **Frequent, well-publicized, enforced deadlines** to create rhythm.
- **Minimal whole-class synchronous activity** (scheduling conflicts), but **small groups meeting synchronously** (e.g. weekly discussion).
- **Learners contribute to shared knowledge:** shared notes, class scribes, contributed problems; let learners edit course notes and credit them.
- **A code of conduct**, publicized and enforced.
- **Many short episodes** rather than long lectures: lower load, more formative checks, cheaper to re-record than to patch.
- **Use video to engage, not to convey information** — people read faster than you talk. Exception: **showing actions** (using an editor, stepping through a debugger) via short screencasts.
- **Find misconceptions early** from data; add alternative explanations and exercises where learners struggle.
- **Increase instructor presence** — it matters more online.
- **Platform:** start with whatever learners already use and requires the least new learning; ask them. Tools that experts love (IRC, pull requests) can fail badly for non-experts.
- **Throttle** discussion (e.g. one message per person per thread per day) so everyone can be heard, not just the loudest.
- **Cheating** is not more frequent online day to day; exam impersonation is the real risk. Understand why people cheat before building surveillance.

## 4. Video
- **Keep videos to about 6 minutes or less.**
- Talking head + slides beats voice over slides; informal recordings can beat studio polish; **drawing on a tablet** engages more than slides or code screencasts; enthusiastic, fairly fast speech is fine. (Engagement isn't learning — measure that separately.)
- **Refutation and dialogue formats** — stating and correcting common misconceptions, or a learner–tutor conversation — produce larger gains than plain exposition, especially for learners with low prior knowledge, without hurting others.
- **Doing beats watching** — extra practice is worth far more than extra video.
- Small edits to video are expensive, so script and test before recording; keep episodes small so re-recording is cheap.
- Check followability by watching at high speed.

## 5. Flipped classrooms
- Learners watch or read at home; class time is for problems and discussion.
- In person, lessons start from the **intersection** of what everyone needs and expand on demand; online lessons must contain the **union**, because nobody is there to expand.
- Online learners complete fewer practice exercises than in-person learners attend lectures; completion predicts exam results in both. Test anxiety and self-efficacy matter more online — so does instructor presence.
- Lecture recordings don't reduce attendance; they help early-career students most.

## 6. Online community and peer learning
- A classroom has three worlds: public (teacher), social (peers), private (each learner's head). **The social world matters most** — facilitate peer interaction.
- Forum posts that show reasoning ("constructive") correlate with better grades; most learners ask few questions; procrastinators avoid forums and do worse. Don't expect a lively community by default; design for it.
- **Co-opetition:** publish a correct but inefficient solution; learners improve it in the open; credit goes to whoever contributes most. A two-stage version (individual, then pairs improve) improves understanding and enjoyment.
- **Peer grading** agrees with experts about as often as experts agree with each other, especially with structured rubrics and filtering careless responses. Use **calibrated peer review**.
- **Streaming live coding plus collaborative notes** comes close to being in the room.

## 7. Presence and hybrid setups
- **Social presence** (feeling with others) matters more than physical presence. Bring learners' everyday tech into the class (e.g. mobile in-class exercises).
- **Works:** live instruction streamed to **2–6 sites, each with local helpers**.
- **Fails:** one group in the room plus scattered remote individuals — the room gets the attention.
- For online meetings and discussions, run a text chat where people queue to speak, and let a moderator call on them.
