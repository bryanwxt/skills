# Building Community (with Meetings and Joining Our Community)

> Source: Greg Wilson, *Teaching Tech Together* (2018), Ch. 13 "Building Community" (§13.1–§13.6), appendix "Meetings, Meetings, Meetings" (including "Online Meetings" and "The Post Mortem"), and appendix "Joining Our Community" (§C.1–§C.3). Licensed CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/); condensed and restructured for skill use, with quotations marked. Rule IDs use prefix `COM`.

## When a skill needs this
- A community-building advisor helps someone decide whether to found a new teaching group or join an existing one, and plans how to recruit, keep, and retire volunteers.
- An organization-health reviewer audits a volunteer group's retention practices, impostor-syndrome risks, governance (board type, published rules), pay policy, and burnout risk.
- A meeting assistant writes agendas with timings, chairs or coaches a chair, takes minutes, runs an online meeting's speaking queue, or facilitates a project post mortem.
- A workshop planner picks a delivery format for instructor training (in person, online in groups, online as individuals, multi-week online).
- A contribution guide or maintainer assistant drafts a contributor covenant, contribution paths ("start small", "discuss, then edit"), and newcomer-friendly issue tags for a teaching commons.

## Concepts

### Chapter 13 introduction: why community, and what kind
- **Decide what you want first.** Many well-meaning people "want the world to be a better place, but don't actually want anything important to change." Many grassroots coding efforts teach people to get good jobs rather than empowering them to change the system that has shut them, and people like them, out of those jobs. The first and most important decision is whether you want to help people succeed in the world as it is, or to give them a way to make a better one (§13 intro).
- **One person can only do so much.** "Just as we learn best together, we teach best when we are teaching with other people", and the best way to achieve that is to build a community (§13 intro).
- **You sometimes have to fix several things to fix one** (Anu Partanen). Finland's teachers succeed because its citizens value equality of opportunity. Copying their methods without making sure children are well nourished, safe, and treated fairly by the courts is harder. You don't have to fix all of society's ills to teach programming, but you must understand, and be involved in, what happens to your learners outside class if you want the class to work (§13 intro).
- **Situated learning** [Weng2015]: legitimate peripheral participation leads people to become members of a community of practice. A community of practice is a group bound by interest in an activity (knitting, particle physics). Legitimate peripheral participation means simple, low-risk tasks the community still recognizes as valid: making your first scarf, stuffing envelopes during an election campaign, proof-reading open source documentation (§13 intro).
- **The newcomer-to-peer progression:** simplified tasks and tools, then similar tasks with more complex tools, then the work of advanced practitioners. Example: children play nursery rhymes on a recorder or ukulele, then simple songs on trumpet or saxophone in a band, then explore their own musical tastes. Healthy communities understand and support these progressions, and each step should give people "a ramp rather than a cliff" (§13 intro).
- **Eleven ways healthy communities of practice support members** (§13 intro), each with the book's example utterance:
  - Problem solving: "I'm stuck—Can we work on this design and brainstorm some ideas?"
  - Requests for information: "Where can I find the code to connect to the server?"
  - Seeking experience: "Has anyone dealt with a customer in this situation?"
  - Reusing assets: a proposal for an event website from last year that someone else can start from.
  - Coordination and synergy: "Can we combine our purchases of web hosting to get a discount?"
  - Building an argument: finding out how people in other companies do things, to persuade a CEO to make changes.
  - Growing confidence: "Before I do it, I'll run it through my community first to see what they think."
  - Discussing developments: "What do you think of the new work tracking system? Does it really help?"
  - Documenting projects: "We have faced this problem five times now. Let us write it down once and for all."
  - Visits: "Can we come and see your after-school program? We need to establish one in our city."
  - Mapping knowledge and identifying gaps: "Who knows what, and what are we missing? What other groups should we connect with?"
- **Learning is social, so teaching must be rooted in a community.** To be effective and sustainable, teaching needs to be rooted in a community; if one doesn't exist, you need to build one (§13 intro).
- **Four community types** (§13 intro): community of action (a shared goal, such as getting someone elected); community of concern (members brought together by a shared exercise, such as dealing with depression); community of interest (a shared love, such as backgammon or knitting); community of place (people who live or work side by side). Most real communities are mixes, such as people in Toronto who like teaching tech. "What matters is that you pick something and stick with it."

### §13.1 Learn, Then Do
- **First decide whether you need a new community at all**, or would be more effective joining an existing organization. Thousands of groups already teach tech skills, from the 4-H Club and literacy programs to non-profits like Black Girls Code and Bridge. Joining gives you a head start on teaching, immediate colleagues, and a chance to learn how to run things. Wilson hopes learning those skills will matter more to you than being able to say you founded or lead something new.
- **Either way, find out what has been done before.** You owe this to yourself and everyone who will work with you. Reading list as given: [Alin1989] (the best-known work on grassroots organizing); [Brow2007] and [Midw2010] (practical manuals rooted in decades of practice); [Adam1975] (a history of the Highlander Folk School, whose approach many successful groups have emulated); [Spal2014] (a guide to teaching adults by someone with deep roots in organizing); NonprofitReady.org (free professional development training).

### §13.2 Three Steps
- **Lifecycle:** everyone, including you, goes through recruitment, retention, and retirement from the organization. You don't need to worry about this when just starting, but it is worth thinking about "as soon as you have more than a couple of non-founders involved."
- **Recruitment via marketing:** marketing should make the organization findable and make its mission and its value to volunteers clear. Share stories that show the kind of help you want and stories about the people you help, and make clear there are many ways to get involved (see `14-marketing.md`).
- **Your own classes are the best source of recruits.** "See one, do one, teach one" has always worked for volunteer organizations. End every class or other encounter with two sentences explaining how people can help and that help is welcome. People recruited this way know what you do and have recent experience of receiving it, which helps the organization avoid **collective expert blind spot**.
- **Start Small (box).** "As Ben Franklin observed", a person who has done someone a favor is more likely to do them another than if they had received a favor from them. So ask for something small first. When teaching, a natural way is to ask people to submit fixes for typos or unclear wording in your lesson materials, or to suggest new exercises or examples. If the materials are written maintainably (§6.3, see `06-lesson-design.md`), contributors practice useful skills and you get a conversation that may lead to a new recruit.
- **Recruiting continues after someone first shows up.** Without follow-through, people come once or twice, decide it isn't for them, and disappear. Two remedies:
  - Have newcomers take part in group activities before doing anything on their own, so they learn how the organization does things and build social ties that keep them involved.
  - Give each newcomer a mentor, and make sure mentors mentor proactively. A mentor's most important jobs are **making introductions** and **explaining the unwritten rules**. Tell mentors these are their primary responsibilities and that they must report back to you every few weeks on what they have done.
- **Retention** gets its own section (§13.3).
- **Retirement:** sooner or later everyone moves on, including you. When someone leaves (quoted items from §13.2):
  - "Ask people to be explicit about their departure" so everyone knows they've actually left.
  - "Make sure they don't feel embarrassed or ashamed about leaving."
  - "Give them an opportunity to pass on their knowledge." For example, they could mentor someone for a few weeks as a last contribution, or be interviewed by someone who is staying, to collect stories worth retelling.
  - "Make sure they hand over the keys." Example: discovering six months later that the departed person was the only one who knew how to book the playing field for the annual softball game.
  - "Follow up 2–3 months after they leave" for further thoughts on what worked and what didn't, or advice they didn't think of, or were uncomfortable giving, on the way out.
  - "Thank them", both when they leave and the next time the group gets together.

### §13.3 Retention
- **People need to enjoy at least some of it.** Alinsky: "If your people aren't having a ball doing it, there is something very wrong." [Alin1989]. Members shouldn't expect to enjoy every moment, but if they enjoy none of it they won't stay. Enjoyment needn't be an annual party; people may enjoy cooking, coaching, or working quietly beside others.
- **Four things every organization should do so people get something they value** (§13.3):
  - **Ask people what they want rather than guessing.** Just as you are not your learners (§6.1), you probably differ from other members. Ask what they want to do, what they're comfortable doing (which may differ), and what constraints they have on their time. An initial "I don't know—anything!" usually turns, after a short conversation, into something like: they like interacting with people but would rather not manage the finances, or the reverse.
  - **Provide many ways to contribute.** The more ways there are to help, the more people can help. Someone who doesn't like standing in front of an audience may maintain the website or handle the accounts; someone who can't do anything else may proof-read lessons. "The more kinds of tasks you do yourself, the fewer opportunities there are for others to get involved."
  - **Recognize contributions**, publicly and privately: mention people in presentations, put them on the website, and so on.
  - **Make space.** Micromanaging or controlling everything centrally means people won't feel they have the autonomy to act, and they drift away. If you are "too engaged or too quick on the reply button", people have less chance to grow as members and to form horizontal collaborations, so the community stays centered on one or two people instead of becoming a highly connected network.
- **Provide training.** Organizations need committees, meetings, budgets, grant proposals, and dispute resolution. Most people are never taught these, any more than they are taught to teach. Training people in them makes the organization run more smoothly, and transferable skills are a powerful reason to get and stay involved. Don't provide the training yourself unless it's your specialty: many civic and community groups have such programs, and you can probably make a deal with one.
- **Cultivate allies.** Other groups may be useful to you, and you to them, now or later. Set aside "an hour or two every month" to find allies and maintain relationships. One way is to ask them for advice: how should you raise awareness? Where have they found space to run classes? What needs aren't being met, and could you meet them, alone or in partnership? Groups that have been around a few years will have useful advice, will be flattered to be asked, and will know who you are next time you call.
- **Government Matters (box).** Tech circles like to disparage government institutions as slow-moving dinosaurs, but in Wilson's experience they are no worse than companies of similar size. Your school board, library, and city councillor's office may offer space, funding, publicity, connections to other groups, help with red tape, and more.
- **Soup, Then Hymns (box).** Most people join a volunteer community to help and be helped, not to argue over the wording of a grand vision statement ("Most people who prefer the latter are only interested in arguing…"). Focus first on immediately useful things, such as what people can create that other members will use right away. Once the organization shows it can achieve small things, people will be more confident it is worth investing in bigger ones. That is the time for manifestos, because that's when you need values to guide growth and operations.
- **Paying people.** Eventually tasks like system administration and accounting need full-time paid staff. Then "either pay people nothing or pay them a proper wage, but not do anything in between." Unpaid, their reward is the satisfaction of doing good. A token payment takes that away without giving them the satisfaction of earning a living.
- **Impostor syndrome (subsection).** It thrives in communities with arbitrary, unnecessary standards, harsh criticism as the norm, and secrecy about how work actually gets done. The Ada Initiative's community-level guidelines (complementing the individual ones in §10.2, see `10-motivation-and-inclusion.md`), quoted as headings:
  - "Encourage people." "This is as simple as it is effective."
  - "Discourage hostility and bickering." Public, hostile, personal arguments are a breeding ground for impostor syndrome.
  - "Eliminate hidden barriers to participation." Explicitly welcome new students and colleagues, and thoroughly document how someone can take part in projects and events in your group and institution.
  - "As a leader, show your own uncertainties" and demonstrate your own learning process. Seeing respected leaders struggle, or admit they didn't know everything at the start, makes realistic self-assessment easier.
  - "Reward and encourage people for mentoring newcomers." Officially make mentoring an important criterion for career advancement.
  - "Don't make it personal when someone's work isn't up to snuff." When enforcing necessary quality standards, the issue is one piece of work that didn't meet expectations, not that the person is wrong, stupid, or "a waste of space".

### §13.4 Governance
- **Every organization has a power structure** [Free1972]; the only question is whether it is formal and accountable or informal and unaccountable. Make yours formal and accountable: write and publish the rules for everything from who may use the name and logo to who decides whether people may charge money to teach with the group's materials.
- **Legal form.** A full discussion of options is out of scope. For-profit corporations and incorporated non-profits are the two most popular models. The mechanics vary by jurisdiction, so seek local advice before doing anything; ties with local government or like-minded organizations pay off here.
- **Wilson's preferred model is a commons**: "something managed jointly by a community according to rules they themselves have evolved and adopted". [Boll2014] stresses that all three parts are essential: a commons is not just a shared pasture, but also the community that shares it and the rules they use. For most of human history most resources were commons. Only in the last few hundred years have impersonal markets pushed them to the margins, which Wilson says required convincing people they are "dispassionate calculators of individual advantage" and erasing or devaluing local knowledge and custom, with tragic consequences.
- **Boards in practice.** Society has difficulty recognizing commons organizations, and most recruits won't have experience with them, so you'll probably end up with a board, a director, and other staff. Two kinds of board:
  - A **service board**, whose members also take on other roles in the organization.
  - A **governance board**, whose primary responsibility is to hire, monitor, and if need be fire the director.
  Board members may be elected by the community or appointed. Either way, prioritize **competence over passion** (passion matters more for the rank and file), and recruit for particular skills such as accounting and marketing.
- **Timing of formal rules.** Don't draft a constitution at the start: it only produces "endless wrangling about what we're going to do rather than formalization of what you're already doing." When the time comes to formalize, **make the organization a democracy**: "sooner or later (usually sooner), every appointed board turns into a mutual agreement society" and loses sight of what the community needs. "Giving the community power is messy", but it is the only way invented so far to keep an organization meeting people's actual needs.

### §13.5 Final Thoughts
- **Burnout** is a chronic risk in any community activity [Pign2016]. "If you don't take care of yourself, you won't be able to take care of your community."
- **Succession.** Every organization eventually needs fresh ideas and leadership. When that time comes, train your successors and move on. They will do things you wouldn't have, as every generation does. Few things are as satisfying as watching something you helped build take on a life of its own: celebrate it.

### Appendix "Meetings, Meetings, Meetings"
- **Most people are bad at meetings.** They have no agenda, take no minutes, waffle or wander into irrelevancies, repeat what others said or recite banalities just to have said something, and hold side conversations, which "pretty much guarantees that the meeting will be a waste of time." Running a meeting efficiently is a core skill. Taking part in someone else's meeting matters just as much but gets less attention: "everyone offers leadership training, nobody offers followership training" (a colleague, quoted by Wilson).
- **The rules** (headings quoted; "not secret, but are rarely followed"):
  - **"Decide if there actually needs to be a meeting."** If the only purpose is to share information, have everyone send a brief email. You can read faster than anyone can speak, so typing facts in is the most polite way to share them.
  - **"Write an agenda."** If nobody cares enough to write a point-form list of what will be discussed, the meeting probably doesn't need to happen.
  - **"Include timings in the agenda."** Timings stop early items stealing time from later ones. First estimates with a new group will be "wildly optimistic", so revise them upward. Don't schedule a second or third meeting because the first ran over; find out why you're running over and fix that.
  - **"Prioritize."** Every meeting is a micro-project: do high-impact, low-time items first and skip high-time, low-impact ones.
  - **"Make one person responsible for keeping things moving."** That person keeps items to time, chides people having side conversations or checking email, and asks people who talk too much to get to the point. They should not do all the talking: "whoever is in charge will talk less in a well-run meeting than most other participants."
  - **"Require politeness."** Nobody is rude, nobody rambles, and if someone goes off topic the chair says, "Let's discuss that elsewhere."
  - **"No technology"** unless required for accessibility. Phones, tablets, and laptops go into "politeness mode" (closed). If that's too stressful, people can keep their "electronic pacifiers" but with the network off, so they only take notes or check the agenda.
  - **"No interruptions."** To speak next, raise a finger, put up a sticky note, or use an auction-style gesture. If the speaker doesn't notice, the chair should.
  - **"Record minutes."** Someone **other than the chair** takes point-form notes of the most important information shared and of every decision made or task assigned.
  - **"Take notes."** While others talk, participants note questions they want to ask or points they want to make ("You'll be surprised how smart it makes you look when it's your turn to speak.")
  - **"End early."** A meeting booked 10:00–11:00 should aim to end at 10:55 so people can get to where they're going next.
- **Circulate minutes as soon as the meeting ends** (by email or wiki), because:
  - People who weren't there can keep track. The book's example is aimed at students juggling other courses: a wiki page, email, or blog entry is a much more efficient way to catch up than asking a teammate "Hey, what did I miss?"
  - Everyone can check what was actually said or promised. People remember differently, "accidentally or not". A written record lets team members correct mistaken or malicious interpretations and saves anguish later. Wilson has read minutes and thought "Did I say that?"
  - People can be held accountable at later meetings. Lists of questions and action items are pointless without follow-up. With a ticketing system, create a ticket for each new question or task right after the meeting and update those carried forward; the next agenda can then start by "rattling through a list of tickets."
- **Further reading:** [Brow2007] and [Broo2016]. To "learn, then do", an hour of training on chairing meetings is the most effective place to start.
- **Sticky Notes and Interruption Bingo (box).**
  - *Sticky notes for speaking time:* give everyone three sticky notes at the start. Each time someone speaks they take one down. When they are out, they can't speak until everyone has used at least one, and then everyone gets all their notes back. Effect: nobody talks more than three times as often as the quietest person. It "completely changes the dynamics of most groups": people who'd given up being heard get space, and over-frequent speakers realize how unfair they've been.
  - *Interruption bingo:* draw a grid with participants' names on rows and columns, and tally each interruption in the interrupter/interrupted cell. Look at it halfway through. Usually one or two people do all the interrupting, often unaware. After that, "All right, I'm adding another tally to the bingo card" is often enough to make them throttle back. Caveat: this manages **interruptions, not speaking time**. It may be completely appropriate for someone with more knowledge of a subject to speak more about it, but "it is almost never appropriate to repeatedly cut people off."

### Appendix: Online Meetings
- **The problem** (Chelsea Troy): in most online meetings the first person to speak during a pause gets the floor. So people who want to contribute stop listening to the speaker and watch for the pause instead. Troy, as quoted: the format "encourages participants who want to contribute to say more and listen less."
- **The fix:** run a text chat beside the video call where people signal that they want to speak, and have the moderator pick people from that waiting list. For a large or argumentative meeting, have everyone mute themselves and let only the moderator unmute people.

### Appendix: The Post Mortem
- **Every project should end with one**, reflecting on what was accomplished and what could be done better next time. Its aim is not to "point the finger of shame at individuals", but if that has to happen, the post mortem is the best place for it.
- **Run it like any other meeting, plus these guidelines** [Derb2006]:
  - **Get a moderator who wasn't part of the project** and has no stake in it; otherwise the meeting goes in circles or covers only some of the important topics. For student projects, the course instructor or a TA.
  - **Set aside an hour, and only an hour.** In Wilson's experience nothing useful is said in the first ten minutes of anyone's first post mortem, because people are shy about praising or damning their own work. Nothing useful is said after the first hour either: anyone still talking probably has a lot to get off their chest.
  - **Require attendance.** Everyone on the project should be there; "the people who have the most to learn from the post mortem are often least likely to show up if the meeting is optional."
  - **Make two lists**, headed "Do Again" and "Do Differently". Go round the room and ask every person for one item not already mentioned for each list.
  - **Comment on actions, rather than individuals.** By the end some people won't be able to stand each other. Don't let that sidetrack the meeting: anyone with a complaint about a teammate must criticize a specific event or decision. "He had a bad attitude" helps no one improve.
- **Afterwards,** organize everyone's thoughts into specific recommendations for next time. That list is one of the two major goals of the post mortem; the other is giving people a chance to be heard.

### §C.1 Contributor Covenant
Governs contributions to the book. Adapted from Contributor Covenant v1.4. The appendix "Code of Conduct" (see `10-motivation-and-inclusion.md`) is the sample for classes and other learning situations. Components:
- **Our Pledge:** contributors and maintainers pledge to make participation "a harassment-free experience for everyone, regardless of age, body size, disability, ethnicity, gender identity and expression, level of experience, education, socioeconomic status, nationality, personal appearance, race, religion, or sexual identity and orientation."
- **Our Standards:** positive behaviors and unacceptable behaviors (listed verbatim under Templates).
- **Our Responsibilities:** maintainers clarify standards and take "appropriate and fair corrective action". They have the right and responsibility to remove, edit, or reject contributions (comments, commits, code, wiki edits, issues) not aligned with the covenant, and to ban contributors temporarily or permanently for behavior they deem inappropriate, threatening, offensive, or harmful.
- **Scope:** project spaces, and public spaces when someone represents the project (an official project email address, an official social media account, acting as an appointed representative at an online or offline event). Maintainers may define representation further.
- **Enforcement:** report to the project team (the book gives the author's email). All complaints are reviewed and investigated and get a response appropriate to the circumstances. The team must keep the reporter's identity confidential. Specific enforcement policies may be posted separately. Maintainers who don't follow or enforce the covenant in good faith may face temporary or permanent repercussions decided by other project leaders.

### §C.2 Using This Material
How the book has been used to train instructors, from multi-week online classes to intensive in-person workshops. Large parts of the early chapters (the EPUB cross-reference is unresolved, "[s:models]–[s:process]", most likely Ch 2 to Ch 6), plus Ch 8 and Ch 10, can usually be covered in **two long days**. Four formats:
- **In person:** "the most effective way to deliver this training, but also the most demanding." Participants are physically together and go to nearby breakout spaces for small-group teaching practice. They use their own tablets or laptops for online material and shared note-taking (§9.7), and pen and paper or whiteboards for other exercises. Questions and discussion are spoken aloud. Use sticky notes as status flags (§9.8.1), to distribute attention fairly (§9.8.2), and as minute cards (§9.8.3). See `09-in-the-classroom.md`.
- **Online in groups:** 10–40 learners in 2–6 geographically distributed groups of 4–12. Each group shares one camera and microphone instead of each person joining separately. **Good audio matters more than good video**: the better the audio, the more learners can talk by voice rather than text. The whole class shares notes and uses them for questions and answers. Several dozen people talking on a call works poorly, so in most sessions the instructor talks and learners respond in the note-taking tool's chat.
- **Online as individuals:** the instructor does most of the talking and learners mostly use text chat. Audio again beats video. Participants use text chat to signal they want to speak next (Meetings appendix). Drawing and sharing concept maps (§3.5) and giving feedback on teaching (§8.5) are harder, so rely more on exercises with written results that go in the shared notes, such as feedback on stock videos of people teaching.
- **Multi-week online:** the first format used, and Wilson **no longer recommends it**. Spreading the class out gives time to reflect and tackle larger exercises, but greatly raises the odds that people drop out because of other demands on their time. Format: a weekly one-hour video meeting, sometimes held twice for time zones; shared notes; homework posted online between classes with peer comments. In practice comments are rare, because people strongly prefer to discuss material in the weekly meetings.

### §C.3 Contributing and Maintaining
- **The book is a community resource.** Contributions of all kinds are welcome, from improvement suggestions and errata to new material. All contributors must follow the covenant. By submitting, contributors agree their work may be incorporated in original or edited form and released under the same licence as the book (appendix "License", CC BY 4.0). Incorporated contributors are added to the acknowledgments (§1.6) unless they ask otherwise.
- **Three contribution channels**, from most to least technical:
  1. Know Git and GitHub: submit a pull request modifying the LaTeX source in the `tex` directory of https://github.com/gvwilson/teachtogether.tech. To preview, follow `BUILD.md` in the repository root.
  2. Want to report an error, ask a question, or suggest something: file an issue on the same repository. This needs a GitHub account but no Git knowledge.
  3. Don't want a GitHub account: email the contribution to the author with "T3" or "Teaching Tech Together" in the subject line. The team aims to respond within a week.
- Improvements to the build process, tooling, typography, and more diagrams are also welcome (see `BUILD.md`). Stories of how people have used the material are welcome too.
- **A Teaching Commons (box).** Recalling §13.4's definition of a commons: open source software and Wikipedia are successful commons, so why don't teachers build lessons collaboratively in the same way? People have proposed various reasons, but Wilson doesn't think any "hold up to close scrutiny." Software Carpentry is "proof by implementation" that a teaching commons can produce and maintain high-quality lessons that hundreds of people use [Wils2016]. Advice for newcomers to this way of working:
  - **"Start small."** Fix a typo, clarify an exercise's wording, correct or update a citation, or suggest a better example or analogy.
  - **"Join the conversation."** Read issues and proposed changes others have filed and comment on them. "It's often possible to improve improvements", and it's a good way to introduce yourself and make friends. Some issues are tagged "Suitable for Newcomers" or "Help Wanted" to make this easy.
  - **"Discuss, then edit."** For a large change, such as reorganizing or splitting a chapter, file an issue tagged "Proposal" outlining the change and your reasoning. Everyone is encouraged to comment, so the whole discussion of what and why is open and archived. If accepted, the work may be broken into several smaller, independent issues or changes.

## Rules
- **COM-1** — Decide, explicitly and early, whether the community aims to help people succeed in the world as it is or to give them a way to make a better one. *Why:* it is "the first and most important" decision; many efforts aim at jobs without changing the system that excluded learners. *Check:* is there a written statement of which aim the group pursues? (§13 intro)
- **COM-2** — Understand and engage with what happens to learners outside class. *Why:* Finland's results depend on wider social conditions; "sometimes you need to fix several things in order to fix one" (Partanen). *Check:* does the plan name learners' out-of-class constraints (food, safety, childcare, transport) and say how they are addressed or worked around? (§13 intro)
- **COM-3** — Root teaching in a community of practice, and build one if none exists. *Why:* situated learning holds that learning is social; teaching that isn't rooted in a community isn't effective or sustainable [Weng2015]. *Check:* is there somewhere learners and teachers keep interacting after the class? (§13 intro)
- **COM-4** — Offer legitimate peripheral participation: graded, low-risk tasks the community recognizes as real contributions, moving from simple tools to complex ones to expert work. *Why:* newcomers become peers through "a ramp rather than a cliff". *Check:* list newcomer tasks and confirm each is low-risk, valued, and leads to a next step. (§13 intro)
- **COM-5** — Name which type(s) of community you are (action, concern, interest, place) and stick with the choice. *Why:* real communities are mixes, but "what matters is that you pick something and stick with it." *Check:* can the organizers state the type in one sentence, and do activities match it? (§13 intro)
- **COM-6** — Before founding a new group, check whether joining an existing one would be more effective. *Why:* joining gives a head start on teaching, colleagues, and a chance to learn how to run things; learning those skills should matter more than founder status. *Check:* did you survey existing groups (e.g. 4-H, literacy programs, Black Girls Code, Bridge) and record why each was or wasn't joined? (§13.1)
- **COM-7** — Study prior organizing practice before building. *Why:* "you owe it to yourself and everyone who's going to work with you to find out what's been done before." *Check:* has the team consulted at least one organizing source ([Alin1989], [Brow2007], [Midw2010], [Adam1975], [Spal2014], NonprofitReady.org)? (§13.1)
- **COM-8** — Once more than a couple of non-founders are involved, plan for all three phases: recruitment, retention, retirement. *Why:* everyone, founders included, passes through all three. *Check:* does the organization have a written practice for each phase? (§13.2)
- **COM-9** — End every class or encounter with two sentences saying how people can help and that help is welcome. *Why:* your own learners are your best recruits ("see one, do one, teach one"), and their recent experience as learners counters collective expert blind spot. *Check:* is the call to help in the closing script or slides? (§13.2)
- **COM-10** — Ask prospective volunteers for a small favor first, such as fixing a typo or unclear wording in lessons or suggesting an exercise or example. *Why:* people who have done you a favor are more likely to do another (Franklin), and this gives them practice and opens a conversation. *Check:* are there visible small-contribution tasks, with lesson materials kept maintainable (§6.3)? (§13.2; §C.3)
- **COM-11** — Have newcomers take part in group activities before working alone. *Why:* without follow-through, people come once or twice and disappear; group work teaches how things are done and builds social ties. *Check:* is a newcomer's first assignment a group activity? (§13.2)
- **COM-12** — Give every newcomer a mentor whose explicit duties are making introductions and explaining unwritten rules, and who reports back every few weeks. *Why:* these are the most important things a mentor does, and mentors must actually mentor proactively. *Check:* does each newcomer have a named mentor and a reporting cadence? (§13.2)
- **COM-13** — When someone leaves, run the retirement checklist: explicit departure, no shame, knowledge transfer, keys handed over, follow-up after 2–3 months, thanks. *Why:* otherwise knowledge and access vanish (the softball-field booking) and useful exit advice is never heard. *Check:* run the Retirement checklist below. (§13.2)
- **COM-14** — Ask members what they want, what they're comfortable doing, and what time they have, instead of guessing. *Why:* you are not your members, just as you are not your learners (§6.1); preferences surface in a short conversation. *Check:* is there a record of each volunteer's stated preferences and constraints? (§13.3)
- **COM-15** — Provide many kinds of contribution and don't hoard tasks. *Why:* "The more kinds of tasks you do yourself, the fewer opportunities there are for others to get involved." *Check:* count the distinct non-teaching roles (website, accounts, proof-reading, etc.) open to members. (§13.3)
- **COM-16** — Recognize contributions publicly and privately (presentations, website). *Why:* everyone likes to be appreciated, and appreciation keeps people. *Check:* where are contributors named? (§13.3)
- **COM-17** — Make space: don't micromanage, centralize control, or always reply first. *Why:* people lose autonomy and drift away, and the community stays centered on one or two people instead of becoming a connected network. *Check:* in the group's channels, what share of replies come from the leader, and do members collaborate without the leader? (§13.3)
- **COM-18** — Offer training in organizational skills (committees, meetings, budgets, grant proposals, dispute resolution), through a partner organization unless that training is your specialty. *Why:* it makes the organization run better, and transferable skills motivate people to join and stay. *Check:* is there a training offer, and who delivers it? (§13.3)
- **COM-19** — Spend an hour or two every month finding and maintaining allies, including government (school board, library, councillor's office), often by asking them for advice. *Why:* allies give advice, space, funding, publicity, connections, and help with red tape; being asked flatters them and they'll remember you. *Check:* is there a recurring calendar slot and a list of ally contacts? (§13.3)
- **COM-20** — Soup, then hymns: deliver immediately useful things before writing manifestos or vision statements. *Why:* people join to help and be helped; small wins build confidence for bigger investments, and that is when values need defining. *Check:* has the group shipped something members use before it debates mission wording? (§13.3)
- **COM-21** — Pay people either nothing or a proper wage, never a token amount. *Why:* a token payment removes the satisfaction of doing good without giving the satisfaction of earning a living. *Check:* look for stipends or honoraria well below a living wage. (§13.3)
- **COM-22** — Counter impostor syndrome at community level: encourage people, discourage hostility and bickering, remove hidden barriers by documenting how to take part, have leaders show their uncertainties, reward mentoring, and criticize work rather than people. *Why:* impostor syndrome thrives on arbitrary standards, harsh criticism, and secrecy (Ada Initiative). *Check:* run the Impostor-syndrome audit below. (§13.3)
- **COM-23** — Make the power structure formal and accountable: write and publish the rules, from use of the name and logo to who may charge for teaching with the group's materials. *Why:* every organization has a power structure; unwritten ones are unaccountable [Free1972]. *Check:* is there a public rules document covering name/logo use and paid teaching? (§13.4)
- **COM-24** — Get local advice before choosing a legal form. *Why:* mechanics vary by jurisdiction; ties to local government and allied groups help here. *Check:* is local legal or peer advice recorded? (§13.4)
- **COM-25** — Know whether your board is a service board or a governance board, prioritize competence over passion in board members, and recruit for specific skills (accounting, marketing). *Why:* passion matters for the rank and file; boards need competence. *Check:* is each board member's role and skill listed, and is the board type stated? (§13.4)
- **COM-26** — Don't draft a constitution at the start; when you do formalize, make the organization a democracy. *Why:* early constitutions cause "endless wrangling"; appointed boards become "mutual agreement" societies that lose sight of community needs. *Check:* does formalization describe existing practice, and does the community elect the board? (§13.4)
- **COM-27** — Treat burnout as a chronic risk and take care of yourself. *Why:* "If you don't take care of yourself, you won't be able to take care of your community" [Pign2016]. *Check:* does any single person carry unsustainable load? (§13.5)
- **COM-28** — When fresh leadership is needed, train your successors, then step away and let them do things differently. *Why:* every organization needs renewal, and successors will rightly differ. *Check:* is there a succession plan and a named, trained successor? (§13.5)
- **COM-29** — Hold a meeting only if it does more than share information; send information by email. *Why:* reading is faster than listening, so typing facts in is more polite. *Check:* does every agenda item need discussion or a decision? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-30** — Write a point-form agenda with a time for each item; revise estimates upward; fix the cause of overruns rather than adding meetings. *Why:* no agenda usually means the meeting isn't needed; timings stop early items stealing time; first estimates are "wildly optimistic". *Check:* does the agenda exist before the meeting, with per-item timings? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-31** — Order agenda items by impact over time: high-impact, low-time items first; drop high-time, low-impact ones. *Why:* every meeting is a micro-project. *Check:* is the order justified by impact and time? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-32** — Appoint one chair to keep time, stop side conversations and email-checking, and cut rambling. The chair talks less than most others. *Why:* someone must keep things moving without dominating. *Check:* is the chair named, and is their talk time below average? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-33** — Require politeness: no rudeness, no rambling; the chair defers off-topic items ("Let's discuss that elsewhere."). *Check:* did the chair redirect off-topic threads? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-34** — No devices in meetings except for accessibility; if necessary, allow devices with the network off. *Check:* is the device norm stated at the start? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-35** — No interruptions: people signal to speak (raised finger, sticky note), and the chair watches for signals. *Check:* is there a visible speaking queue? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-36** — Have someone other than the chair take point-form minutes of key information, every decision, and every task; circulate them immediately; turn each task or question into a ticket and start the next agenda by reviewing tickets. *Why:* minutes keep absentees informed, let people correct mistaken or malicious recollections, and enable accountability. *Check:* were minutes sent the same day, and do tickets exist for each action? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-37** — Participants should note their questions and points while others speak, rather than interrupting. *Check:* do participants bring written questions to their turn? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-38** — End five minutes early (e.g. 10:55 for a 10:00–11:00 slot). *Why:* gives people time to get to their next commitment. (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-39** — When a few voices dominate, use three-sticky-note speaking limits; when people interrupt, use interruption bingo and review it halfway through. Don't use bingo to limit knowledgeable speakers. *Why:* sticky notes cap the ratio of most to least talkative at 3:1; bingo makes unaware interrupters visible. The goal is to stop cutting off, not to equalize expertise. *Check:* which problem is present, speaking-time imbalance or interruptions, and is the matching tool used? (Meetings appendix)
- **COM-40** — In online meetings, run a side text chat as a speaking queue picked by the moderator; for large or argumentative meetings, everyone mutes and only the moderator unmutes. *Why:* otherwise the first person to speak in a pause gets the floor, which rewards saying more and listening less (Troy). *Check:* is there a queue channel and a moderator? (Online Meetings)
- **COM-41** — End every project with a post mortem chaired by an outside moderator with no stake, lasting exactly one hour, with compulsory attendance. *Why:* insiders go in circles or cover only some topics; little useful is said in the first ten minutes or after an hour; those with most to learn skip optional meetings [Derb2006]. *Check:* moderator independence, the time box, and the attendance list. (Post Mortem)
- **COM-42** — In a post mortem, collect "Do Again" and "Do Differently" items one per person per lap, criticize actions not individuals, and finish with organized, specific recommendations. *Why:* the recommendations list and giving everyone a hearing are the two goals; personal attacks ("bad attitude") help no one improve. *Check:* does every complaint name an event or decision, and does the output include recommendations? (Post Mortem)
- **COM-43** — Govern contributions to shared material with a published contributor covenant: pledge, standards, maintainer responsibilities, scope (including public representation), a reporting contact, confidentiality for reporters, and consequences for maintainers who don't enforce it in good faith. *Why:* it makes the community harassment-free and gives maintainers clear authority. *Check:* compare against the Contributor covenant skeleton below. (§C.1)
- **COM-44** — For instructor training, prefer in-person delivery; if online, put good audio ahead of video and use shared notes and chat; avoid multi-week online formats. *Why:* in person is the most effective; audio quality determines how much voice interaction is possible; multi-week formats greatly raise drop-out. *Check:* format choice and AV plan. (§C.2)
- **COM-45** — When participants are online individually, replace drawn and live-feedback exercises with exercises that produce written results in the shared notes (e.g. feedback on stock teaching videos). *Why:* concept maps and teaching feedback are hard to share individually online. *Check:* does every exercise produce something that can go in the shared notes? (§C.2)
- **COM-46** — Offer contribution paths for every skill level (pull request, issue, email), credit contributors, state the licence contributions fall under, tag newcomer-suitable issues, and require a "Proposal" issue before large changes. *Why:* lowering the barrier invites contributions; discussing in the open archives the reasoning; large accepted changes can be split into smaller tasks. *Check:* does the CONTRIBUTING guide cover all three channels, credit, licence, tags, and the proposal step? (§C.3)

## Procedures

### P1. Decide whether to found or join (§13 intro, §13.1)
1. State your aim: help people succeed in the current world, or help them change it (COM-1).
2. List what your learners face outside class that will affect the class (COM-2).
3. Survey existing groups doing related work (clubs, literacy programs, coding non-profits). For each, note what joining would give you: a head start, colleagues, learning how to run things.
4. Decision point: if an existing group serves the aim, **join it** and "learn, then do". Found a new group only if none fits; check whether you're motivated by being "the founder" rather than by the work.
5. Either way, read prior organizing work (COM-7) and talk to experienced groups (COM-19).
6. If founding, name the community type(s) (action, concern, interest, place) and commit to it (COM-5).
7. Design a newcomer ramp: list simple, low-risk, recognized tasks, then intermediate, then expert tasks (COM-4).

### P2. Recruit (§13.2)
1. Make the organization findable and its mission and value to volunteers clear; share stories of the help you want and of the people helped; show many ways to get involved (see `14-marketing.md`).
2. Add a two-sentence "how you can help, and help is welcome" close to every class and event (COM-9).
3. Offer a small first ask: typo fixes, wording clarifications, a suggested exercise or example (COM-10).
4. Put newcomers into group activities before solo tasks (COM-11).
5. Assign a mentor; brief them that introductions and unwritten rules are their main duties; collect a report from each mentor every few weeks (COM-12).

### P3. Retain (§13.3)
1. Hold a short conversation with each volunteer: what do you want to do, what are you comfortable doing, what time constraints do you have? Expect "anything!" at first and dig further (COM-14).
2. Map roles beyond teaching (website, accounts, proof-reading, logistics) and hand tasks over instead of doing them yourself (COM-15).
3. Recognize contributors in presentations and on the website, and privately (COM-16).
4. Step back: delay your replies, let members answer one another, avoid central control (COM-17).
5. Arrange training in organizational skills through a civic or community group (COM-18).
6. Block an hour or two a month for allies, including local government; open by asking for advice (COM-19).
7. Prioritize immediately useful outputs; defer manifestos until small wins exist (COM-20).
8. When paid roles become necessary (sysadmin, accounting), pay a proper wage or nothing (COM-21).
9. Audit for impostor-syndrome conditions (COM-22).
10. Watch your own and others' load for burnout (COM-27).

### P4. Retire a member (§13.2)
1. Ask the person to announce their departure explicitly.
2. Reassure them: no embarrassment or shame.
3. Arrange knowledge transfer: a few weeks of mentoring a successor, or an interview by someone staying to capture stories worth retelling.
4. Inventory and hand over the "keys": anything only they know how to do or access (e.g. booking the venue), such as accounts, bookings, contacts, and recurring logistics.
5. Thank them now, and again at the next gathering.
6. Diary a follow-up 2–3 months later to ask what worked, what didn't, and what advice they held back.

### P5. Formalize governance (§13.4)
1. Early on: don't write a constitution; write down and publish the rules you already operate by, at least for name/logo use and charging for teaching with the materials (COM-23, COM-26).
2. Get local advice on legal form (for-profit corporation, incorporated non-profit, others) (COM-24).
3. Consider the commons model: shared resource + community + self-made rules, all three together.
4. Choose a board type: service board (members also do other work) or governance board (hires, monitors, fires the director) (COM-25).
5. Recruit board members for competence and specific skills, not passion.
6. When formalizing, make the board accountable to the community through democratic election (COM-26).
7. Plan succession: train successors, then move on (COM-28).

### P6. Run a meeting (Meetings appendix)
1. Ask: is this only information-sharing? If yes, send an email instead and stop (COM-29).
2. Write a point-form agenda; if nobody will write one, cancel (COM-30).
3. Put a time on each item; inflate estimates for a new group (COM-30).
4. Order items high-impact/low-time first; cut high-time/low-impact ones (COM-31).
5. Name a chair (timekeeper and moderator, who talks less) and a separate minute-taker (COM-32, COM-36).
6. At the start, state the norms: politeness, no devices (accessibility excepted), signal to speak, take your own notes (COM-33 to COM-35, COM-37).
7. During the meeting: the chair enforces timings, defers off-topic items ("Let's discuss that elsewhere."), watches for speaking signals, and calls out side conversations.
8. Decision point: if a few people dominate airtime, use the three-sticky-note rule; if people interrupt, run interruption bingo and review it at the halfway mark (COM-39).
9. Aim to finish five minutes before the scheduled end (COM-38).
10. Immediately afterwards, circulate the minutes; create or update a ticket for each question or task (COM-36).
11. Start the next agenda by going through the open tickets.
12. Decision point: if meetings keep overrunning, diagnose the cause instead of scheduling extra meetings (COM-30).

### P7. Run an online meeting (Online Meetings)
1. Open a text chat alongside the video call as a speaking queue.
2. Ask people to type a signal (e.g. a hand or "next") to join the queue.
3. The moderator calls on people in queue order.
4. Decision point: if the meeting is large or argumentative, everyone mutes and only the moderator unmutes the next speaker.
5. Apply all of P6.

### P8. Facilitate a post mortem (Post Mortem; [Derb2006])
1. Schedule it as the project's last step, for exactly one hour; make attendance compulsory for every project member (COM-41).
2. Get a moderator from outside the project with no stake in it (for student projects, the instructor or a TA).
3. Expect the first ten minutes to be slow; don't cut the session short because of it.
4. Write "Do Again" and "Do Differently" as two headings on the board.
5. Go round the room: each person gives one new (not yet mentioned) item for each list. Repeat laps as time allows.
6. Redirect any personal criticism to a specific event or decision (COM-42).
7. Stop at the hour, even if someone still has things to get off their chest.
8. Organize the items into specific recommendations for next time and circulate them.

### P9. Choose a format for instructor training (§C.2)
1. Default to **in person** if feasible (two long days covers large parts of Ch 2 to Ch 6, Ch 8, and Ch 10).
   - Arrange breakout spaces for small-group teaching practice, shared note-taking, pen and paper or whiteboards, and sticky notes as status flags, attention distribution, and minute cards.
2. If participants are clustered in several places, use **online in groups**: 10–40 people in 2–6 rooms of 4–12, one camera and microphone per room, invest in audio first, shared notes plus chat for Q&A, instructor mostly talking.
3. If participants are scattered, use **online as individuals**: text chat for a speaking queue, audio over video, and written-output exercises such as critiquing stock teaching videos in place of concept maps and live teaching feedback.
4. Avoid **multi-week online** unless there is no alternative; if used, run a weekly one-hour call (repeated for time zones), shared notes, and posted homework, expecting few written peer comments and higher drop-out.

### P10. Set up a teaching commons for contributions (§C.1, §C.3)
1. Publish a contributor covenant (template below) with a reporting contact and a confidentiality promise (COM-43).
2. State the licence that contributions are released under, and how contributors are credited.
3. Offer three channels: pull request (with build/preview instructions), issue (no Git needed), email (with a subject-line tag and response-time target).
4. Tag issues "Suitable for Newcomers" and "Help Wanted".
5. Tell newcomers: start small, join the conversation, discuss then edit.
6. Require large changes to start as an issue tagged "Proposal"; keep the discussion public; split accepted proposals into smaller tasks.
7. Ask users to share stories of how they've used the material.

## Templates and checklists

### Closing "how to help" lines (§13.2)
```
[Two sentences at the end of every class or encounter]
1. How people can help: <e.g., fix a typo in the lessons, help at the next workshop, mentor a newcomer>.
2. That help is welcome: <e.g., "Everyone who helps started as a learner here—we'd love to have you.">
```

### Mentor brief (§13.2)
```
Mentor for: <newcomer>
Primary responsibilities:
  1. Make introductions (who to know, for what).
  2. Explain the unwritten rules (how things are actually done here).
Be proactive: don't wait to be asked.
Report back to <organizer> every few weeks: what you did.
```

### Retirement checklist (§13.2)
```
[ ] Departure made explicit (everyone knows they've left)
[ ] Person reassured: no embarrassment or shame about leaving
[ ] Knowledge passed on (few weeks mentoring a successor, or exit interview for stories)
[ ] Keys handed over (accounts, bookings, contacts, recurring logistics)
[ ] Thanked on departure
[ ] Thanked again at next group gathering
[ ] Follow-up scheduled 2–3 months later: what worked, what didn't, unspoken advice
```

### Volunteer-preference conversation (§13.3)
```
- What would you like to do?
- What are you comfortable doing? (may differ from the above)
- What constraints are there on your time?
- (If "I don't know—anything!") Would you rather work with people, or behind the scenes
  (e.g., website, accounts, proof-reading)?
```

### Retention audit (§13.3)
```
[ ] Do members enjoy at least some of the work? ("having a ball")
[ ] Preferences asked, not guessed
[ ] Many ways to contribute; leader not hoarding tasks
[ ] Contributions recognized publicly and privately
[ ] Space made: no micromanaging; leader not always first to reply
[ ] Training offered in organizational skills (via a partner group)
[ ] 1–2 hours/month on allies, including government
[ ] Immediately useful outputs before manifestos ("soup, then hymns")
[ ] Pay policy: nothing or a proper wage, no token pay
```

### Impostor-syndrome community audit (Ada Initiative, §13.3)
```
[ ] Encourage people
[ ] Discourage hostility and bickering (no public, hostile, personal arguments)
[ ] Eliminate hidden barriers: explicit welcome + thorough docs on how to participate
[ ] Leaders show their own uncertainties and learning process
[ ] Mentoring of newcomers rewarded (ideally a formal advancement criterion)
[ ] Quality problems framed as about the work, never the person
```

### Governance checklist (§13.4)
```
[ ] Published rules: use of name and logo
[ ] Published rules: who decides whether people may charge to teach with the materials
[ ] Local advice obtained on legal form
[ ] Board type stated: service board / governance board
[ ] Board members chosen for competence and specific skills (accounting, marketing, …)
[ ] No premature constitution; formalization records existing practice
[ ] When formalized: democratic (community elects)
[ ] Succession plan; burnout watch
```

Norms: polite; devices closed (accessibility excepted); signal to speak; take your own notes

0. Review open tickets from last meeting ............ 5 min

  - <decision>

  - <task> — owner — due — ticket #

### Speaking-balance tools (Meetings appendix)
```
Sticky-note rule: 3 notes each. Speak → remove one. At zero, wait until everyone has used ≥1;
then all notes are returned. (Caps speaking at 3× the quietest person.)

Interruption bingo: grid, rows = interrupter, columns = interrupted, tally each interruption.
Review at halfway. Then: "All right, I'm adding another tally to the bingo card."
(Manages interruptions, not speaking time.)
```

### Online meeting setup (Online Meetings)
```
[ ] Text chat beside video, used as the speaking queue
[ ] Moderator calls on people from the queue
[ ] Large/argumentative meeting: all muted; only moderator unmutes
```

### Post mortem agenda (Post Mortem; [Derb2006])
```
Moderator: <outside the project, no stake>   Duration: 60 min, hard stop   Attendance: required for all
Board headings:   DO AGAIN            |   DO DIFFERENTLY
Process: lap the room; each person gives one new item per list; repeat.
Rule: criticize events/decisions, not people ("He had a bad attitude" is not allowed).
Output: organized list of specific recommendations for next time; everyone heard.
```

### Contributor covenant skeleton (§C.1; adapted from Contributor Covenant v1.4)
```
Our Pledge: harassment-free participation for everyone regardless of age, body size, disability,
  ethnicity, gender identity and expression, level of experience, education, socioeconomic status,
  nationality, personal appearance, race, religion, or sexual identity and orientation.
Our Standards — positive:
  - Using welcoming and inclusive language
  - Being respectful of differing viewpoints and experiences
  - Gracefully accepting constructive criticism
  - Focusing on what is best for the community
  - Showing empathy towards other community members
Our Standards — unacceptable:
  - The use of sexualized language or imagery and unwelcome sexual attention or advances
  - Trolling, insulting/derogatory comments, and personal or political attacks
  - Public or private harassment
  - Publishing others' private information, such as a physical or electronic address, without explicit permission
  - Other conduct which could reasonably be considered inappropriate in a professional setting
Our Responsibilities: maintainers clarify standards; take appropriate and fair corrective action;
  may remove/edit/reject non-aligned contributions; may ban temporarily or permanently.

Enforcement: report to <project team contact>; all complaints reviewed and investigated; response
  appropriate to circumstances; reporter kept confidential; detailed policies may be posted separately;
  maintainers who don't enforce in good faith face repercussions decided by project leadership.
```

### Contribution guide skeleton (§C.3)
```
Licence: contributions released under <same licence as project>; may be used in original or edited form.
Credit: added to acknowledgments unless you ask otherwise.
Ways to contribute:
  1. Pull request (Git users) — edit <source dir>; preview via <build instructions>.
  2. Issue — errors, questions, suggestions (account needed, no Git).
  3. Email — put "<tag>" in the subject; we aim to reply within a week.
New here?
  - Start small: typo, exercise wording, citation, better example or analogy.
  - Join the conversation: comment on existing issues/changes ("Suitable for Newcomers", "Help Wanted").
  - Discuss, then edit: large changes start as an issue tagged "Proposal".
We also welcome: build/tooling/typography improvements, diagrams, stories of how you used this.
```

### Instructor-training format chooser (§C.2)
| Format | Setup | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| In person | Together; breakout spaces; shared notes; sticky notes (status, attention, minute cards) | Most effective | Most demanding |
| Online in groups | 10–40 people, 2–6 sites of 4–12, one camera/mic per site; shared notes and chat | Group interaction within sites | Audio quality decides voice interaction |
| Online as individuals | Instructor talks; text chat queue; audio over video | Reach | Concept maps and teaching feedback hard; use written-output exercises |
| Multi-week online | Weekly 1-hr call (maybe twice); homework posted; peer comments | Time to reflect; larger exercises | High drop-out; few peer comments; **no longer recommended** |

## Examples
- **Finland (Anu Partanen):** teachers' outstanding results rest on a society that values equality of opportunity. Copying their methods without nutrition, safety, and fair courts is harder. Lesson: be involved in learners' out-of-class lives (§13 intro).
- **Music progression:** recorder/ukulele nursery rhymes → simple band songs on trumpet/saxophone → own musical tastes. Lesson: legitimate peripheral participation is a ramp (§13 intro).
- **Peripheral tasks:** first scarf; stuffing envelopes in an election campaign; proof-reading open source docs. Lesson: low-risk but recognized contributions (§13 intro).
- **Community mix:** "people in Toronto who like teaching tech" combines place and interest (§13 intro).
- **Lesson-fix favor:** asking learners to submit typo or wording fixes to lessons uses the Franklin effect, builds skills, and starts recruiting conversations (§13.2).
- **The softball field:** discovering six months after a departure that only that person knew how to book the annual game's field. Lesson: hand over the keys (§13.2).
- **"I don't know—anything!":** a short conversation reveals the volunteer likes people-facing work but not finances, or the reverse (§13.3).
- **Government:** school boards, libraries, and councillors' offices can give space, funding, publicity, connections, and help with red tape; no worse than similar-sized companies (§13.3).
- **Token pay:** paying a token amount removes the satisfaction of doing good without the satisfaction of earning a living (§13.3).
- **Minutes surprise:** Wilson has read minutes and thought "Did I say that?" or "I didn't promise to have it ready then!" Lesson: circulate minutes so people can correct the record (Meetings appendix).
- **Student teams:** students juggling other courses miss meetings; a wiki page or email beats "Hey, what did I miss?" (Meetings appendix).
- **Post mortem lists:** Wilson writes "Do Again" / "Do Differently" on the board and laps the room for one fresh item each (Post Mortem).
- **Software Carpentry:** "proof by implementation" that a teaching commons can produce and maintain high-quality lessons used by hundreds [Wils2016]; Wilson re-started it in 2010 (§C.3; cf. §15 intro).
- **Multi-week online training:** the first format used; dropped because people left under other time pressures; peer comments on homework were rare (§C.2).

## Evidence and caveats
- **Situated learning / communities of practice** [Weng2015] are presented as a framework for thinking, not as an empirical result with effect sizes.
- **Franklin observation** ("Start Small"): presented as an observation attributed to Ben Franklin, not as a cited study.
- **Organizing literature** ([Alin1989], [Brow2007], [Midw2010], [Adam1975], [Spal2014]) is practitioner guidance rooted in decades of practice, not controlled research. Many exercises in §13.6 come from [Brow2007], which Wilson calls "exceptionally useful".
- **Governance:** [Free1972] (every organization has a power structure) and [Boll2014] (the commons) are argument and theory. Wilson's preference for a commons and for democracy is explicitly **his opinion** ("The model I prefer…"). The claim that appointed boards "sooner or later (usually sooner)" become mutual agreement societies is his experience-based assertion. The full range of governance options is "outside the scope of this book."
- **Government is no worse than companies of similar size**: "in my experience", explicitly anecdotal.
- **Burnout** is a chronic risk [Pign2016] (a practitioner guide).
- **Meeting rules** come from experience plus [Brow2007] and [Broo2016]. The sticky-note 3:1 cap follows arithmetically from the rule. Its effect on group dynamics, and the claim that interruption bingo usually shows one or two interrupters, are Wilson's experience, not measured findings.
- **Post mortem timing claims** (nothing useful in the first ten minutes of a first post mortem or after an hour) are "in my experience"; the guidelines follow [Derb2006].
- **Online meetings** draw on Chelsea Troy's blog-style analysis, not a study.
- **Format advice (§C.2)**, including "good audio matters more than good video" and multi-week drop-out, is reported as "we have found" from running the training, not formal evaluation.
- **Teaching commons:** Wilson asserts that none of the proposed reasons teachers don't co-author lessons "hold up to close scrutiny", but doesn't list or rebut them here; the evidence offered is Software Carpentry [Wils2016].
- Citation key [Kuch3011] used elsewhere in this part is a typo in the book for a 2011 work (see `14-marketing.md`).

## Practice exercises
- **What Kind of Community?** — individual, 15 min: re-read the four community types and decide which your group is or aspires to be (§13.6).
- **People You May Meet** — small groups, 30 min: pick three of seven difficult personas (Anna the know-it-all, Catherine who won't decide alone, Frank who hoards knowledge, Hediyeh who is quiet and criticism-sensitive, Kenny who shirks plausibly, Melissa who always finishes last-minute, Raj who is rude) and discuss how to help each contribute better (§13.6, from [Brow2007]).
- **Values** — small groups, 45 min: individually answer, then compare: what values does your organization express, are they the ones you want, what behaviors demonstrate them and their opposites (§13.6).
- **Meeting Procedures** — small groups, 30 min: how meetings are run, whether rules are explicit, who decides, majority vs. consensus, how attendees and absentees know a decision was made, and is it working (§13.6).
- **Size** — small groups, 20 min: how big is the group, what size do you want, any membership limits, would a limit help (§13.6).
- **Staffing** — small groups, 30 min: paid or volunteer, should there be staff, titles, duties, supervision, pay, benefits, and whether each is what you want (§13.6).
- **Money** — small groups, 30 min: who pays for what, funding sources, plans for change and who follows up, how much you have and need, main spending (§13.6).
- **Becoming a Member** — small groups, 45 min: how people join, membership criteria, behavior rules on joining, dues, and whether each works (§13.6).
- **Borrowing Ideas** — whole class, 15 min: read one section of the Open Source Guides site (e.g. "Finding Users for Your Project", "Leadership and Governance"); give a two-minute talk on one idea you found useful or disagreed with; [Foge2005] as background (§13.6).
- **Who Are You?** — small groups of 3–6, 20 min: read NOAA's guide to disruptive meeting behaviors (labels like "talkative", "indecisive", "shy"), decide which fits you, and judge whether the suggested strategies for people like you work (§13.6).
