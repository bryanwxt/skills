# Partnerships

> Source: Greg Wilson, *Teaching Tech Together* (2018), Ch. 15 "Partnerships" (§15.1–§15.4). Licensed CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/); condensed and restructured for skill use, with quotations marked. Rule IDs use prefix `PTN`.

## When a skill needs this
- A partnership advisor plans how a grassroots teaching group should approach a school, university department, bootcamp, church, court program, or company.
- A change-management coach diagnoses why teachers or faculty aren't adopting a better practice and proposes tactics that address their actual concerns.
- A proposal reviewer checks a plan to introduce a new teaching practice or tool into an institution against known adoption killers (Farmer's ten rules) and change patterns (Manns and Rising).
- A strategy skill decides which Borrego and Henderson change category an initiative falls into and picks strategies to match.
- A pitch assistant tailors an approach to bootcamps by emphasizing teaching expertise and low-cost, evidence-based practices.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Implementation science | The study of how to get research findings adopted in everyday practice (glossary: "translate research findings to everyday clinical practice"; §15.1, [Baue2015]). |
| Glass's Law | "any new tool or practice initially slows you down" (§15.1, as a faculty concern from [Bark2015]). |
| Individual vs. system change | Whether change targets individual teachers or the institution as a whole ([Borr2014], §15.1). |
| Prescribed vs. emergent change | Top-down change vs. bottom-up change ([Borr2014], §15.1). |
| Bootcamp | Intensive one-to-six-month program, usually run for profit, retraining people for tech jobs (§15.2). |
| Change pattern | A named tactic for introducing new ideas in organizations, from [Mann2015] (e.g. Small Successes, Champion Skeptic) (§15.3). |
| Educationalization | Pushing the solution of social problems onto educational institutions ([Laba2008], §15.4 exercise). |

## Concepts

### Chapter 15 introduction
- Whether you build a new community or join one (§13.1), your organization will eventually need to work with more established groups: schools, community programs, churches, the courts, and companies. The chapter offers strategies for doing that, and for judging when it's worthwhile.
- **Hedge:** unlike most of the book, this chapter draws more on what Wilson has seen than on what he has done. Most of his attempts to get large institutions to change were unproductive, which is part of why he left a university position to re-start Software Carpentry in 2010. He particularly invites contributions on this chapter.

### §15.1 Working With Schools
- **Fear drives non-adoption.** Everyone fears the unknown and embarrassing themselves, so "most people would rather fail than change." Lauren Herckis studied why university faculty don't adopt better teaching methods:
  - main reason: **fear of looking stupid in front of their students**;
  - secondary: **worry that the inevitable bumps of changing methods will hurt course evaluations**, and **wanting to keep emulating the lecturers who inspired them**.
  - "It's pointless to argue about whether these issues are 'real' or not": faculty believe they are, so any plan to work with faculty must address them.
- **[Bark2015]: how CS educators adopt new teaching practices** (a two-part study covering individuals, organizations, and society). Three questions:
  1. **How do faculty hear about new practices?** They seek them out to solve a problem (especially student engagement); through deliberate institutional initiatives; from colleagues; or through expected and unexpected interactions at conferences, teaching-related or technical.
  2. **Why do they try them?**
     - Institutional incentives (e.g. innovating to improve promotion chances), though at research institutions there is tension because rhetoric about the importance of teaching "is largely disbelieved".
     - Their own **cost/benefit analysis**: will it save them time?
     - Inspiration from **role models**; this mostly affects innovations aimed at engagement and motivation rather than learning outcomes.
     - **Trusted sources**: e.g. people met at conferences in the same situation who report successful adoption.
     - **Concerns** advocates often didn't address: **Glass's Law** (any new tool or practice initially slows you down), and **physical classroom layout** ("discussion groups just don't work in theater-style seating").
     - **The most telling result:** "Despite being researchers themselves, the CS faculty we spoke to for the most part did not believe that results from educational studies were credible reasons to try out teaching practices." This fits other findings: even career researchers disregard education research.
  3. **Why do they keep using them?** "Student feedback is critical", often the strongest reason, even though students' self-reports don't correlate strongly with learning outcomes. Lecture attendance is seen as an indicator of engagement. **Institutional requirements** also keep practices in place, but people often drop the practice and regress when the explicit incentive or monitoring is removed.
- **Systematic approach: implementation science.** [Baue2015] studied adoption of new medical techniques in the US Veterans Administration: evidence-based practices take **an average of 17 years** to reach routine general practice, and **only about half** are ever widely adopted. Findings like this spurred implementation science, the scientific study of how to get people to adopt better evidence-based practices. "The good news is, you can tackle these problems systematically."
- **Start by finding out what people believe they need** (as in Ch 13). [Yada2016] summarizes K-12 teachers' feedback on the preparation and support they want. It won't all apply to your setting, but "having a cup of tea with a few people and listening before you speak can make a world of difference."
- **Then change incrementally, within institutions' own frameworks.** Examples:
  - [Nara2018]: an intensive **three-year bachelor's program** built on tight-knit cohorts and administrative support **tripled graduation rates**.
  - [Hu2017]: a **six-month certification program** for existing high-school teachers who want to teach computing (replacing an older two-year, five-course program). The number of computing teachers had been flat from 2007 to 2013 and **quadrupled** after the new program, **without diluting quality**: new-to-computing teachers seemed as effective as those with more computing training at teaching the introductory Exploring Computer Science (ECS) course. Quote: "How much CS content students self-reported learning in ECS appears to be based on how much they believed they knew before taking ECS, and appears to have no correlation to their teacher's CS background."
- **[Borr2014]: categories of change strategy in higher education.** Two dimensions: whether the change is **individual or to the system as a whole**, and whether it is **prescribed (top-down) or emergent (bottom-up)**. The change agent has a different role in each, and "should pursue different strategies accordingly." The paper explains each method in detail; [Hend2015a, Hend2015b] present the same ideas in more actionable form.
- **Where an outsider usually starts:** coming from outside, you'll probably fall into **Individual/Emergent** at first, approaching teachers one by one and pushing change bottom-up. Borrego and Henderson's strategies for that category centre on **having teachers reflect on their teaching, individually or in groups**. Since teachers may know more about teaching than you do, this often comes down to **live coding sessions with them**, so they learn to program themselves, and **demonstrating whatever curriculum you already have**.

### §15.2 Working Outside Schools
- **Bootcamps:** a growing number of people learn programming at intensive bootcamps, typically **one to six months**, **run by private firms for profit**, aimed at people retraining for tech. "Some are very high quality, but others exist primarily to separate people (often from low-income backgrounds) from their money" [McMi2017].
- **[Thay2017]** interviewed **26 bootcamp alumni** from bootcamps offering a second chance to people who had missed computing education earlier. (Wilson notes that phrasing this as "missed earlier opportunities" makes "some pretty big assumptions" about people from underrepresented groups.) Findings:
  - Students face high personal costs and risks: significant time, money, and effort before, during, and after; a career change could take a year or more.
  - Several felt employers looked down on their certificates. Getting a job means passing an interview, but interviewers often won't say why they rejected someone, so it's hard to know what to fix or learn.
  - Many resorted to **internships** (paid or not) and spent a lot of time on **portfolios** and **networking**.
  - The three informal barriers most clearly identified: **knowledge (really jargon)**, **impostor syndrome**, and **a sense of not fitting in**.
- **[Burk2018]** compared what tech recruiters want with what 4-year degrees and bootcamps provide. They interviewed **15 hiring managers** from firms of various sizes and ran focus groups:
  - recruiters uniformly emphasized **soft skills**, especially teamwork, communication, and the ability to keep learning;
  - many companies **required a 4-year degree**, though not necessarily in CS;
  - many **praised bootcamp graduates** for being older or more mature and having more up-to-date knowledge.
- **How to approach bootcamps and similar groups:** your best strategy may be to **emphasize what you know about teaching rather than tech**, since many founders and staff have programming backgrounds but little or no training in education. The book's first few chapters "have played well with this audience in the past", and [Lang2016] describes evidence-based practices that can be adopted with minimal effort at low cost. These may not have the most impact, but "scoring a few early wins helps build support for larger and riskier efforts."

### §15.3 Final Thoughts
- "It is impossible to change large institutions on your own: you need allies, and to get allies, you need tactics."
- **[Mann2015] (*Fearless Change*)** is the most useful guide Wilson has found. It catalogs more than four dozen patterns, organized by whether they're best used early, later, throughout the change cycle, or when you meet resistance. Four the book highlights:
  - **Small Successes:** celebrate even small successes, to avoid being overwhelmed by everything a change effort involves.
  - **In Your Space:** keep the new idea visible by placing reminders throughout the organization.
  - **Token:** hand out tokens identified with the topic, to keep the idea alive in people's memory.
  - **Champion Skeptic:** ask strong opinion leaders who are skeptical to play the role of "official skeptic"; use their comments to improve your effort, even if you don't change their minds.
- **[Farm2006]: ten tongue-in-cheek rules for making sure a new tool is *not* adopted**, all of which apply to teaching practices too (verbatim list):
  1. Make it optional.
  2. Economize on training.
  3. Don't use it in a real project.
  4. Never integrate it.
  5. Use it sporadically.
  6. Make it part of a quality initiative.
  7. Marginalize the champion.
  8. Capitalize on early missteps.
  9. Make a small investment.
  10. Exploit fear, uncertainty, doubt, laziness, and inertia.
- **The most important strategy:** be willing to **change your goals** based on what you learn from the people you're trying to help. Tutorials on using a spreadsheet might help them more quickly and reliably than an introduction to JavaScript. Wilson: "I have often made the mistake of confusing things I was passionate about with things that other people ought to know". If you truly want to be a partner, "learning and change have to go both ways."

## Rules
- **PTN-1** — Address partners' fears directly: of looking stupid in front of students, of bumpy transitions hurting course evaluations, and of abandoning the teachers who inspired them. Don't argue about whether the fears are "real". *Why:* fear is the main reason faculty don't adopt better methods (Herckis); people "would rather fail than change". *Check:* does the plan name each fear and include a mitigation (e.g. private practice, rehearsal, a low-stakes pilot)? (§15.1)
- **PTN-2** — Don't lead with research results when persuading teachers; lead with cost/benefit (time saved), engagement payoffs, role models, and trusted peers in the same situation. *Why:* in [Bark2015], CS faculty mostly didn't find education research a credible reason to try a practice; time savings, role models, and trusted sources drove trials. *Check:* is the pitch's main argument a citation, or a peer's success and a time-saving? (§15.1)
- **PTN-3** — Plan for the known adoption costs: the initial slowdown (Glass's Law) and physical constraints such as theater-style seating. *Why:* advocates often ignored these faculty concerns [Bark2015]. *Check:* does the proposal say how the first-term slowdown is absorbed and whether the room supports the practice? (§15.1)
- **PTN-4** — Build continued use on student feedback and visible engagement, not on mandates alone; read student feedback with care. *Why:* student feedback is the strongest reason to keep a practice; mandated practices regress when incentives or monitoring stop; students' self-reports don't correlate strongly with learning [Bark2015]. *Check:* is there a feedback loop that will still exist after any mandate ends, and is learning measured separately from satisfaction? (§15.1)
- **PTN-5** — Approach institutional change systematically and expect it to be slow. *Why:* evidence-based medical practices take an average of 17 years to reach routine use and only about half are ever widely adopted [Baue2015]; implementation science exists to tackle this. *Check:* does the plan have multi-year horizons and explicit adoption steps rather than a single launch? (§15.1)
- **PTN-6** — Find out what the people you want to help believe they need before proposing anything; listen before you speak. *Why:* echoes Ch 13; [Yada2016] shows teachers have specific preparation and support needs. *Check:* is there a record of conversations with prospective partner teachers before the proposal was written? (§15.1)
- **PTN-7** — Make changes incrementally, within the institution's own frameworks (programs, certifications, cohorts). *Why:* [Nara2018] tripled graduation rates with cohorts and administrative support; [Hu2017] quadrupled computing teachers with a six-month certification without losing quality. *Check:* does the change fit an existing institutional structure rather than demand a new one? (§15.1)
- **PTN-8** — Classify your initiative on [Borr2014]'s two axes (individual vs. system; prescribed vs. emergent) and choose strategies for that category. As an outsider in Individual/Emergent, help teachers reflect on their teaching, run live coding sessions so they learn to program, and demonstrate your curriculum. *Why:* the change agent's role differs by category. *Check:* is the category named, and do the activities match it? (§15.1)
- **PTN-9** — When approaching bootcamps and similar organizations, lead with teaching expertise, offer low-effort, low-cost, evidence-based practices ([Lang2016]), and aim for early wins. *Why:* their staff often know programming but not education; early wins build support for bigger, riskier efforts. *Check:* does the offer focus on pedagogy, and is the first step small? (§15.2)
- **PTN-10** — Vet bootcamps or for-profit partners for quality and for how they treat learners, and plan support for bootcamp learners' known barriers: jargon, impostor syndrome, not fitting in, opaque rejections, and the need for portfolios, networking, internships, and soft skills. *Why:* some bootcamps exist mainly to take money from low-income learners [McMi2017]; alumni face high costs and informal barriers [Thay2017]; recruiters prize soft skills and often require degrees [Burk2018]. *Check:* does the partnership plan address each named barrier and recruiters' soft-skill emphasis? (§15.2)
- **PTN-11** — Recruit allies with deliberate change tactics, e.g. celebrate Small Successes, keep the idea visible (In Your Space, Token), and make a skeptical opinion leader your Champion Skeptic. *Why:* "It is impossible to change large institutions on your own"; [Mann2015] catalogs patterns by phase. *Check:* does the plan name tactics for early, later, ongoing, and resistance phases? (§15.3)
- **PTN-12** — Audit any adoption plan against Farmer's ten anti-adoption rules and invert each one that appears (e.g. don't make it optional, invest in training, use it on real work, integrate it, use it consistently, protect the champion, make a real investment). *Why:* each rule reliably kills adoption of tools and teaching practices [Farm2006]. *Check:* run the Anti-adoption audit below. (§15.3)
- **PTN-13** — Be willing to change your goals based on what partners need, even if it means teaching spreadsheets instead of JavaScript. *Why:* don't confuse what you're passionate about with what others need to know; "learning and change have to go both ways". *Check:* has the offering changed in response to partner input, and can you say what? (§15.3)

## Procedures

### P1. Prepare to approach an institution (§15 intro, §15.1)
1. Decide whether working with this institution is worthwhile for your goals (the chapter's framing: how, and when it's worthwhile).
2. Listen first: meet a few prospective partners informally ("a cup of tea") and ask what they need; consult sources like [Yada2016] for typical needs (PTN-6).
3. List their likely fears (looking stupid, evaluations, loyalty to inspiring lecturers) and concerns (initial slowdown, room layout) (PTN-1, PTN-3).
4. Build the case around what moves them: time saved, engagement, role models, trusted peers. Don't lead with study results (PTN-2).
5. Revise your own goals in light of what you heard (PTN-13).

### P2. Choose a change strategy ([Borr2014], §15.1)
1. Place the initiative on two axes: individual teachers vs. whole system; prescribed (top-down) vs. emergent (bottom-up).
2. Decision point: as an outsider approaching teachers one by one, you are probably **Individual/Emergent**. Then:
   - run reflective sessions where teachers examine their own teaching, alone or in groups;
   - do live coding sessions with teachers so they learn to program;
   - demonstrate your existing curriculum.
3. For other categories, consult [Borr2014] and [Hend2015a, Hend2015b]; the book doesn't detail their strategies.
4. Fit the change into existing frameworks (a certification, a cohort program) and move incrementally (PTN-7).

### P3. Sustain adoption (§15.1, §15.3)
1. Set up a student-feedback loop and track engagement (e.g. attendance), knowing self-reports don't measure learning (PTN-4).
2. Don't rely on mandates alone; expect regression when monitoring ends.
3. Use change patterns: celebrate small successes, place reminders, hand out tokens, recruit a Champion Skeptic (PTN-11).
4. Run the Farmer anti-adoption audit and fix each item present (PTN-12).
5. Plan on a multi-year horizon (PTN-5).

### P4. Approach a bootcamp or other non-school organization (§15.2)
1. Check the organization's quality and how it treats learners (PTN-10).
2. Lead with teaching expertise; offer material like the book's early chapters and [Lang2016]'s low-cost practices (PTN-9).
3. Propose a small, low-risk first step aimed at an early win.
4. Where you support bootcamp learners, address jargon, impostor syndrome, belonging, portfolios, networking, internships, and soft skills (teamwork, communication, continued learning).

## Diagnostics
| Symptom (in a lesson, class, or community) | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Faculty nod at the proposal but never try it | Unaddressed fear of looking stupid or of bad evaluations | PTN-1 |
| A pitch full of citations persuades no one in a CS department | Researchers distrust education research as a reason to change | PTN-2 |
| Teachers abandon the practice after the first term | Glass's Law slowdown not planned for; room layout fights the practice | PTN-3 |
| Practice disappears once the dean stops checking | Adoption sustained only by mandate | PTN-4 |
| Group expects a school district to adopt its curriculum within a year | Unrealistic timeline; no systematic implementation plan | PTN-5 |
| Proposal written before talking to any teachers | Not listening first | PTN-6 |
| Plan requires a brand-new degree or a long retraining program | Not working within existing frameworks; not incremental | PTN-7 |
| Outsider tries to mandate changes to a whole department | Mismatched change category (acting system/prescribed from an individual/emergent position) | PTN-8 |
| Pitch to a bootcamp is about a new language or framework | Leading with tech rather than teaching expertise | PTN-9 |
| Partnering with a bootcamp that leaves low-income learners in debt without jobs | Partner not vetted; learner barriers ignored | PTN-10 |
| Lone champion burns out; skeptics block the initiative | No allies or tactics; skeptics not engaged | PTN-11 |
| New practice is "optional", training is skimped, used only in toy settings | Farmer's anti-adoption rules in effect | PTN-12 |
| Group keeps pushing JavaScript when partners need spreadsheets | Confusing your passion with partners' needs | PTN-13 |

## Templates and checklists

### Partner needs interview (§15.1)
```
- What are you trying to achieve with your learners this year?
- What preparation or support would help you most? (cf. [Yada2016])
- What worries you about changing how you teach? (looking stupid / evaluations / losing what inspired you)
- What would make a new practice worth your time? (time saved? engagement?)
- Whom do you trust for teaching advice?
- What does your classroom look like? (layout constraints)
```

### Adoption-drivers map ([Bark2015], §15.1)
| Stage | Drivers to use | Concerns to address |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing about it | Problem-solving search (esp. engagement), institutional initiatives, colleagues, conferences | — |
| Trying it | Incentives (e.g. promotion), cost/benefit (time saved), role models, trusted peers | Glass's Law slowdown; classroom layout; distrust of education research |
| Keeping it | Student feedback, attendance as an engagement signal, institutional requirements | Self-reports ≠ learning; regression when mandates lapse |

### Borrego and Henderson positioning grid ([Borr2014], §15.1)
```
                    PRESCRIBED (top-down)       EMERGENT (bottom-up)
INDIVIDUAL          (see Borr2014/Hend2015)     Typical outsider start: teacher reflection
                                                (individual/group), live coding with teachers,
                                                demonstrate curriculum
SYSTEM (whole)      (see Borr2014/Hend2015)     (see Borr2014/Hend2015)
```

### Change patterns starter set ([Mann2015], §15.3)
```
Small Successes  — celebrate even small wins to avoid overwhelm
In Your Space    — keep the idea visible with reminders around the organization
Token            — hand out tokens tied to the topic to keep it in memory
Champion Skeptic — make a skeptical opinion leader the "official skeptic"; use their comments
(Mann2015 has 48+ patterns, grouped: early / later / throughout / when meeting resistance)
```

### Anti-adoption audit ([Farm2006], §15.3)
```
Is the plan doing any of these? (Each one kills adoption.)
[ ] Make it optional
[ ] Economize on training
[ ] Don't use it in a real project
[ ] Never integrate it
[ ] Use it sporadically
[ ] Make it part of a quality initiative
[ ] Marginalize the champion
[ ] Capitalize on early missteps
[ ] Make a small investment
[ ] Exploit fear, uncertainty, doubt, laziness, and inertia
```

### Bootcamp-learner barrier checklist ([Thay2017], [Burk2018], §15.2)
```
[ ] Jargon (knowledge barrier)
[ ] Impostor syndrome
[ ] Sense of not fitting in
[ ] Opaque interview rejections (help learners get feedback)
[ ] Portfolios, networking, internships
[ ] Soft skills: teamwork, communication, ability to keep learning
[ ] Time/money/effort costs; career change may take a year or more
```

## Examples
- **Herckis on faculty:** the main barrier is fear of looking stupid in front of students; secondary barriers are course evaluations and emulating inspiring lecturers (§15.1).
- **Veterans Administration** [Baue2015]: 17 years on average from evidence to routine practice; only about half of evidence-based practices ever widely adopted (§15.1).
- **Three-year bachelor's program** [Nara2018]: tight cohorts plus administrative support tripled graduation rates (§15.1).
- **Six-month CS teacher certification** [Hu2017]: replacing a two-year, five-course route quadrupled computing teachers (flat 2007–2013) without diluting quality; students' self-reported learning tracked their prior beliefs, not the teacher's CS background (§15.1).
- **Bootcamp alumni** [Thay2017]: 26 interviews; high costs, stigmatized certificates, opaque rejections, internships, portfolios, networking; jargon, impostor syndrome, not fitting in (§15.2).
- **Hiring managers** [Burk2018]: 15 interviews plus focus groups; soft skills valued; degrees often required; bootcamp grads praised for maturity and current knowledge (§15.2).
- **Spreadsheets vs. JavaScript:** spreadsheet tutorials may help partners faster and more reliably than JavaScript; Wilson admits confusing his passions with others' needs (§15.3).
- **Wilson's own record:** most attempts to change large institutions were unproductive, which is part of why he left a university to re-start Software Carpentry in 2010 (§15 intro).

## Evidence and caveats
- **Chapter-level hedge:** drawn "more from things I have seen than from things I have done"; Wilson's own institutional change efforts mostly failed. Treat the strategic advice as observed practice, not tested method.
- **Herckis** (named, but with no citation key in the text): fear of looking stupid as the main reason faculty don't adopt better methods.
- **[Bark2015]:** a qualitative two-part study of CS faculty adoption. The key finding is that faculty mostly didn't consider education research results credible reasons to change. The book says this is "consistent with other findings" but cites none here.
- **Student feedback caveat:** it is the strongest retention driver, but "students' self-reports don't correlate strongly with learning outcomes" (stated as known; no key given here).
- **[Baue2015]:** the 17-year average and about-half adoption figures come from medicine (US Veterans Administration), applied to education by analogy.
- **[Hu2017]:** the "as effective" claim is hedged ("seemed to be as effective"); the authors' quote suggests student self-reported learning tracks prior beliefs, not teacher background.
- **[Nara2018]:** a single-program result (graduation rates tripled).
- **[Borr2014]:** the book gives only the two axes and the Individual/Emergent strategies; the other three quadrants' strategies are not described in this text. The EPUB contains no figure for this.
- **[Thay2017]:** interview study (n = 26); Wilson critiques its framing ("missed earlier opportunities").
- **[Burk2018]:** interviews (n = 15) plus focus groups.
- **[McMi2017]:** some for-profit programs exploit low-income learners; a book-length critique.
- **[Lang2016]** practices "may not have the most impact", but early wins build support.
- **[Farm2006]** is explicitly "tongue-in-cheek"; **[Mann2015]** is a pattern catalog, not experimental evidence.

## Practice exercises
- **Collaborations** — small groups, 30 min: individually answer, then compare: existing agreements with other groups, which relationships you want, how collaborations help your goals, your key collaborators, whether they're the right ones, and which groups you'd like agreements with (§15.4).
- **Educationalization** — whole class, 10 min: read Labaree's argument [Laba2008] that societies push social problems onto education, which fails at them while serving as a showplace for ideals and a convenient scapegoat; discuss how teaching computational thinking and digital citizenship in schools fits this framework (§15.4).
- **Institutional Adoption** — whole class, 15 min: re-read §15.1's motivations for adopting practices; which apply to you and colleagues, which are irrelevant, and which do you emphasize with formal educational institutions? (§15.4)
- **Making It Fail** — small groups, 15 min: re-read Farmer's ten anti-adoption rules; which have you seen done recently, which have you done yourself, and in what form? (§15.4)
- **Mentoring** — whole class, 15 min: read the Institute for African-American Mentoring in Computer Science's guidelines for mentoring doctoral students (iaamcs.org/guidelines) individually, then as a class rate your own group on each item as +1 (definitely doing), −1 (definitely not), or 0 (unsure or not applicable) (§15.4).
