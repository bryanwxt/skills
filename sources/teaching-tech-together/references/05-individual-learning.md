# Individual Learning

> Source: Greg Wilson, *Teaching Tech Together* (2018), Ch. 5 "Individual Learning" (§5.1–§5.4), appendix "Teamwork Rubric" (Appendix K). Licensed CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/); condensed and restructured for skill use, with quotations marked. Rule IDs use prefix `IND`.

## When a skill needs this
- A study-skills coach or course designer builds a study plan or review schedule (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, elaboration, concrete examples, dual coding) and teaches learners the strategies by name.
- An exercise generator produces flash cards, read-cover-retrieve sheets, "solve twice" tasks, guided notes, or ADEPT-structured explanations.
- A workshop or course planner advises learners (or a team) on workload, all-nighters, interruptions and focus blocks.
- A course designer sets up peer assessment, calibrated peer review, contributing-student pedagogy, or a team self- and peer-evaluation using the Teamwork Rubric.
- A myth-checker answers claims such as "far transfer from chess to maths", "learning styles", "digital natives", "multi-tasking works" or "crunch time is productive".

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Metacognition | Thinking about one's own thinking: planning, setting goals, monitoring progress. |
| Transfer of learning | When learning one thing helps you learn another more quickly. |
| Near transfer | Transfer between similar areas (fractions ↔ decimals; loops in different languages). Common. |
| Far transfer | Transfer between dissimilar domains (chess ↔ maths). Rare [Sala2017]. |
| Spaced practice | Spreading study over time instead of cramming. |
| Retrieval practice | Practising recall (tests, summaries from memory) to strengthen access to memories. |
| Transfer-appropriate processing | Recall is better when practice resembles the test format. |
| Read-cover-retrieve | Cover key terms with sticky notes while reading, then try to recall them on a second pass. |
| Hypercorrection effect | The more confident someone was in a wrong answer, the more likely they are to fix it once corrected [Metc2016]. |
| Interleaving | Mixing study of different topics, ideally in a varied order. |
| Elaboration / self-explanation | Explaining to yourself why an answer is right or wrong and how ideas relate. |
| Two-stage exam | An individual exam immediately followed by a small-group exam on the same problems [Cao2017a, Cao2017b]. |
| Concrete examples | Pairing every general principle with specific instances, and vice versa. |
| ADEPT | Analogy, Diagram, Example, Plain language, Technical details. |
| Teaching by contrast | Showing what a solution is *not*, or what a technique *won't* solve. |
| Dual coding | Presenting complementary words and images together. |
| Ego depletion | Willpower runs out with use, like a muscle [Mill2016a]. |
| Automaticity | Doing a routine task in the background while doing something else. |
| Flow | The fully immersed, highly productive state ("in the zone") [Csik2008]. |
| Peer assessment | Learners rate or review one another's work or contributions. |
| Contributing student pedagogy | Students produce artifacts that help other students learn. |
| Calibrated peer review | Students practise reviewing against an instructor's review until they agree closely, then review peers [Kulk2013]. |
| Guided notes | Instructor-prepared notes with cues (blanks, asterisks) for students to fill in during a lecture. |
| Observables | Behaviours that can be seen (e.g., "listens attentively"), as opposed to personality traits. |

## Concepts

### Chapter 5 opening (unnumbered): metacognition and transfer
- Ch 2–4 cover what *instructors* can do. Ch 5 covers what *learners* can do for themselves: change their study strategies and get enough rest.
- **Metacognition is the key.** Good musicians listen to their own playing and good teachers reflect on their teaching (Ch 8). In the same way, learners learn better and faster if they make plans, set goals and monitor progress.
  - These skills are hard to master in the abstract. "Just telling them to make plans doesn't have any effect."
  - Lessons *can* be designed to encourage particular study practices, and drawing attention to those practices in class shows learners that **learning is a skill that can be improved** [McGu2015, Miya2018].
- **Transfer of learning** is "the big prize."
  - **Near transfer** "undoubtedly occurs"; without it, no learning beyond memorization could happen. Instructors use it constantly by giving exercises close in form or content to what was just presented.
  - **Far transfer**: [Sala2017]'s analysis of many studies found "small to moderate effects. However, the effect sizes are inversely related to the quality of the experimental design… We conclude that far transfer of learning rarely occurs."
  - When far transfer does occur, it seems to happen only after a subject has been **mastered** [Gick1987]. In practice, "learning to program won't help you play chess and vice versa."

### 5.1 Six Strategies
- Psychologists study learning in many ways but have reached similar conclusions about what works [Mark2018]. **The Learning Scientists** catalogued six strategies and published downloadable posters about them. **Teaching these strategies to students, and naming them when you use them in class**, helps students learn how to learn faster and better [Wein2018].

#### Spaced Practice
- Ten hours of study over five days beats two five-hour days, which is "far better" than one ten-hour day. Build a schedule that spreads study out: **block off at least half an hour per topic per day** instead of cramming the night before the exam [Kang2016].
- **Review after each class, but not immediately:** take at least a half-hour break first. Include some older material, e.g., **20 minutes on that day's notes, then 5 minutes each on the previous day's and on material from a week before.** This also catches gaps and mistakes in earlier notes while there is still time to fix them or ask (the "Demodulate!!" joke: discovering the night before the exam that you don't know why you underlined something three times).
- While reviewing, **note what you had forgotten**: make a flash card for each fact you couldn't remember or remembered wrongly, so the next round focuses on what needs it most.
- **"The Value of Lectures" box** [Mill2016a]: lectures "are relatively ineffective ways to teach, but they probably contribute to spacing material over time," because they follow a set schedule. Online students can sometimes avoid the material entirely "until an assignment is nigh."

#### Retrieval Practice
- The limiting factor for long-term memory is believed to be **recall** (what can be accessed), not **retention** (what is stored). Recall improves with practice, so take practice tests or summarize a topic from memory and then check what you missed. [Karp2008]: repeated testing improved recall of word lists **from 35% to 80%**.
- **Transfer-appropriate processing:** recall is better when practice resembles the test. Writing personal journal entries helps with multiple-choice quizzes, but less than doing multiple-choice quizzes does [Mill2016a].
- **Methods:**
  - **Solve problems twice.** First entirely from memory, with no notes or peers. Grade it yourself against an instructor-supplied rubric. Then solve it again with any resources you like. The difference shows how well you retrieved and applied your knowledge.
  - **Flash cards.** A prompt on one side and the answer on the other; digital versions suit phones. In a group, swap cards with a partner, which also reveals ideas you missed or misunderstood.
  - **Read-cover-retrieve.** While reading, cover key terms or sections with small sticky notes. On a second pass, guess what's under each one.
  - **Don't only drill facts and definitions.** Check understanding of big ideas and the connections between them: sketch a concept map and compare it to your notes or an earlier map.
- **"Hypercorrection" box** [Metc2016]. You might expect that the more confident someone is in a wrong answer, the harder it is to change their mind. The opposite is true: "the more confident someone is that they were right, the more likely they are not to repeat the error if they are corrected."

#### Interleaving
- Space practice by **interleaving** topics: shuffle study sessions instead of mastering one subject, then the next. Better still, vary the order: **A-B-C-B-A-C beats A-B-C-A-B-C, which beats A-A-B-B-C-C** [Rohrer2015]. It works because interleaving creates more links between topics, which improves retention and recall.
- **Session length per item:** it depends on the subject and how well you know it, but **10–30 minutes** is long enough to reach flow (§5.2) without your mind wandering.
- **It will feel harder at first,** and that is "a sign that it's working." With flash cards or practice tests you should see improvement **after only a couple of days**.

#### Elaboration
- Explaining things to yourself as you go helps you understand and remember them. Ways to do it:
  - After each practice-quiz answer, explain **why it's correct**, or **why another plausible answer isn't**.
  - Tell yourself how a new idea is **similar to or different from** one you've seen before.
- [Biel1995] explicitly trained people in self-explanation, and they outperformed untrained people.
- **Group exercise:** go through code line by line with a different person explaining each line in turn: why it is there and what it does.
- [Chi1989] (physics mechanics problems): some learners simply halt at an unexplained step, or one whose explanation they don't understand. Others **pause their "execution" of the example to generate an explanation**, and they learn faster. **Instructors should demonstrate the latter strategy.**
- **Explaining to others even works on exams**, though the extent of the benefit is still being studied. **Two-stage exams** [Cao2017a, Cao2017b]: a normal individual exam immediately followed by a small-group exam on a set of problems.
  - They found significant **short-term** gains but **not long-term** ones: the benefit seen a couple of weeks after the mid-term had faded by the final.
  - **Students in the middle of the class benefited strongly.**
  - **Homogeneous-ability groups benefited; heterogeneous groups did not.**

#### Concrete Examples
- A specific form of elaboration. For every general principle, give one or more examples of its use. Conversely, for each particular problem, list the general principles it embodies. [Raws2014]: **interleaving examples and definitions** made learners more likely to remember the definitions correctly.
- **ADEPT:** give an **A**nalogy, draw a **D**iagram, present an **E**xample, describe the idea in **P**lain language, then give the **T**echnical details.
- **In pairs or groups:** swap work and check whether each other's examples really embody the principle, and which principles an example uses that the author didn't list.
- **Teach by contrast:** show what a solution is *not*, or what kind of problem a technique *won't* solve. E.g., when teaching children to simplify fractions, include a few like 5/7 that can't be simplified, so they don't get frustrated searching for answers that don't exist.

#### Dual Coding
- Present **words and images together.** Different brain subsystems handle linguistic and visual information (§4.1). Complementary information on both channels reinforces itself, but the *same* information on both at once forces the brain to cross-check them [Maye2003].
- **Ways to apply it:** draw or label timelines, maps, family trees or anything else that fits the material. Wilson likes call graphs showing which functions call which.
- **Drawing a diagram without labels, then labelling it later, "is excellent retrieval practice."**

### 5.2 Time Management
- **Wilson's anecdote:** as a student he bragged about all-nighters (showing up at noon, unshaven, mentioning he'd worked until 6 a.m.). He later saw how much of that work he threw away after sleeping, and how much the rest damaged his grades. His mistake was confusing "working" with "being productive."
- **Findings on overwork and sleep deprivation** (studied since at least the 1890s; summary in [Robi2005]):
  - Working **more than eight hours a day for an extended period lowers total productivity**, not just hourly productivity: crunch mode gets less done in total.
  - Working **more than 21 hours in a stretch** raises the odds of a catastrophic error as much as being **legally drunk**.
  - Productivity peaks in the **first four to six hours** of the workday. After enough hours it approaches zero and eventually goes negative.
  - These results "have been reproduced and verified for over a century," with data "as solid as the data linking smoking to lung cancer."
  - **People don't notice their own decline.** Like drunk drivers who think they're fine, sleep-deprived people don't realize they aren't finishing their sentences or thoughts.
  - **Five eight-hour days a week** has been shown to maximize long-term total output in every industry studied. Studying and programming are no different.
- **Short bursts (all-nighters):** "Your ability to think drops by 25% for each 24 hours you're awake." The average person's effective IQ is about 75 after one all-nighter (bottom 5% of the population) and about 50 after two, the level at which people are usually judged incapable of independent living.
- **"When You Just Can't Say No" box:** willpower runs out like a muscle. Resisting the last donut makes you less likely to fold the laundry, and vice versa. This is **ego depletion** [Mill2016a]. The counter is to **build habits** so that doing the right thing is automatic.
- **"So many assignments, all due at once!"** The answer isn't extra hours but prioritizing and focusing, and learners must be **taught how**. Technique:
  1. List what needs doing.
  2. Sort it by priority.
  3. **Switch off email and other interruptions for 30–60 minutes** and complete one task.
  4. Break any task longer than an hour into smaller pieces and prioritize those separately.
- **Switching off interruptions matters most.** People aren't good at multi-tasking. What we *can* become good at is **automaticity**, doing something routine in the background [Mill2016a]: talking while chopping onions, drinking coffee while reading, and with practice taking notes while listening. But we can't study, program or do other mentally demanding tasks while attending to something else.
- **Flow** [Csik2008] ("in the zone"; musicians "losing themselves") produces much more per unit of time. The bad news: it takes **about ten minutes to get back into flow after an interruption**, however short the interruption. Interrupted half a dozen times an hour, "you are never at your productive peak."

### 5.3 Peer Assessment
- Peer rating within teams is common in industry. [Sond2012] surveyed student peer assessment, distinguishing **grading** from **reviewing**.
  - **Benefits:** more, more diverse and more timely feedback; practice in higher-level thinking; encouragement of reflective practice; development of social skills.
  - **Concerns:** validity and reliability, motivation and procrastination, trolls, collusion, plagiarism. These are legitimate, "the evidence shows that they aren't significant in class."
  - [Kauf2000] compared confidential peer ratings and grades in two undergraduate engineering courses. Self-ratings and peer ratings statistically agreed; collusion (everyone giving peers the same grades) wasn't significant; students didn't inflate self-ratings; and, crucially, **ratings weren't biased by gender or race**.
- **Contributing student pedagogy:** students produce artifacts for other students' learning, such as a short lesson shared with the class, additions to a question bank, or write-ups of lecture notes for in-class publication. [Fran2018]: students who made short videos teaching concepts to peers learned significantly more than those who only studied the material or watched the videos.
- **Calibrated peer review** [Kulk2013]: a student reviews one or more examples with a rubric and compares their evaluation with the instructor's review of the same work. **Only when the student's evaluations are close enough to the instructor's** may they review peers' actual work.
- **Observables, not personality traits.** Peer assessment "can actually be as accurate as assessment by TAs and other outsiders" **as long as evaluation is based on observables**. Ask "Does the person listen attentively during meetings?" or "Does the person attempt to solve problems before asking for help?", not "Is the person outgoing?" or "Does the person have a positive attitude?"
- **The Teamwork Rubric (Appendix K)** is a starter form: rate yourself and each teammate, then calculate and compare the scores.

## Rules
- **IND-1** — Teach learners the six study strategies by name, and name them whenever a class activity uses one. *Why:* teaching and naming the strategies helps learners learn how to learn [Wein2018]; it shows learning is an improvable skill [McGu2015, Miya2018]. *Check:* the course materials introduce the six strategies, and activities are labelled with the one they use. (§5.1, Ch 5 intro)
- **IND-2** — Build metacognitive habits through lesson design (planning, goal-setting and progress-monitoring activities), not by simply telling learners to plan. *Why:* "just telling them to make plans doesn't have any effect." *Check:* the plan contains concrete structured activities for planning and monitoring, not exhortations. (Ch 5 intro)
- **IND-3** — Rely on near transfer: give exercises close in form or content to what was just taught. Don't promise far transfer ("programming improves your chess/maths/logic"). *Why:* near transfer is real; far transfer rarely occurs, and only after mastery [Sala2017, Gick1987]. *Check:* course copy makes no far-transfer claims, and practice items resemble the taught material. (Ch 5 intro)
- **IND-4** — Schedule spaced study: at least half an hour per topic per day, never cramming. *Why:* spreading the same hours over more days is more effective [Kang2016]. *Check:* the study plan distributes each topic across several days. (§5.1)
- **IND-5** — Advise reviewing after each class, after at least a 30-minute break, mixing in older material (e.g., 20 minutes today, 5 minutes yesterday, 5 minutes last week) and making flash cards for anything forgotten. *Why:* spacing; and it catches note errors early. *Check:* the review routine includes a delay, older material, and capture of forgotten items. (§5.1)
- **IND-6** — Use retrieval practice (practice tests, recall-then-check summaries, flash cards, read-cover-retrieve, solve-twice) rather than re-reading. *Why:* recall, not retention, limits memory; repeated testing raised recall from 35% to 80% [Karp2008]. *Check:* each unit includes at least one recall-from-memory activity with a check against the source. (§5.1)
- **IND-7** — Make practice resemble the eventual assessment format. *Why:* transfer-appropriate processing [Mill2016a]. *Check:* if the exam is MCQ, practice includes MCQs; if it is coding, practice includes coding. (§5.1)
- **IND-8** — Include retrieval of big ideas and connections, not only facts. *Why:* understanding needs connections; concept-map recall checks them. *Check:* the retrieval activities include drawing and comparing a concept map or explaining relationships. (§5.1)
- **IND-9** — Correct confidently held errors directly. Don't avoid them out of fear of resistance. *Why:* hypercorrection: high-confidence errors are the most likely to be fixed once corrected [Metc2016]. *Check:* feedback on quizzes gives the right answer even (especially) for confident wrong answers. (§5.1)
- **IND-10** — Interleave topics in a varied order (A-B-C-B-A-C) with about 10–30 minutes per item, and warn learners it will feel harder. *Why:* it creates more cross-topic links and improves retention and recall [Rohrer2015]; difficulty is "a sign that it's working." *Check:* practice sets and schedules mix topics non-cyclically, and learners are told to expect difficulty. (§5.1)
- **IND-11** — Prompt elaboration: after each practice answer, have learners explain why it is right, or why a plausible alternative is wrong, and how new ideas relate to old ones. *Why:* self-explanation training improves performance [Biel1995]. *Check:* quizzes and exercises include "explain why" follow-ups. (§5.1)
- **IND-12** — Demonstrate pausing at an unexplained step to generate an explanation. *Why:* learners who do this learn faster than those who halt [Chi1989]. *Check:* worked examples and live demos model "let me stop and explain why this step works." (§5.1)
- **IND-13** — Use line-by-line group explanation of code, with each person explaining one line's purpose. *Why:* this is elaboration in a group. *Check:* code-reading activities assign lines to individuals for explanation. (§5.1)
- **IND-14** — If you use two-stage (individual then group) exams, expect short-term but not long-term gains, the strongest benefit for mid-ranked students, and benefits only in homogeneous-ability groups. *Why:* [Cao2017a, Cao2017b]; the extent of the benefits is "still being studied." *Check:* group composition for two-stage exams is ability-homogeneous, and claims of lasting benefit aren't made. (§5.1)
- **IND-15** — Pair every general principle with concrete examples, and every example with the principles it embodies. Interleave examples with definitions. *Why:* [Raws2014]: interleaving examples and definitions improves correct recall of definitions. *Check:* no principle appears without an example, and examples are tied back to principles. (§5.1)
- **IND-16** — Structure new-concept introductions with ADEPT: Analogy, Diagram, Example, Plain language, Technical details. *Why:* it is a structured way to provide concrete examples. *Check:* the explanation contains all five elements in that order. (§5.1)
- **IND-17** — Teach by contrast: include non-examples and problems a technique won't solve (e.g., fractions that can't be simplified). *Why:* it prevents learners from hunting for answers that don't exist. *Check:* exercise sets include at least one non-example or "no solution" case. (§5.1)
- **IND-18** — Use dual coding: pair words with complementary images (timelines, maps, family trees, call graphs), but don't duplicate the same content in both channels at once. *Why:* complementary channels reinforce each other; redundant ones force cross-checking [Maye2003]. *Check:* visuals add information the words don't, and text isn't read out verbatim. (§5.1)
- **IND-19** — Use "draw unlabelled now, label later" diagrams as retrieval practice. *Why:* Wilson calls it "excellent retrieval practice." *Check:* the review activities include labelling previously drawn blank diagrams. (§5.1)
- **IND-20** — Discourage long hours and all-nighters, and recommend sustainable schedules (about eight hours a day, five days a week). *Why:* overwork lowers *total* output; more than 21 hours awake equals legal drunkenness in error risk; thinking ability drops 25% per 24 hours awake; people don't notice their own decline [Robi2005]. *Check:* schedules and deadlines don't require crunch, and advice to learners rejects all-nighters. (§5.2)
- **IND-21** — Teach prioritization and focus explicitly: list tasks, sort by priority, work 30–60-minute interruption-free blocks, and split any task over an hour. *Why:* learners must be taught how; extra hours aren't the answer. *Check:* study-skills material includes this procedure. (§5.2)
- **IND-22** — Eliminate interruptions and multi-tasking during demanding work. *Why:* people can't multi-task on demanding tasks (only routine automaticity), and it takes about ten minutes to regain flow after any interruption [Mill2016a, Csik2008]. *Check:* class and study design minimize notifications and context switches during focused work. (§5.2)
- **IND-23** — Counter willpower limits by building habits, not by relying on self-control. *Why:* ego depletion [Mill2016a]. *Check:* advice favours routines and automatic habits over "try harder." (§5.2)
- **IND-24** — Use peer assessment to increase the amount, diversity and timeliness of feedback. Don't reject it over fears of collusion or bias. *Why:* [Sond2012] benefits; [Kauf2000] found self and peer ratings agreed, no significant collusion, no self-inflation, and no gender or race bias. *Check:* the course includes peer review or rating with a rubric. (§5.3)
- **IND-25** — Base peer and team evaluations on observable behaviours, never on personality traits. *Why:* evaluations based on observables can be as accurate as those of TAs or outsiders. *Check:* every rubric item describes an action someone could be seen doing (e.g., "listens attentively"), not a trait ("is outgoing"). (§5.3)
- **IND-26** — Calibrate reviewers before they review peers: have them grade sample work with the rubric, compare against the instructor's grading, and let them review peers only once they are close enough. *Why:* calibrated peer review [Kulk2013]. *Check:* a calibration step with an agreement threshold precedes peer review. (§5.3)
- **IND-27** — Have learners create artifacts for other learners (mini-lessons, question-bank items, lecture write-ups, short teaching videos). *Why:* contributing student pedagogy; making teaching videos significantly increased the makers' own learning [Fran2018]. *Check:* at least one assignment produces material used by peers. (§5.3)
- **IND-28** — For team projects, have each member rate themselves and each teammate on the Teamwork Rubric, then calculate and compare the scores. *Why:* it is a ready-made, observables-based form (Appendix K). *Check:* the team-project plan includes self- and peer-ratings with score comparison. (§5.3, Appendix K)

## Procedures

### P1: Build a learner study plan
1. List the topics and the assessment dates.
2. Allocate **at least 30 minutes per topic per day**, spread over many days (IND-4).
3. **Interleave** topics in a varied order (A-B-C-B-A-C) in 10–30-minute blocks (IND-10).
4. After each class, wait at least 30 minutes, then review: **20 minutes** on today's notes, **5 minutes** on yesterday's, **5 minutes** on last week's (IND-5).
5. Make a flash card for every item forgotten or misremembered. Next session, start with those cards.
6. Replace re-reading with retrieval (IND-6): practice tests in the exam's format (IND-7), read-cover-retrieve, solve-twice, concept-map recall (IND-8).
7. After each practice answer, write a one-line "why right / why the alternative is wrong" (IND-11).
8. Keep sessions within sustainable hours. No all-nighters (IND-20). Work in 30–60-minute interruption-free blocks (IND-21, IND-22).
9. Tell learners that interleaving and retrieval feel harder and that this means they're working. Expect improvement within a couple of days.

### P2: Solve-twice retrieval exercise
1. The learner solves the problem from memory only, with no notes or peers.
2. The learner self-grades against the instructor's rubric.
3. The learner solves it again using any resources.
4. The learner compares the two attempts. The gap shows what they couldn't retrieve or apply, and that is what to study next.

### P3: Introduce a concept with ADEPT
1. **Analogy:** relate it to something familiar.
2. **Diagram:** draw it, ideally piece by piece (see `04-cognitive-load.md`).
3. **Example:** a concrete instance, plus a non-example (teach by contrast, IND-17).
4. **Plain language:** a jargon-free statement.
5. **Technical details:** the precise definition or syntax.
6. Optional peer check: partners verify each other's examples embody the principle and name principles left unlisted.

### P4: Set up calibrated peer review
1. Write a rubric of 5–10 items (for code, e.g., "good variable names", "no redundant code", "properly-nested control flow"). Items must be observable.
2. Prepare sample work with known problems (e.g., a small program with 3–4 rubric violations) and the instructor's graded review of it.
3. Each student grades the sample with the rubric.
4. Compare with the instructor's review. Discuss what the student accepted or criticized that the instructor didn't.
5. Repeat with further samples until the student's evaluations are close enough to the instructor's.
6. Only then assign peer work to review.

### P5: Run a team self- and peer-evaluation (Teamwork Rubric)
1. Give every team member the rubric (below).
2. Each member rates **themselves and each teammate** on every item: Yes / Iffy / No / N/A.
3. Calculate scores per person, from self-rating and from peer ratings.
   > **Skill note:** the book doesn't specify a scoring scheme. A simple choice is Yes = 2, Iffy = 1, No = 0, with N/A excluded from the denominator. That mapping is ours.
4. Compare the self score with the peers' average, and the scores across teammates. Discuss large gaps using the specific observable items.
5. Keep ratings confidential where appropriate (the [Kauf2000] study used confidential ratings).

### P6: Teach prioritization (for overloaded learners)
1. List every task.
2. Sort by priority.
3. Break any task longer than an hour into subtasks and prioritize those.
4. Switch off email and notifications. Work on one task for 30–60 minutes.
5. Repeat. Protect blocks from interruptions, since each costs about ten minutes of flow.
6. Build the routine into a habit so it doesn't depend on willpower (IND-23).

## Diagnostics
| Symptom (in a lesson, class, or community) | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Learners cram the night before and forget within days | No spacing | IND-4, IND-5, P1 |
| Learners re-read notes repeatedly and feel ready, then fail | Recognition mistaken for recall; no retrieval practice | IND-6, IND-7 |
| Practice is essays, the exam is MCQ (or vice versa) | Transfer-inappropriate practice | IND-7 |
| Learners can define terms but can't connect ideas | Fact-only retrieval | IND-8, IND-15 |
| Learners complain mixed practice "feels harder" and want blocked practice | A desirable difficulty of interleaving | IND-10 (explain it's working) |
| Learners stall at a step they don't understand in a worked example | No self-explanation habit | IND-12, IND-11 |
| The course claims "learning to code makes you better at maths / logic in general" | A far-transfer myth | IND-3 |
| A teacher tailors lessons to "learning styles" | A debunked myth [Guzd2015b, Kirs2013] | See Evidence; use IND-18 instead |
| Learners hunt endlessly for a simplification that doesn't exist | No non-examples taught | IND-17 |
| Slides read aloud verbatim | Redundant dual channels | IND-18 |
| Learners brag about all-nighters; quality drops near deadlines | Overwork and sleep deprivation; decline unnoticed | IND-20, P6 |
| Learners study with phones and chat open | Multi-tasking myth; flow lost | IND-22 |
| "I just need more willpower" | Ego depletion | IND-23 |
| The instructor is the only source of feedback, which is slow and sparse | No peer assessment | IND-24, IND-26 |
| Peer reviews are vague or wildly off | Reviewers uncalibrated | IND-26, P4 |
| Team ratings say "bad attitude" or "not a team player" | Trait-based rather than observable criteria | IND-25, IND-28 |
| Fear that peers will collude or be biased | Concern not borne out in class [Kauf2000] | IND-24 |
| Telling learners to "make a study plan" changes nothing | Metacognition taught in the abstract | IND-2 |

## Templates and checklists

**Six-strategy checklist (name each in class when used):**
| Strategy | Minimal implementation |
|---|---|
| Spaced practice | ≥30 min per topic per day; post-class review after ≥30 min: 20 min today + 5 min yesterday + 5 min last week |
| Retrieval practice | Practice tests in the exam format; solve-twice; flash cards; read-cover-retrieve; concept-map recall |
| Interleaving | Varied order (A-B-C-B-A-C), 10–30 min per item; expect it to feel harder |
| Elaboration | "Why is this right / why is that wrong?"; similarities and differences to known ideas; line-by-line group explanation |
| Concrete examples | Principle ↔ examples; ADEPT; teach by contrast |
| Dual coding | Complementary words and images; draw unlabelled, label later |

## Examples
- **"Demodulate!!"**: reviewing old notes regularly catches cryptic or wrong notes before the night before the exam (§5.1).
- **Word-list recall rising from 35% to 80%** with repeated testing [Karp2008] (§5.1).
- **Journal entries vs MCQ practice:** both help with MCQ quizzes, but MCQ practice helps more (transfer-appropriate processing) (§5.1).
- **Fractions like 5/7:** include unsimplifiable cases so children don't search for non-existent answers (teach by contrast) (§5.1).
- **Wilson's all-nighters:** he threw much of the work away after sleeping, and the rest hurt his grades. Working isn't the same as being productive (§5.2).
- **Donut and laundry:** willpower draws on a shared, depletable pool (ego depletion) (§5.2).
- **Talking while chopping onions:** automaticity is possible for routine tasks, but not for studying or programming alongside something else (§5.2).
- **Student-made teaching videos** [Fran2018]: the makers learned significantly more than students who only studied or watched (§5.3).
- **Engineering-course peer ratings** [Kauf2000]: self and peer ratings agreed, with no collusion, no inflation and no gender or race bias (§5.3).
- **Saskatchewan → statistics in a four-link chain** (exercise): practising elaboration by connecting unrelated ideas (§5.4).
- **Counting, alphabet, interleaved** (exercise): saying "1, A, 2, B, …" takes significantly longer than either task alone, a demonstration of the cost of multi-tasking (§5.4).

## Evidence and caveats
- **Metacognition can be fostered by lesson design and naming strategies** [McGu2015, Miya2018, Wein2018]. Simply telling learners to plan has no effect.
- **Far transfer "rarely occurs"** [Sala2017]: effect sizes shrink as study quality rises. It appears only after mastery [Gick1987]. **Treat "learning X makes you better at unrelated Y" as unsupported.**
- **Convergence of findings** [Mark2018]: different research traditions reach similar conclusions about what works.
- **Spacing** [Kang2016]. **Lectures** are "relatively ineffective" but probably aid spacing [Mill2016a].
- **Retrieval** [Karp2008]: recall from 35% to 80%. **Transfer-appropriate processing** [Mill2016a].
- **Hypercorrection** [Metc2016]: confident errors are corrected more readily.
- **Interleaving** [Rohrer2015]: a varied order beats a cyclic order, which beats blocked practice.
- **Self-explanation** [Biel1995, Chi1989].
- **Two-stage exams** [Cao2017a, Cao2017b]: short-term gains only; mid-ranked students gain most; homogeneous groups benefit and heterogeneous ones don't. Wilson says the extent of the benefits is **"still being studied."**
- **Examples interleaved with definitions** [Raws2014].
- **Dual coding / redundancy** [Maye2003].
- **Overwork and sleep data** [Robi2005]: Wilson calls it as solid as smoking-cancer data and reproduced for over a century. The specific numbers (21 hours ≈ legally drunk; −25% per 24 hours awake; IQ about 75 after one all-nighter, about 50 after two; peak in the first 4–6 hours) are as he reports them.
- **Ego depletion, automaticity** [Mill2016a]; **flow** [Csik2008]; about **10 minutes to regain flow** after any interruption (no specific key given).
- **Peer assessment** [Sond2012, Kauf2000]: the concerns are legitimate but not significant in class. **Contributing student pedagogy** [Fran2018]. **Calibrated peer review** [Kulk2013]. Peer assessment based on observables can match TA accuracy.
- **Debunked myths recorded in this chapter's exercises:**
  - **Learning styles** ("good teachers personalize education for students' learning styles"): listed as a myth in [Guzd2015b]. [Kirs2013] treats learning styles as an "urban legend".
  - **Digital natives** and **self-educators** (learners know what's best for them): myths per [Kirs2013].
  - The other [Guzd2015b] myths are listed in full above, including "some people are just born to program" and "student evaluations are the best way to evaluate teaching."
  - [Kirs2013] cautions that we may be in a downward spiral in which every attempt by researchers to rebut these myths confirms opponents' belief that learning science is pseudo-science.
- **Multi-tasking on demanding tasks:** people aren't good at it. Only routine automaticity is possible.

## Practice exercises
- **Learning Strategies** — note which of the six strategies you use and don't use; write three general concepts with two concrete examples each; work backward from one example per concept to explain how the concept explains it (elaboration). Individual / 20 min.
- **Connecting Ideas** — partners each pick an idea, then find a four-link chain connecting the two (e.g., Saskatchewan → Canada → country → government → elections → statistics). Pairs / 5 min.
- **Convergent Evolution** — create 2–4 guided-note cards for a lesson, swap them, and judge how clear the prompts are and how long they take to fill in. Pairs / 15 min.
- **Changing Minds** — choose something from the book that surprised you or contradicted your beliefs and practise explaining it convincingly to a partner in 1–2 minutes (framed by [Kirs2013] on myths). Pairs / 10 min.
- **Flash Cards** — make a dozen flash cards for a topic, swap them, time how long each of you takes to reach 100% recall, then re-test after an hour. Individual / 15 min.
- **Using ADEPT** — outline a short lesson introducing something you recently taught or learned, using the five ADEPT steps. Whole class / 15 min.
- **The Cost of Multi-Tasking** — in pairs, time counting 1–26, reciting A–Z, and interleaving them ("1, A, 2, B…"), then pool the results (the interleaved task is usually much slower). Pairs / 10 min.
- **Myths in Computing Education** — in groups of 3–4, vote true or false on six statements (women in CS like other STEM; need more female faculty; student evaluations are best; personalize for learning styles; teachers should model good software development; some people are naturally better programmers), then check against [Guzd2015b]. Small groups / 20 min.
- **Calibrated Peer Review** — write a 5–10 point code rubric, choose or write a small program with 3–4 violations, grade it, have a partner grade it, and compare what each accepted or criticized. Pairs / 20 min.
- **Top Ten Myths** — everyone votes +1, −1 or 0 on Guzdial's ten myths, reads his explanations, votes again, and discusses who changed their mind and why some still hold their beliefs. Whole class / 15 min.
