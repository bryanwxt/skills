# Marketing

> Source: Greg Wilson, *Teaching Tech Together* (2018), Ch. 14 "Marketing" (§14.1–§14.5). Licensed CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/); condensed and restructured for skill use, with quotations marked. Rule IDs use prefix `MKT`.

## When a skill needs this
- An outreach assistant works out who a teaching program's real "customers" are and writes stakeholder-specific elevator pitches (learners, sponsors, councillors, volunteers).
- A website or flyer reviewer checks whether a teaching group's public materials are findable (online and offline) and answer what visitors actually want first.
- A cold-email drafter writes or critiques first-contact emails to schools, companies, or community groups using the book's eight rules and template.
- A strategy advisor judges positioning: whether the group is first in a category, should create a new one, or should join someone else.
- A recruitment planner tells stories (props, short videos, founding myth) instead of leading with data.

## Key terms

|---|---|

| SEO | "Search engine optimization": folklore about making things findable in search (§14.2). |

| Conversational programmer | Someone who needs to know enough computing to talk meaningfully with programmers but won't program (glossary; [Wang2018]). |

## Concepts

### Chapter 14 introduction: what marketing is
- People with academic or technical backgrounds find it hard to take marketing seriously because they see it as "spin and misdirection". Wilson's reframing: marketing is "the craft of seeing things from other people's perspective, understanding their wants and needs, and finding ways to meet them."
- This parallels Chapter 6: the lesson-design techniques (personas, learner needs) do the same for lessons. Ch 14 applies them to getting people to understand and support what you're doing.

### §14.1 What Are You Offering to Whom?
- **First step:** work out what you offer to whom, i.e. what actually brings in the volunteers, funding, and other support you need to keep going. [Kuch3011]: the answer is often counter-intuitive.
- **Scientists' example:** most scientists think their papers are their product, but it's their grant proposals, because those bring in money. Papers are the advertising that persuades people to fund proposals, just as albums now persuade people to buy musicians' concert tickets and t-shirts.
- **Worked example: weekend programming workshops for people re-entering the workforce after years caring for young children.**
  - If learners pay enough to cover costs, the learners are the customers and the workshops are the product.
  - If workshops are free, or learners pay only a token amount "to cut the no-show rate", the real product may be some mix of: your **grant proposals**; your **alumni** whom sponsoring companies want to hire; the **half-page summary in the mayor's annual report** to city council showing her support for the local tech sector; and the **personal satisfaction** your volunteer instructors get from teaching.
- **Personas:** as in Chapter 6, create personas for people who might be interested and work out which of their needs your program meets.
- **Elevator pitches:** write a set, each aimed at a different stakeholder. The widely used template (§14.1):
  - For *target audience*
  - who *dissatisfaction with what's currently available*
  - our *category*
  - provide *key benefit*.
  - Unlike *alternatives*
  - our program *key distinguishing feature*.
- **Two pitches for the same workshop** (§14.1):
  - *For participants:* for people re-entering the workforce after raising children, who still have regular childcare duties, the workshops offer weekend classes **with on-site childcare**; unlike online classes, participants meet people at the same stage of life.
  - *For companies asked to donate staff time to teach:* for companies that want to recruit entry-level developers but struggle to find mature, diverse candidates, the workshops provide a pool of recruits in their thirties, including many people from underrepresented groups; unlike college recruiting fairs, the program connects companies directly with a diverse audience.
  - Lesson: same program, different audience, different dissatisfaction, benefit, and "unlike".
- **Ask stakeholders why they're interested.** If you don't know why a stakeholder might be interested, ask. If you do know, "ask them anyway": answers change over time, and asking uncovers things you missed.
- **Use pitches to drive the website and publicity**, so people can tell quickly whether you have something to talk about. But don't copy the pitches verbatim: many people in tech have seen the template so often "their eyes will glaze over."
- **People are not just economic animals.** A sense of accomplishment, control over their own lives, and being part of a community motivate as much as money. People may volunteer because their friends do. A company may say it sponsors classes for economically disadvantaged high-school students to grow its future hiring pool, while the CEO is actually doing it "simply because it's the right thing to do."

### §14.2 Branding and Positioning
- **Brand:** someone's first reaction to a mention of a product. If the reaction is "what's that?", you don't have a brand yet. It matters because people won't help with something they don't know or care about.
- **Positioning becomes more important as attention falls.** Most branding talk today is about online awareness (mailing lists, blogs, Twitter). As the volume of "(mis)information" grows, the attention people give each interruption shrinks, so positioning ("differentiation") matters more. It is the "unlike" section of your elevator pitches. When reaching people already familiar with your field, emphasize positioning; it's what catches their attention.
- **Props and short videos tell stories.** Use props, such as a robot car a student made from scraps found around the house, or a website another student built for his parents' retirement home: anything that makes the work seem real. Or make a short video, "no more than a few minutes long", showcasing students' backgrounds and accomplishments. The aim of both is a story: "while people always ask for data, stories are what they believe."
- **Equity caveat:** these examples assume access to money, materials, or technology to make such products. Many people don't have it, and groups serving economically disadvantaged learners almost certainly don't. Rosario Robinson: "Free works for those that can afford free." There, stories matter even more, because they can be shared and re-shared without limit.
- **Foundational Myths (box).** One of the most compelling stories is why and how you started. Are you teaching what you wish someone had taught you? Was there one person you wanted to help, who "opened the floodgates"? If your website has no section starting "Once upon a time", consider adding one.
- **Online findability.** Whatever else you do, make the organization findable in search. [DiSa2014b] found that the search terms parents were likely to use for out-of-school computing classes didn't find those classes. SEO is mostly "folklore". Given Google's near-monopoly and lack of transparency, most of it amounts to staying one step ahead of algorithms designed to stop people gaming rankings. Unless you're very well funded, the best you can do is:
  - search for yourself and your organization regularly and see what comes up;
  - read Moz's guidelines and do what you can to improve your site.
- **What visitors want first** (the book points to a cartoon on this): not your org chart or a virtual tour, but your **address, parking information**, and above all **what you teach, when, how to get in touch, and how it will change their life**.
- **Offline findability** matters just as much for new organizations. Many people you hope to reach aren't online as often as you, and some not at all. Notice boards in schools, libraries, drop-in centers, and grocery stores still work.
- **Build Alliances (box).** As in Ch 13, alliances pay off, including through **referrals**. If someone approaches you who would be better served by another organization, take a moment to introduce them. After doing this several times, add something to your website to help the next person. The organizations you help will soon start to help you in return.

### §14.3 The Art of the Cold Call
- Building a website and hoping people find it is one thing; calling or knocking on a door with no prior introduction is another. Like teaching, it is "a craft that can be learned like any other". The rules (headings quoted):
  1. **"Establish a point of connection"**, e.g. "I was speaking to X" or "You attended bootcamp Y". It **must be specific**: spammers and headhunters have trained everyone to ignore openings like "I recently read your website".
  2. **"Create a slight sense of urgency"**, e.g. "We're booking workshops right now." Use it cautiously: the web's race to the bottom has conditioned people to discount anything that sounds like a hustle.
  3. **"Explain how you are going to help make their lives better."** E.g. "Your students will be able to do their math homework much faster if you let us tutor them" gets attention.
  4. **"Be specific about what you are offering."** E.g. "Our usual two-day curriculum includes…" helps listeners decide right away whether the conversation is worth pursuing.
  5. **"Make yourself credible"**: mention your backers, size, how long you've been around, or your instructors' backgrounds.
  6. **"Tell them what your terms are."** Do you charge? Must they cover instructors' travel? Can they reserve seats for their own staff?
  7. **"Write a good subject line."** Keep it short; avoid ALL CAPS and words like "sale" or "free", which make spam filtering more likely; never use exclamation marks.
  8. **"Keep it short"**, because "the purest form of respect is to treat other people as if their time was as valuable as your own."
- **Results with the template:** about half of emails were answered, about half of those wanted to talk more, and about half of those led to workshops. So **10–15% of targeted emails became workshops**, much better than the **2–3%** response rate most organizations expect from cold calls, but still "pretty demoralizing if you're not used to it."
- **The "Mail Out of the Blue" template** (reproduced in Templates) shows each rule: a specific point of connection (a conversation at last week's tech showcase), slight urgency (scheduling the next batch over the next couple of weeks), the offer (a one-day instructor training class), benefit and specifics (key research-grounded practices; hands-on; short lessons alternating with individual and group exercises and practice teaching), credibility (delivered dozens of times on four continents), a low-pressure close, and brevity.

### §14.4 A Final Thought
- [Kuch3011]: if you can't be first in a category, create a new category you can be first in. If you can't do that, join an existing group or think about doing something else entirely.
- "This isn't defeatist": if someone else already does what you do, and does it better, there are probably many other equally useful things you could do instead.

## Rules
- **MKT-1** — Treat marketing as seeing things from other people's perspective and meeting their needs, not spin. *Why:* it is the same skill as learner-centered lesson design (Ch 6). *Check:* does each outreach artifact start from the audience's wants rather than the organization's self-description? (§14 intro)
- **MKT-2** — Identify what actually brings in your support (money, volunteers, backing) and whose needs it meets; don't assume the workshop is the product. *Why:* the real product is often counter-intuitive (grant proposals, hireable alumni, a line in the mayor's report, volunteer satisfaction) [Kuch3011]. *Check:* list each source of support and, for each, the "product" it is buying. (§14.1)
- **MKT-3** — Build personas for each stakeholder group and write down which of their needs the program meets. *Why:* same method as lesson design (Ch 6). *Check:* is there one persona per stakeholder type (learner, sponsor, volunteer, official)? (§14.1)
- **MKT-4** — Write a separate elevator pitch for each stakeholder using the For / who / our / provide / Unlike / our-program template. *Why:* different stakeholders have different dissatisfactions, benefits, and alternatives. *Check:* does each pitch fill all six slots, and do the pitches differ in benefit and "unlike"? (§14.1)
- **MKT-5** — Ask stakeholders why they're interested even when you think you know, and ask again over time. *Why:* answers change, and asking reveals what you missed. *Check:* when were stakeholders last asked? (§14.1)
- **MKT-6** — Let the pitches drive website and publicity content, but don't paste the template verbatim. *Why:* helps people quickly see whether you have something to talk about; the raw template makes tech readers' "eyes glaze over". *Check:* does the site convey each pitch's content without the template's wording? (§14.1)
- **MKT-7** — Appeal to non-economic motives too: accomplishment, control over one's life, community, friends, doing the right thing. *Why:* "people are not just economic animals"; a sponsor's stated business reason may not be the real one. *Check:* does each pitch name a non-monetary benefit? (§14.1)
- **MKT-8** — When contacting people already familiar with the field, lead with positioning (your "unlike"). *Why:* attention per message is shrinking; differentiation is what catches informed people's attention. *Check:* does the opening paragraph say how you differ? (§14.2)
- **MKT-9** — Tell stories rather than leading with data: props made by learners, a video of a few minutes at most about learners' backgrounds and accomplishments, and a founding story ("Once upon a time"). *Why:* "while people always ask for data, stories are what they believe." *Check:* does public material include at least one concrete learner story and an origin story? (§14.2)
- **MKT-10** — When serving people without money, materials, or technology, rely on shareable stories rather than artifacts that need resources. *Why:* "Free works for those that can afford free"; stories can be shared without limit. *Check:* does the plan assume learners can afford to make showcase products? (§14.2)
- **MKT-11** — Make the organization findable: search for yourself regularly, use the terms your audience would actually search for, follow Moz's guidelines, and don't chase SEO folklore. *Why:* [DiSa2014b] found parents' likely search terms didn't find out-of-school computing classes. *Check:* search with an audience member's words and see whether you appear. (§14.2)
- **MKT-12** — Put what visitors want first on the website: address, parking, what you teach, when, how to get in touch, and how it will change their lives; not the org chart or a virtual tour. *Check:* can a visitor find all six from the landing page? (§14.2)
- **MKT-13** — Advertise offline too: notice boards in schools, libraries, drop-in centers, grocery stores. *Why:* many people you want to reach are online less often than you, or not at all. *Check:* is there an offline channel in the outreach plan? (§14.2)
- **MKT-14** — Refer people better served elsewhere to allied organizations, and after doing this several times, add referral information to the website. *Why:* builds alliances; helped organizations soon return the favor. *Check:* does the site have a "other groups that can help" section? (§14.2; see COM-19)
- **MKT-15** — Open a cold contact with a specific point of connection (a named person, an event, a program they attended). *Why:* generic openers like "I recently read your website" are ignored as spam or headhunting. *Check:* does the first sentence name a specific shared connection? (§14.3)
- **MKT-16** — Add only a slight, genuine sense of urgency. *Why:* it prompts action, but anything that sounds like a hustle is discounted. *Check:* is the urgency a real scheduling fact, without pressure words? (§14.3)
- **MKT-17** — State how you'll make the recipient's life better, specifically what you offer, why you're credible (backers, size, longevity, instructors' backgrounds), and your terms (cost, travel costs, reserved seats). *Why:* lets them decide immediately whether to continue. *Check:* can a reader answer "what, for me, from whom, on what terms" from the message? (§14.3)
- **MKT-18** — Write short subject lines without ALL CAPS, "sale", "free", or exclamation marks. *Why:* those features raise the odds of being treated as spam. *Check:* scan the subject for the banned features and length. (§14.3)
- **MKT-19** — Keep cold messages short. *Why:* "the purest form of respect is to treat other people as if their time was as valuable as your own." *Check:* is the email about three short paragraphs, like the book's template? (§14.3)
- **MKT-20** — Set realistic expectations for cold outreach: even a good template turned about 10–15% of targeted emails into workshops (versus the typical 2–3% response). *Why:* the rejection rate is demoralizing if unexpected. *Check:* does the outreach plan size its target list for roughly one success per seven to ten emails? (§14.3)
- **MKT-21** — Be first in a category; if you can't, create a new category you can lead; if you can't do that, join an existing group or do something else. *Why:* if someone already does it better, your effort is more useful elsewhere [Kuch3011]. *Check:* name the category you're first in, or the group you should join. (§14.4; see COM-6)

## Procedures

### P1. Work out what you're offering to whom (§14.1)
1. List every source of support: learner fees, grants, sponsors, donated staff time, volunteers, officials.
2. Decision point: do learner fees cover costs? If yes, learners are customers and workshops are the product. If no (free or token fee), identify the product each other supporter is really buying: grant proposals, alumni for hiring, publicity for an official, volunteer satisfaction.
3. Write a persona for each stakeholder: who they are, what they're dissatisfied with, what they want, including non-economic motives.
4. If you don't know a stakeholder's reasons, ask them. If you think you do, ask anyway, and repeat periodically.

### P2. Write stakeholder elevator pitches (§14.1)
1. For each persona, fill the six slots: target audience; dissatisfaction with what's available; category; key benefit; alternatives; key distinguishing feature.
2. Check the pitches differ in benefit and "unlike" (e.g. on-site childcare and meeting peers for participants vs. a mature, diverse recruiting pool for companies).
3. Add a non-economic motive where relevant.
4. Turn the pitches into website and flyer copy in natural prose; don't publish the template as is.

### P3. Build brand and findability (§14.2)
1. Test the brand: ask people what comes to mind at your name; "what's that?" means you have work to do.
2. Write positioning (the "unlike") for audiences already in the field.
3. Collect stories: learner-made props, a short video (a few minutes at most), a "Once upon a time" founding story.
4. Decision point: if learners lack resources, prioritize stories that can be retold and re-shared over artifacts.
5. Search for yourself regularly using your audience's words, and fix what you can using Moz's guidelines.
6. Restructure the landing page: address, parking, what, when, contact, life change.
7. Post on offline notice boards (schools, libraries, drop-in centers, grocery stores).
8. Refer misdirected inquiries to allies; publish a referral list once patterns emerge.

### P4. Write a cold email (§14.3)
1. **Subject:** short, no ALL CAPS, no "sale"/"free", no exclamation marks.
2. **Opening:** a specific point of connection ("following up on our conversation at <event>", "I was speaking to <person>").
3. **Urgency:** one light, true scheduling note ("we're scheduling the next batch over the next couple of weeks").
4. **Benefit:** how it makes their (or their learners') lives better.
5. **Specifics:** what exactly you offer (length, format, content).
6. **Credibility:** backers, size, longevity, delivery record, instructors' backgrounds.
7. **Terms:** cost, travel costs, seat reservations.
8. **Close:** a low-pressure invitation to talk.
9. **Trim** to the shortest version that still covers 2–8.
10. Send in a batch sized for roughly 10–15% conversion, and track replies, conversations, and bookings separately (each stage was about half the previous one).

### P5. Decide whether to compete (§14.4)
1. Name your category and the others in it.
2. Decision point: are you first? If yes, proceed. If no, can you define a new category you'd be first in? If yes, reposition. If no, join an existing group or choose another useful thing to do.

## Diagnostics
| Symptom (in a lesson, class, or community) | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Free workshops thrive but the organization runs out of money | Mistaking the workshop for the product; neglecting what funders buy | MKT-2 |
| One generic pitch is used for learners, sponsors, and councillors | No per-stakeholder personas or pitches | MKT-3, MKT-4 |
| Website reads "For X who Y, our Z provides…" word for word | Template copied verbatim | MKT-6 |
| Pitch to a sponsor is all ROI; the sponsor's real motive was civic | Ignoring non-economic motives | MKT-5, MKT-7 |
| People in the field ignore announcements | No positioning; attention exhausted | MKT-8 |
| Reports full of statistics fail to move funders | Data without stories | MKT-9 |
| Showcase relies on learners building costly projects in a low-income program | Ignoring "free works for those that can afford free" | MKT-10 |
| Parents can't find the class via search | Site not found by audience search terms | MKT-11 |
| Landing page leads with mission, team, and org chart; no address or schedule | Not answering visitors' first questions | MKT-12 |
| Target community largely offline; only online ads used | No offline findability | MKT-13 |
| Inquiries the group can't serve are turned away cold | Missed referral and alliance opportunity | MKT-14 |
| Cold emails start "I recently came across your website" and get no reply | No specific point of connection | MKT-15 |
| "LIMITED SEATS!!! FREE WORKSHOP" emails land in spam | Bad subject line; hustle tone | MKT-16, MKT-18 |
| Recipients reply "what exactly is this and what does it cost?" | Missing specifics, credibility, or terms | MKT-17 |
| Long cold emails | Not respecting the recipient's time | MKT-19 |

## Templates and checklists

### Elevator pitch template (§14.1, "widely-used")
```
For <target audience>
who <dissatisfaction with what's currently available>,
our <category>
provide <key benefit>.
Unlike <alternatives>,
our program <key distinguishing feature>.
```
Use one per stakeholder; rewrite into natural prose before publishing.

### Book's example pitches (§14.1, condensed)
| Stakeholder | Dissatisfaction | Benefit | Unlike | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parents re-entering the workforce, still doing childcare | Can't attend classes around childcare | Weekend intro programming classes with on-site childcare | Online classes | Meet people at the same stage of life |
| Companies recruiting entry-level developers | Can't find mature, diverse candidates | Pool of recruits in their thirties, many from underrepresented groups | College recruiting fairs | Direct connection with a diverse audience |

### Stakeholder "product" worksheet (§14.1)
```
Supporter: <learners / funder / sponsor / official / volunteers>
What they give: <money / staff time / space / publicity / time>
What they're really getting (the "product"): <workshop / grant outcomes / hireable alumni / report line / satisfaction>
Their stated motive:            Possible non-economic motive:
Last asked them why: <date>
```

### Website first-screen checklist (§14.2)
```
[ ] Address
[ ] Parking information
[ ] What you teach
[ ] When you teach it
[ ] How to get in touch
[ ] How it will change the visitor's life
[ ] "Once upon a time" founding story (somewhere on the site)
[ ] Referral links to allied groups
[ ] (Not leading with) org chart / virtual tour
```

### Findability routine (§14.2)
```
Regularly: search for your organization using the words your audience would use.
Read Moz's guidelines; fix what you can. Ignore SEO folklore.
Offline: notice boards at schools, libraries, drop-in centers, grocery stores.
```

### Cold-call / cold-email checklist (§14.3)
```
[ ] Specific point of connection ("I was speaking to X" / "You attended bootcamp Y")
[ ] Slight, honest urgency (no hustle)
[ ] How it makes their life better
[ ] Specific offer ("Our usual two-day curriculum includes…")
[ ] Credibility (backers, size, longevity, instructors' backgrounds)
[ ] Terms (cost? travel costs? reserved seats for their staff?)
[ ] Subject line: short, no ALL CAPS, no "sale"/"free", no exclamation marks
[ ] Short overall
```

### "Mail Out of the Blue" (§14.3, verbatim template from the book)
```
Hi NAME,

I hope you don't mind mail out of the blue, but I wanted to follow up on our conversation
at the tech showcase last week to see if you would be interested having us run an instructor
training workshop - we're scheduling the next batch over the next couple of weeks.

This one-day class will introduce your volunteer teachers to a handful of key practices that
are grounded in education research and proven useful in practice. The class has been delivered
dozens of times on four continents, and will be hands-on: short lessons will alternate with
individual and group practical exercises, including practice teaching sessions.

If this sounds interesting, please give me a shout - I'd welcome a chance to talk ways and means.

Thanks,
NAME
```
> **Skill note:** the book's template states no terms (cost, travel, seats), though rule 6 of §14.3 asks for them; a drafting skill should add a terms line when relevant.

### Cold outreach funnel (§14.3)
```
Targeted emails sent        100
Answered            ~1/2 →   50
Want to talk more   ~1/2 →   25
Lead to workshops   ~1/2 →  ~12   (book: 10–15%; typical cold-call response: 2–3%)
```

## Examples
- **Scientists' papers vs. grant proposals** [Kuch3011]: papers are advertising; proposals are the product, like albums selling concert tickets and t-shirts (§14.1).
- **Weekend workshops for parents re-entering the workforce:** the product depends on who pays; two contrasting pitches (participants, companies) (§14.1).
- **Token fees** are charged "to cut the no-show rate", which doesn't make learners the customers (§14.1).
- **The mayor's report:** a half-page summary showing the mayor supports the local tech sector can be part of the product (§14.1).
- **CEO motive:** the stated reason is a future hiring pool; the real reason may be "simply because it's the right thing to do" (§14.1).
- **Props:** a robot car made from household scraps; a website a student built for his parents' retirement home (§14.2).
- **Rosario Robinson:** "Free works for those that can afford free" (§14.2).
- **[DiSa2014b] "They can't find us":** parents' likely search terms didn't find out-of-school CS classes (§14.2).
- **Instructor-training cold email:** the book's template converted 10–15% of targeted emails to workshops (§14.3).

## Evidence and caveats
- **[Kuch3011]** (Kuchner, *Marketing for Scientists*, 2011; the key's "3011" is a typo in the book) is a practitioner guide, the source of the "what's your product" and "be first in a category" ideas.
- **[DiSa2014b]** is the only empirical study in the chapter: parents' search terms didn't surface out-of-school computing classes.
- **[Wang2018]** (exercise): most learning resources don't meet conversational programmers' needs.
- **Cold-email conversion (10–15%)** is Wilson's own outreach data ("we found"), reported as roughly halving at each stage; the 2–3% baseline is stated as what "most organizations expect", without a citation.
- **SEO:** Wilson calls most advice "folklore" and doesn't endorse specific techniques beyond self-searching and Moz's guidelines.
- **"Stories are what they believe"** is stated as a maxim, not backed by a cited study.
- **The elevator pitch template** is "widely-used" and overexposed in tech: use its structure, not its wording.
- **Urgency** comes with an explicit caution: people discount anything that sounds like a hustle.

## Practice exercises
- **Write an Elevator Pitch for a City Councillor** — individual, 10 min: pitch the weekend-workshop organization to a city councillor whose support it needs (§14.5).
- **Write Elevator Pitches for Your Organization** — individual, 30 min: identify two groups whose support you need and write a pitch for each (§14.5).
- **Email Subjects** — pairs, 10 min: write only subject lines for three emails (new course, new sponsor, change in project leadership); compare with a partner, merge the best features, and shorten (§14.5).
- **Identify Causes of Passive Resistance** — small groups, 30 min: list three or four reasons people might not want your teaching initiative (including passive resistance such as never getting round to it, or raising one problem after another to make change look riskier and costlier than it is) and what you can do about each with your time and resources (§14.5).
- **Why Learn to Program?** — individual, 15 min: revisit §1.7's exercise; where do your reasons for teaching and learners' reasons for learning align or diverge, and how does that affect your marketing? (§14.5)
- **Appealing to Your Learners** — think-pair-share, 15 min: write a one-paragraph pitch for a web design class aimed at adults (better at managing time, learning because they want or need to, bringing past learning experience, better at telling productive struggle from plain struggle); compare across pairs (§14.5).
- **Conversational Programmers** — think-pair-share, 15 min: write a pitch for a half-day workshop for conversational programmers, whose needs most resources don't meet [Wang2018]; share with the class (§14.5).
