## When a skill needs this
- Drafting the opening of a memo, report, proposal, article, or deck so it leads the reader to the single question the document answers, then gives the answer.
- Reviewing a draft that opens with a statement of purpose plus a list of topics, has a "Background" section, or packs data and exhibits into its opening.
- Deciding how long an introduction should be, and which order of Situation/Complication/Question/Answer produces the tone wanted.
- Writing the short lead-in stories at the top of each major section (each Key Line point) of a longer document.
- Recovering the governing question when the writer can't articulate it, by reasoning backward from the body.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Opening that uses a story to recall the reader's question, then answers it and (in long documents) shows the plan. |
| Story form | The narrative arc of beginning, middle, and end, mapped to Situation, Complication, and resolution (Answer). |
| Situation (S) | Opening statements about the subject, tied to a time and place, that the reader knows or will accept. |
| Complication (C) | The story's next development. It creates tension and prompts the Question, but need not be a problem. |
| Question (Q) | The one question the reader has, or would have, after hearing S and C. Frequently left unstated. |
| Answer (A) / resolution / main point | The writer's principal point, which is the apex of the pyramid. |
| Key Line | The row of points directly under the main point. It answers the question the main point provokes and doubles as the document's plan. |
| Self-sufficient statement | An opening statement whose precise meaning needs no earlier sentence. |
| Noncontroversial statement | A statement the reader grasps and accepts at once. |
| Reminding vs planting the question | Reminding: the reader already holds the question (e.g. a memo to a known colleague). Planting: arranging accepted knowledge so the question forms (broad-audience pieces, directives). |
| "Standing in the same place" | Writer and reader share a starting point and a question before any reasoning begins. This is Minto's test of an adequate introduction. |
| Standard / Direct / Concerned / Aggressive | Four orders for the introduction's parts (S-C-A, A-S-C, C-S-A, Q-S-C-A). Each shifts the tone. |
| Setting out the Key Line points | Displaying the Key Line points as an indented list right after the main point in a long document. |
| Mini-introduction | A brief S-C-Q story at the top of each Key Line section. Appendix C's term; Ch. 4 phrases it as introducing each Key Line point. |
| Vantage point | What the reader has just absorbed at a given moment in the document. It determines what each section's introduction must supply. |

  4. Every **Key Line grouping** receives its own short introduction.

### Why a Story? (pp. 35–36) — *p. 35 portion reconstructed: pp. 34–35 missing from source scan*
- **Where the argument starts.** The index cites "Story form — why used, 35", so the argument begins on the missing page.
- **The surviving fragment.** p. 36 opens mid-sentence, finishing a claim that the most dependable ("…proof") device of this kind is the **pull of an unfinished story**.
  - *Inference from the surviving text only:* the lost p. 35 discussion concerned capturing a reader whose mind is elsewhere. p. 36 stresses that the story seizes attention whatever the reader had been thinking about.
- **Illustration (p. 36).** A joke-opening about two Irishmen meeting at midnight on a bridge in an unfamiliar city. That one line pulls the reader's mind off other concerns, pins it to a moment and a place, and keeps it there, steered toward what the two men said or did, until the punch line lets go.
- **Application.** An introduction should exploit the reader's existing interest in the subject the same way: tell a story about that subject.
  - The story's beginning, middle, and end are Situation, Complication, and resolution.
  - The resolution is always the main point, because every document exists to settle a problem or to answer a question the reader already carries.
- **It must be a "good" story for this reader.** Minto's analogy is that children's favourite stories are ones they've heard before. So the writer should tell a story the reader knows, or would know if reasonably well informed.
- **Psychological rationale.** A familiar story lets the writer present points the reader accepts *before* points the reader might contest. Easy, agreeable reading at the start leaves the reader more receptive than slogging through a thicket of detail would.

### Where Do You Start the Situation? (pp. 36–37)
- **Open with a statement about the subject that the reader will agree with**, either because they know it is true or because they will accept it as true.
- **Self-check.** If no such opening suggests itself, either the subject is wrong or the starting point in the story is wrong.
- **Known reader (letter, memo).** The start point is usually easy to find: the earliest point where a statement about the subject can be both:
  - *self-sufficient*: no earlier sentence is needed to fix its meaning; and
  - *noncontroversial*: the reader takes it in and agrees without effort.
- **Broad readership (a report circulated widely, a magazine piece, a book).** The job is no longer to remind the reader of a question; it is to *plant* one. That is harder.
  - Assume moderately well-informed readers and lay out what is already generally accepted about the subject.
  - Minto's rule of thumb is the business-press test: material of the sort *Business Week* or *Fortune* might print will be taken as true.
  - Arranged as a narrative, often from an angle the reader hadn't considered, such material moves readers to ask the question the writer means to answer.
- **Anchor in a specific time and place.** Every opening Situation sentence does this, and that anchoring lays the base for the story. Minto offers one sample opening per genre (p. 37):
  - Memo: a named firm is weighing exports from one of its plants to a named plant abroad.
  - Report: health services everywhere face mounting pressure on scarce resources, and a named national service is among them.
  - Magazine article: across most of the archaeological record, the only human artifacts were purely practical (stone tools).
  - Book: managers, like everyone, are shaped by their own culture.
- **The intended reaction is a nod followed by "so what?"** (politely: "why tell me this?"). That reaction is the cue for the Complication.

### What's a Complication? (pp. 37–39)
- **It is not "complication" in the sense of a problem,** though it often is one. It is the complication *of the story*, and its function is to generate the tension that sets off the Question.
- **It continues from the Situation.** Taking the truth already established, the Complication reports the next development, and that development must lead unavoidably to a question.
- **Exhibit 10 (p. 37): the four usual question types.** The "next development" is normally a variant of one of these:

| Situation (established truth) | Complication (next development) | Question it provokes |
|---|---|---|
| A task needs doing | Something prevents doing it | What should we do? |
| A problem exists | Its solution is known | How do we put the solution into effect? |
| A problem exists | Someone has put forward a solution | Is that solution right? |
| An action was taken | It failed | Why? |

- **Exhibit 11 (pp. 38–39)** gives one published introduction per type, all *Harvard Business Review* articles collected in Henry Strage's anthology *Milestones in Management*. It shows how many different styles can flesh out the same bare S-C-Q skeleton. See Worked examples.

### Why that Order? (p. 40)
- **The content is fixed; the order is not.** Situation, complication, and solution must all be there, but their sequence can change to set a tone, and each sequence shifts the tone a little.
- **Minto's demonstration.** She takes one skeleton and writes it four ways:
  - S = diversification work has risen 40% over five years.
  - C = no significant client benefit from any of it can be shown.
  - Q (implied) = how do we make sure such studies really benefit clients?
  - A = launch a Firm Development Project to study the problem.
- **The four versions:**
  - **Standard** (situation → complication → solution): the measured, factual default.
  - **Direct** (solution → situation → complication): leads with the recommendation.
  - **Concerned** (complication → situation → solution): leads with the troubling fact, so it reads as worried and conscience-driven.
  - **Aggressive** (question → situation → complication, then solution): leads with the challenge, stresses risk and urgency, and calls for immediate action.
- Worked examples gives the structure of each version.
- **Think from the Situation regardless.** Ch. 3 (p. 31, `02-building-the-pyramid.md`) adds that whatever order is used on the page, the thinking should start from the Situation, since that sequence best surfaces the right Complication and Question.

### What About the Key Line? (pp. 41–42)
- **Two jobs.** The Key Line both answers the fresh question the main point raises and reveals **the document's plan**.
- **Long document.** Display the Key Line points indented, mid-page, immediately after the main point (Exhibit 12). Then put a heading for the first point and begin (see `10-pyramid-on-the-page.md`).
  - *Rationale:* in about the first half-minute the reader has the writer's entire line of thought. Everything later only explains or defends it, so the reader can choose to keep reading or accept the conclusions as given.
  - The reader also knows what is coming and can read without fearing surprises. Minto frames this as courtesy.
- **Short document (a paragraph or two per point).** Listing the points and then repeating them as headings would be redundant. Instead, make each point the topic sentence of its paragraph and underline it so it stands out.
- **The points must be ideas, not subjects.**
  - *Minto's counter-example (p. 42):* an introduction announcing that a memo describes a project-team approach and has six sections: background; the approach's principles; the nature of project work; the program's organisation; distinctive benefits and results; conditions for success.
  - A list like this transmits nothing. It pushes on the reader a string of labels they can't place in any perspective, costing time and delaying understanding.
- **Never title a section "Background" or "Introduction".** Its contents won't sit at the same abstraction level as the sibling sections.
- **Listing subjects hides weak logic.** The ideas behind the labels probably don't add up to a clear inductive or deductive argument.
  - In the six-section example the logic looks scrambled: the benefits material probably belongs under principles, and the conditions for success under organisation.
  - Minto's maxim: write about ideas, never about categories.

### How Long a Story? (pp. 42–45)
- **The test.** Minto answers "how long?" with the old quip about leg length: just long enough to reach the floor. An introduction needs only enough length for reader and writer to be **"standing in the same place"** before the reasoning starts.
- **Typical length:** two or three paragraphs, arranged as in Exhibit 12.
  - Situation and Complication may *each* reach three or four paragraphs, **but no more**. Reminding someone of what they already know can't take longer than that.
- **Exhibits in an introduction are a red flag.** They show the writer belabouring the obvious.
- **Minimum length:** a single sentence can do, for instance a reply that begins by citing the date and query of the reader's letter.
  - The closer the writer's day-to-day relationship with the reader, the shorter the introduction can be.
  - Even so, it must always say enough to bring the reader's Question back to mind.
- **Length tracks the reader, not the document.** The governing question is what this reader must be told (a) to appreciate fully why the main point matters and (b) to *want* to continue and see how the writer reached it.
- **Introductions are hard.** More things go wrong in introductions than anywhere else in a document. The remedy is to read many examples until it's clear when one sounds right, and to keep reworking one's own until it does.
- **Length samples (pp. 43–45).** Eight real openings of widely varying length and genre: a letter, a newspaper editorial, a magazine article, an internal memo, a consulting report, an essay (Keynes), a book (Gibbon), and the founding introduction of an early-18th-century periodical (reproduced via Trevelyan's social history). Summarised in Worked examples.

### Do I Need to Introduce the Key Line Points? (pp. 45–49)
- **Yes.** Each Key Line point gets its own introduction, built by roughly the same S-C-Q method as the opening one but **far more briefly**. The aim again is a short story that brings the reader to the writer's position just as the point's question arises.
- **Exhibit 13 (p. 46).** A paper called "Management Tools for the Nineties" (full skeleton under Worked examples).
  - *Generic opening:* S = tool X is believed to deliver result Y; C = we use X, yet others are the ones achieving Y; Q = what explains their success?; A = they combine X with A and B.
  - That answer prompts a new question (how do these combine to produce Y?), which the Key Line answers.
  - Each Key Line point then invites "how does that work?". The plural noun describing the points is "steps".
- **Don't jump straight from point to support.** For each point:
  1. Mark its place with a **heading that conveys what the point actually says**, not a bare topic label. The book contrasts "BENCHMARKING" with "BENCHMARKING PROCESS EFFICIENCY".
  2. **Build up to the point.** Recap what the reader already knows about that sub-subject and show how a question arises that this point answers.
- **What each section's introduction covers depends on where the reader stands (p. 48):**
  - *Opening introduction:* recall what the reader knows about the document's overall subject.
  - *First Key Line point:* show why this sub-subject bears on the overall main point.
  - *Each later Key Line point:* show how the coming sub-subject connects with the one just covered.
  - The general discipline: stay aware of what has just been placed in the reader's mind. Given that vantage point, work out what more they must hear before the next question forms.
- **Theory behind good introductions (p. 48), restated:**
  1. *Remind, don't inform.* Leave out anything the reader would need proved before accepting the points; hence no exhibits.
  2. *Always include the story elements:* Situation, Complication, Solution, plus, in longer documents, a preview of what is coming. The first three may depart from classic narrative order, but all must be present and blended into a story.
  3. *Length follows the reader's needs and the subject's demands.* There is room for whatever full understanding requires: the problem's history or background, the writer's role in it, earlier studies by anyone and their conclusions, definitions, admissions. All of it should be **blended into the story**, not set apart.
- **One Question per document (pp. 48–49).**
  - The opening Question is the hinge of the whole document, and a document has only one.
  - Two apparent questions must be linked so that one absorbs the other. Minto's example is a two-part "whether, and if so how" question about entering a market, which reduces to the single question of how to enter. A "no" to the first part would make the second part moot. A "yes" becomes the top point and raises "how?", which the Key Line answers.
- **If the Question won't emerge from the introduction (p. 49), reason backward from the body.**
  - A writer wants to make a set of points because the reader ought to know them.
  - The reader ought to know them only because they answer some question.
  - That question exists only because of the reader's situation.
  - Following this chain in reverse lets the writer construct a credible introduction that explains where the question came from.

### Closing: what a good introduction accomplishes (p. 59)
- **It shapes perception.** A good introduction doesn't merely capture and keep interest; it shapes how the reader sees the situation.
- **It makes the writer's reading feel plausible.** The narrative lends credibility to the writer's interpretation, even though any account of a situation is unavoidably a partial selection of the facts.
  - That felt plausibility narrows the reader's freedom to see things another way.
  - Minto's comparison is a trial lawyer's opening statement, which hands the jury a frame for the evidence to come.
- **It makes the conclusion feel inevitable**, so the reader is less disposed to quarrel with the reasoning that follows.
- **It conveys consideration.** It shows a writer who wants the reader to understand the situation clearly and to see through the story to the reality it stands for.

## Rules
- **INT-1** — Make every introduction contain a Situation, a Complication, a Question (stated or implied), and an Answer, joined into one story. *Why:* this is the essential form; drop a part and the reader can't arrive at the document's question. *Check:* tag each sentence S, C, Q, or A. All four must appear (Q may be implicit), and no sentence should be left untagged. (pp. 36, 40, 48; App. C p. 236)
- **INT-2** — Put in the introduction only what the reader already knows or will accept as true. Nothing that needs proof, and no exhibits. *Why:* introductions remind rather than inform, and exhibits there signal overstating the obvious. *Check:* for each statement, ask whether this reader would need evidence to accept it; if yes, move it to the body. Any chart or table in the introduction fails. (pp. 42, 48; cf. Ch. 3 p. 32)
- **INT-3** — Tell a story the reader already knows, so that agreeable points come before contestable ones. *Why:* easy agreement up front increases receptiveness, and a familiar story is the "good" kind for that reader. *Check:* the first claim the reader could dispute appears no earlier than the Answer. (p. 36)
- **INT-4** — Open the Situation with a statement *about the subject* that the reader will endorse. *Why:* it establishes the shared starting point. If no such opening is possible, the subject or the starting point is wrong. *Check:* does sentence one concern the document's subject, and would the reader nod at it? (p. 36)
- **INT-5** — Begin at the earliest point where a self-sufficient, noncontroversial statement can be made. *Why:* the opening must neither lean on unstated context nor invite argument. *Check:* read the first sentence alone. It should need no prior sentence and draw no objection. (p. 36)
- **INT-6** — For broad-audience documents (widely circulated reports, articles, books), plant the question by narrating generally accepted knowledge, assuming moderately well-informed readers. *Why:* these readers don't yet hold the question, so the story has to raise it. *Check:* apply Minto's business-press test to each Situation fact: is it the sort of thing *Business Week* or *Fortune* might print? (pp. 36–37)
- **INT-7** — Tie the opening Situation to a specific time and place. *Why:* this grounds the story and fixes attention. *Check:* the opening names a concrete organisation, place, period, or event rather than a floating generality. (p. 37)
- **INT-8** — Write the Complication as the next development in the story, growing from the established Situation and leading unavoidably to one Question. It need not be a problem. *Why:* the Complication's role is to create the tension that sets off the Question. *Check:* after S and C, could the reader reasonably ask anything other than the intended Q? Does C fit one of Exhibit 10's development types (task blocked; solution known; solution proposed; action failed)? (p. 37)
- **INT-9** — Make the Answer (the story's resolution) the main point, the apex of the pyramid. *Why:* documents always exist to settle a problem or answer a question the reader already has. *Check:* the A sentence matches the pyramid's top box and directly answers Q. (p. 36)
- **INT-10** — Reorder S, C, Q, A only deliberately, to set a tone (standard, direct, concerned, aggressive), and never omit an element. *Why:* order changes tone, but the content is essential. *Check:* name the order used and the tone intended, and confirm every element is still present. (pp. 40, 48)
- **INT-11** — In a long document, close the introduction with the plan: the Key Line points listed (indented) right after the main point, followed by the first heading. *Why:* the reader grasps the whole argument in about 30 seconds and can choose whether to read further. *Check:* in a multi-section document, are all Key Line points visible before the first section heading? (pp. 41, 48)
- **INT-12** — In a short document (a paragraph or two per point), skip the listed points and headings. Make each point the underlined topic sentence of its paragraph. *Why:* at that length, list plus headings is redundant. *Check:* a short memo should not list its points and then reuse the same wording as headings. (p. 41)
- **INT-13** — Express Key Line points (both in the list and in headings) as ideas, meaning full assertions, not subject labels. *Why:* a list of subjects carries no message, wastes the reader's time, and conceals whether the ideas form a coherent argument. *Check:* each item is a sentence that asserts something someone could disagree with. Bare noun phrases ("Background", "Principles", "Benefits") fail. (p. 42)
- **INT-14** — Never include a section headed "Background" or "Introduction". *Why:* its material sits at a different level of abstraction from its siblings, and background belongs inside the introduction's story. *Check:* scan headings for "Background", "Introduction", "History", or the like. (p. 42)
- **INT-15** — Make the introduction long enough for writer and reader to stand in the same place, and no longer. *Why:* it only has to recall what the reader knows. *Check:*
  - Normally two or three paragraphs.
  - Situation and Complication each capped at three or four paragraphs (count them).
  - As short as one sentence for a reader the writer deals with closely.
  - Always enough to recall the reader's Question: confirm the Q can be inferred from the text.
  (pp. 42–43)
- **INT-16** — Set introduction length by the reader's needs and the subject's demands, not by the document's length. *Why:* what counts is what this reader must hear to see why the main point matters and to want to read on. *Check:* a long document to a close colleague may have a very brief introduction; a short one to a stranger may need more. (pp. 43, 48)
- **INT-17** — Blend necessary background (problem history, the writer's involvement, earlier studies and their findings, definitions, admissions) into the story rather than presenting it as a separate block. *Why:* the introduction stays narrative and the body stays ideas only. *Check:* background facts sit inside the S/C narrative, not in a standalone block or list. (p. 48)
- **INT-18** — Open every Key Line section with a brief S-C-Q story, under a heading that conveys the substance of the point (not a one-word topic), before stating and supporting the point. *Why:* the reader again needs to reach the writer's vantage point just as the point's question arises. *Check:* each major section begins with a few sentences of known context and a triggering development, and its heading is more specific than a single topic word. (pp. 45–47; App. C p. 236)
- **INT-19** — Fit each section introduction to the reader's current vantage point. *Why:* what the reader needs depends on what has just been put into their head. *Check:*
  - Opening introduction: what the reader knows about the overall subject.
  - First Key Line point: why its sub-subject bears on the main point.
  - Later points: how each sub-subject connects to the previous one, so second and later section introductions should refer back to the preceding section.
  (p. 48)
- **INT-20** — Allow exactly one opening Question per document. If two appear, fold them so one contains the other (e.g. "whether and how" becomes "how", with "yes" as the top point). *Why:* the Question is the hinge of the whole document. *Check:* write Q as a single sentence. If it contains "and", test whether the second half only arises under one answer to the first. (pp. 48–49)
- **INT-21** — When the Question won't surface from the introduction, reason backward from the body. Ask what question the points answer, then what situation would have prompted it. *Why:* points are worth making only because they answer a question that grows out of the reader's situation. *Check:* the derived Q is answered by the top point, and the derived S/C credibly explain where it came from. (p. 49)
- **INT-22** — Write the Question down privately before drafting, even when the text will leave it unstated. *Why:* without that step a writer risks being unsure which question the document answers. *Check:* the writer can state Q in one sentence when asked. (p. 50)
- **INT-23** — Spend disproportionate effort on the introduction and rework it until it sounds right. *Why:* it is where most failures occur, and it frames how everything after it is perceived. *Check:* reread the introduction on its own. Does it lead smoothly and inevitably to the Q and the main point? (pp. 43, 59)

## Procedures

### A. Draft the opening introduction
1. **Identify the reader and whether they already hold the question.**
   - Known, named reader (letter, memo) → the introduction *reminds*.
   - Broad readership (widely circulated report, article, book) or a directive → the introduction *plants* the question (INT-6; see Giving Direction in `03b`).
2. **Write the first Situation sentence:** about the subject (INT-4), self-sufficient (INT-5), noncontroversial, and anchored in time and place (INT-7).
   - Decision: if no such sentence can be written, revisit the subject or move the starting point earlier or later in the story.
3. **Extend the Situation only as far as the story needs.** Test each sentence: does the reader know this, or would they accept it without proof? (INT-2). For a broad readership, apply the business-press test (INT-6).
4. **Write the Complication.** Imagine the reader's "yes, so what?" and supply the next development (INT-8). Compare it with Exhibit 10's four types: task blocked; solution known; solution proposed; action failed.
5. **Write the Question down privately** (INT-22) and decide whether the text will state it or leave it implied.
   - Decision: does C provoke exactly this Q? If not, either adopt the Q that C actually provokes or rework C.
6. **State the Answer**, which is the pyramid's top point (INT-9). Confirm it answers Q directly.
7. **Check for a single question** (INT-20). Collapse "whether and how" pairs.
8. **Size it** (INT-15, INT-16): usually 2–3 paragraphs; S and C ≤ 3–4 paragraphs each; one sentence for close readers. Remove any exhibits.
9. **Pick the order for tone** (INT-10): standard S-C-A; direct A-S-C; concerned C-S-A; aggressive Q-S-C-A.
10. **Add the plan** (INT-11, INT-12, INT-13).
    - Long document → list the Key Line points as ideas, indented after the main point, then the first heading.
    - Short document → no list; underlined topic sentences instead.
11. **Blend in background** (INT-17) and remove any "Background"/"Introduction" section (INT-14).
12. **Read it as a story** and revise until it sounds right (INT-23).

### B. Recover the Question when it is unclear (reason backward)
1. List the points the body will make.
2. For the points collectively, ask why the reader should know them, and name the question they jointly answer.
3. Ask why that question would have arisen. That is the Complication.
4. Ask in what stable, known circumstances it arose. That is the Situation.
5. Draft S-C-Q-A from these answers, then run Procedure A from step 4 to check that C provokes Q and A answers it.

### C. Write the introduction for each Key Line section
1. Write a heading that states the point's substance, more specific than a topic word (INT-18).
2. Determine the reader's vantage point (INT-19):
   - First section: why does this sub-subject matter to the overall main point?
   - Later sections: how does it follow from the section just finished?
3. Write a brief S (what the reader knows about this sub-subject, or a scenario they'd readily accept), then a C (the development that raises a question). Note the Q privately.
4. State the Key Line point as the answer, then support it.
5. Keep it much shorter than the opening introduction.

### D. Review an existing introduction (checklist)
1. Tag sentences S / C / Q / A. Anything missing or untagged? (INT-1)
2. Does the opening sentence concern the subject, stand alone, invite agreement, and fix time and place? (INT-4, INT-5, INT-7)
3. Any statements the reader would need proved? Any exhibits? (INT-2)
4. Does C grow out of S and force exactly one Q? (INT-8, INT-20)
5. Is A the pyramid's top point, and does it answer Q? (INT-9)
6. Is the length within bounds for this reader? (INT-15, INT-16)
7. Long document: Key Line listed as ideas? Short document: underlined topic sentences instead? (INT-11–INT-13)
8. Any "Background"/"Introduction" section, or subject-label headings? (INT-13, INT-14)
9. Does each Key Line section have its own mini-story tied to the reader's position? (INT-18, INT-19)

## Diagnostics
| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Opens with "This memo describes / gathers thoughts on…" followed by a list of section topics | No story; Key Line given as subject labels, so no message reaches the reader | Rebuild as S-C-Q-A; restate sections as ideas (INT-1, INT-13, INT-11) |
| A body section headed "Background" or "Introduction" | Background treated as a peer of the main points; wrong abstraction level | Blend the facts into the introduction's story; delete the section (INT-14, INT-17) |
| Charts, tables, or new data in the introduction | Informing instead of reminding; belabouring the obvious | Move evidence into the body; keep only accepted facts (INT-2, INT-15) |
| The reader would contest something in the opening paragraphs | Disputable material placed ahead of the Answer | Move the claim into the argument beneath the main point (INT-2, INT-3, INT-5) |
| The first sentence only makes sense after a later one ("This issue has…") | Opening isn't self-sufficient | Begin at a point that stands alone (INT-5) |
| Opening is a timeless generality with no who/where/when | Story never anchored | Pin it to a specific organisation, place, period, or event (INT-7) |
| Situation runs past four paragraphs or reads like a history lecture | Over-reminding; probably includes material the reader doesn't know | Cut to what's needed to stand in the same place (INT-15, INT-2) |
| Introduction too brief for the reader to tell what is being answered | Under-reminding | Add enough S/C to bring back the question (INT-15) |
| Complication is a list of unrelated issues, or creates no tension | C doesn't provoke a single Q | Rewrite C as the one next development (INT-8) |
| Main point doesn't answer the question the introduction sets up | C/Q and A are mismatched | Re-derive Q from C, or reason backward from the body (INT-8, INT-9, INT-21) |
| A "whether X, and if so how?" question kept as two | Two-question document | Make "yes" the top point and "how" the Key Line question (INT-20) |
| Writer can't say what the document's question is | Q never written down | Write Q down; if still unclear, reason backward (INT-22, INT-21) |
| Long report reveals its main points only section by section | No plan in the introduction | List the Key Line points after the main point (INT-11) |
| Short memo lists its points, then repeats each one as a heading | Redundant list | Use underlined topic sentences (INT-12) |
| Each section starts with a one-word heading, restates its point, then bullets | No section introduction; heading lacks substance | Add a brief S-C-Q lead-in under a substantive heading (INT-18) |
| Later section introductions repeat the opening context rather than linking to the previous section | Ignores the reader's current vantage point | Bridge from the section just finished (INT-19) |
| Tone misfires (too blunt, too alarmed, too flat) | Order of parts chosen without thought | Pick the standard, direct, concerned, or aggressive order deliberately (INT-10) |

## Templates and patterns

**Thinking skeleton (draft in this order even if the final text uses another):**
```
Subject: <what the document is about>
S  = <established, agreed truth about the subject, fixed in time/place>
C  = <next development that creates tension>
Q  = <the single question C provokes>   (may stay implicit in the text)
A  = <main point = apex of the pyramid>
New Q raised by A = <why? / how? / which?>
Key Line = <points answering the new Q>
```

**Exhibit 12 page layout for a long document (p. 41):**
```
TITLE

[Situation paragraph(s)]

[Complication paragraph(s) — Question normally implicit here]

[Main point]
      • Key Line point 1 (an idea, not a topic)
      • Key Line point 2
      • Key Line point 3

FIRST HEADING
[mini-introduction (S-C-Q) → point 1 → support]
```

**Short document variant (p. 41):**
```
[S] [C] [Main point]
<u>Key Line point 1 as topic sentence.</u> Support…
<u>Key Line point 2 as topic sentence.</u> Support…
```

**Four orders for tone (p. 40):**
```
Standard:   S → C → A (solution)        measured, factual default
Direct:     A → S → C                    recommendation first
Concerned:  C → S → A                    worried; troubling fact first
Aggressive: Q → S → C → A                challenging; question first, stresses risk
```

**Exhibit 10 cheat-sheet (p. 37):**
```
Task to do        + something blocks it        → What should we do?
Problem exists    + solution is known          → How do we put it into effect?
Problem exists    + a solution is put forward  → Is it the right one?
Action was taken  + it failed                  → Why?
```

**Key Line section heading + mini-introduction (pp. 46–48):**
```
<HEADING THAT CARRIES THE POINT'S SUBSTANCE, e.g. "BENCHMARKING PROCESS EFFICIENCY">
S = <what the reader knows / a scenario they readily accept, linked to the prior section>
C = <development that raises a question>
Q = <question>                      (usually implicit)
A = <this Key Line point>
    <support for the point>
```

**Generic "others get the result" opening (Exhibit 13, p. 46):**
```
S = Tool X is supposed to deliver result Y
C = We use X, but competitors are the ones achieving Y
Q = What explains their success?
A = They combine X with A and B      → new Q: how do these produce Y?  → Key Line = steps
```

## Worked examples

**Two Irishmen (p. 36) — the unfinished story as attention device.** A one-line joke opening (two Irishmen, a bridge, midnight, a strange city) takes over the reader's attention, fixes it on a time and place, and holds it until the punch line. Lesson: an introduction should hook the reader's existing interest in its subject the same way, by telling a story.

**Typical opening sentences (p. 37) — anchoring in time and place.** Four genre samples:
- memo: a company weighing exports between two plants;
- report: health services under resource strain, including a named national one;
- magazine article: early human artifacts were purely practical;
- book: managers are products of their culture.

Each states something acceptable, launches a story, and invites "so what?". Lesson: the Situation's first line is concrete, agreeable, and story-launching in any genre.

**Exhibit 10 (p. 37) — four S/C/Q types.** See the table under Concepts. Lesson: the Complication usually takes one of four forms, and each fixes the reader's question.

**Exhibit 11 (pp. 38–39) — four HBR introductions, one per Exhibit 10 type.** Minto presents the set as illustrating every one of the four types, and the order matches Exhibit 10's rows.
- *Hertz, on risk in capital investment:* S = executives must choose among competing capital projects; C = they lack a way to gauge the risk created by uncertain assumptions; Q = can that risk be measured realistically?; A = yes. Ch. 5 (pp. 66–67) returns to this article as a case where a deductive Key Line is justified: the reader has to grasp the reasoning behind the method before its steps make sense (see `04-deduction-and-induction.md`).
- *Herzberg, on motivating employees:* S = managers want staff to take particular actions; C = that means applying the psychology of motivation, which is poorly understood and much hyped; Q = how?; A = use this article's ideas.
- *Levitt, "Marketing Myopia":* S = many big industries have stalled or face decline; C = the presumed cause is market saturation; Q = is that presumption right?; A = no, management has failed.
- *Hayes & Abernathy, on U.S. economic decline:* S = U.S. business has deteriorated sharply; C = the standard explanations (oil, regulation, taxes) hit Germany and France just as hard, yet U.S. decline is steeper; Q = why?; A = managers neglect long-term technological competitiveness.
- Lesson: one bare S-C-Q skeleton can carry very different styles, and the Answer can be a single word.

**Firm Development Project memo (p. 40) — one skeleton, four orders.**
- *Skeleton:* S = diversification work up 40% in five years; C = no provable client benefit; Q = how do we make such studies pay off for clients?; A = run a Firm Development Project.
- *Standard (S-C-solution):* the firm has charged many clients heavily for diversification work → yet nobody in the office has won the prize offered for proving a client deal the firm made happen → with 40% growth, now is the time for a project → the memo will set out issues and hypotheses to test (a preview of what follows).
- *Direct (solution-S-C):* the top project priority should be helping clients diversify → this work grew 40% → yet no deal can be credited to the firm.
- *Concerned (C-S-solution):* to the writer's knowledge, no study here has produced results the client couldn't have managed alone → alarming given 40% growth → billing for this endangers our reputation and our conscience → so run a project.
- *Aggressive (Q-S-C, then solution):* how do we keep diversification work significant? → it's 40% of practice, but we've rarely added value → momentum is at risk → launch the project now.
- Lesson: identical content, with tone set by the order of the parts.

**Exhibit 12 (p. 41) — listing the Key Line.** Page mock-up: title, Situation block, Complication (Question) block, main point, three indented Key Line points, then the first heading. Lesson: in a long document the reader should hold the whole argument before the first heading.

**Project-team approach memo (p. 42) — a useless list.**
- *Before:* the introduction announces a description of a project-team approach to profit improvement and lists six section subjects.
- *Diagnosis:* subjects rather than ideas; a "Background" section; probably scrambled logic (benefits belong under principles, prerequisites under organisation).
- *After (implied by the critique):* a story introduction ending in a main point plus a few Key Line *assertions*.
- Lesson: write about ideas, never categories.

**Length samples (pp. 43–45) — length follows the reader.** Minto gives these without S/C/Q labels; the structural readings are this file's.
- *Letter:* recalls a published claim (that a Japanese firm, not the American inventor, commercialised the transistor because the inventor didn't know what to do with it), then flatly denies it. Two short paragraphs: S, then A.
- *Newspaper editorial:* a government's attack on TV networks and the networks' reply are both bogus, leaving the public thinking liberty is threatened. The real issue is what kind of society television should help build.
- *Magazine article (Ames, HBR):* product managers deserve credit, and the concept is widely adopted → yet dissatisfaction is surging → does that mean the concept is unworkable? → no, management misapplies a sound tool. Full S-C-Q-A in three paragraphs.
- *Internal memo:* a department maintains a procedures manual that needs periodic revision → for consistency, everyone should follow the method set out below. A two-sentence directive opening (reused in `03b`).
- *Consulting report (an insurer):* long-standing industry leader with steady growth → its market is shifting in three named ways → management sees chronic field problems and home-office weaknesses, and that the home office must be fixed first → the report explains how.
- *Essay (Keynes, 1930):* the world is slow to grasp that it is in an economic catastrophe, and ordinary people now overreact with fear → dream ending or passing nightmare? → a nightmare that will pass, since productive capacity is intact and the trouble is mismanagement of a delicate machine.
- *Book (Gibbon):* second-century Rome's extent, order, and prosperity, then a statement of the plan (these chapters describe the prosperity; later ones trace the decline).
- *Publishing project (1704 weekly review, via Trevelyan):* the founding introduction presents the paper as the start of a large design to clarify European affairs against contradictory street-press accounts.
- Lesson: openings range from a sentence or two to several paragraphs across genres. The reader's needs set the length.

**Exhibit 13 (pp. 46–48) — "Management Tools for the Nineties": Key Line points need introductions.**
```
S = TQM was the leading 1980s tool for cutting cost / lifting quality → competitive edge, profits
C = Most big firms adopted it without the hoped-for gains; market leaders still hold or gain share and profits
Q = Why? What are the leaders doing differently?
A = Leaders are supplementing TQM with Benchmarking and Activity-Based Management (ABM)
    (new Q: how does that produce the advantage? → plural noun "steps")
  ├─ Benchmark: compare how efficiently and effectively their delivery processes perform against rivals'
  ├─ ABM: establish what each product or service truly costs to offer
  └─ Aim TQM at the processes that matter most to the business
```
- *Wrong way:* a one-word heading ("BENCHMARKING"), the point restated, then three bare sub-steps (measure key-process efficiency; compare with competitors; find causes of gaps).
- *Right way:* section introductions under substantive headings:
  - BENCHMARKING PROCESS EFFICIENCY: S = imagine TQM has cut loan-processing time from two days to two hours; C = it's tempting to assume that secures an edge; Q = does it?; A = only a comparison with competitors can tell.
  - DETERMINING REAL COSTS: S = imagine benchmarking has made you the best; C = pride is justified only if the product's return exceeds its true cost; Q = how do you know your strength is worth having?; A = cost by activity rather than by function (ABM).
  - ADJUSTING TQM TECHNIQUES: S = with benchmarking and ABM done, you know your weak processes and your costly or highly profitable products; C = now to tighten those processes; Q = is that TQM's job?; A = yes, but focused on the processes that matter most.
- Lesson: each section gets its own mini-story fitted to the reader's position. The first links to the overall point, and later ones link to the section before. These mini-stories use readily accepted hypotheticals ("imagine…") as their Situation.

**Whether-and-how market entry question (pp. 48–49) — one question only.**
- *Before:* a compound question about whether to enter a market and, if so, how.
- *After:* the Q becomes how to enter. If the answer to "whether" were no, "how" would never come up. If it is yes, "yes, enter" becomes the top point and "how?" is answered on the Key Line.
- Lesson: related questions collapse into one, and the conditional part becomes the pyramid's apex.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **The Complication need not be a problem** (p. 37). It is whatever development creates tension and raises the question: "the solution is known", "a solution has been proposed", "we want X but need you to do Y".
- **The Question is often left unwritten.** Exhibit 12 even places it inside the Complication block. Write it down privately anyway (INT-22, p. 50).
- **Remind vs plant.**
  - The core doctrine is that introductions remind.
  - Broad-audience documents (pp. 36–37) and directives (p. 50, in `03b`) instead *plant* a question the reader doesn't yet have. Even then the content is accepted knowledge, arranged so the question arises.
  - Appendix B (p. 230) refines this further: when the reader doesn't know a problem exists, the introduction may still say enough for the reader to *see* that one could exist and that the proposed fix could be plausible.
- **Order is flexible on the page, not in the head.** Any order can serve tone (p. 40), but Ch. 3 (p. 31) still advises thinking from the Situation first.
- **A one-sentence introduction is legitimate** for a reader the writer deals with closely, provided it still recalls that reader's question (p. 42).
- **Introduction length is independent of document length** (p. 43). A long report to a close colleague may have a very short introduction.
- **Background is allowed, but only inside the story.** The p. 48 list explicitly admits history, the writer's involvement, earlier studies, definitions, and admissions. What is banned is a separate "Background" section (p. 42) or material that needs proof (p. 48).
- **The book names the elements inconsistently.**
  - p. 48 counts three story elements ("Situation, Complication, and Solution"), and pp. 40/48 speak of a situation-complication-solution form.
  - Appendix C (p. 236) and Ch. 2 name four: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer.
  - Treat Question as always present but often implicit, and Solution as equivalent to Answer.
- **The reader's Question may be open, not just yes/no.** Ch. 4's own examples include "how?", "why?", and "which?" questions (Exhibit 10, Exhibit 13, and the patterns in `03b`). Ch. 9's requirement that issues be phrased as yes/no questions applies to analytical issues, not to an introduction's Question (see `08-structuring-the-analysis.md`).
- **Section introductions are much briefer** than the opening one (p. 45), and their content depends on position (p. 48).
- **Short-document exception** to listing the Key Line: use underlined topic sentences (p. 41).
- **Persuasive power cuts both ways.** Minto openly says the narrative is a partial selection of facts that narrows the reader's alternative interpretations (p. 59).
  > **Skill note:** a reviewing skill may want to flag introductions whose Situation omits facts the reader would consider material, since the story's plausibility can carry a one-sided framing.
- **Beginners' warning:** introductions are where most failures occur (p. 43). The cure is exposure to many examples until a right-sounding one is recognisable.

> **Skill note:** a useful automated test that combines INT-2, INT-4, and INT-8 is to simulate the reader. After the Situation, the reader should only be able to respond "yes, I know, so what?". After the Complication, that "so what?" should sharpen into exactly the Question the main point answers.
