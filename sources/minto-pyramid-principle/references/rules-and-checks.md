## Part 1 — The core on one page

These are the rules a skill should hold in working memory. Everything else is elaboration.

**Think first, write later.** Arrange the ideas as a pyramid and test it before drafting any prose, slide, or heading. Unclear writing is usually an ordering problem, not a style problem. (PYR-1, PYR-2, BLD-1)

**The three pyramid rules.** (PYR-7, PYR-8, PYR-9)
1. An idea at any level summarizes the ideas grouped directly beneath it.
2. The ideas in a grouping are the same kind of idea, nameable with one plural noun.
3. The ideas in a grouping are in a logical order: deductive (argument), time, structural, or degree.

**One top point, delivered first.** The whole document supports a single governing thought, and every summary comes before what it summarizes. (PYR-3, PYR-6)

**Vertical: question and answer.** Each point raises a question in the reader's mind; the grouping beneath it answers that question and nothing else. Stop descending when the reader would have no further question. (PYR-11, PYR-12, PYR-13)

**Horizontal: deductive or inductive, never both.** Deductive: a statement, a comment on its subject or predicate, then the "therefore"; summarize on the last point; at most four points and two "therefores"; push it low in the pyramid. Inductive: like ideas under one plural noun; the parent states what their likeness implies, about these items only. Prefer induction at the Key Line. (PYR-14, DED-3, DED-5, DED-9, DED-10, DED-13)

**Introduction = a story the reader already knows.** Situation (accepted, about the subject) → Complication (the next development) → Question (one, stated or implied) → Answer (the top point). It reminds; it never informs, proves, or shows exhibits. In long documents it ends by listing the Key Line points. (INT-1, INT-2, INT-8, INT-9, INT-11, INT-20)

**Order follows source.** Actions or causes producing an effect go in time order; parts of a whole go in structural order; members of a class go in degree order, strongest first. No discernible order means the grouping is wrong or incomplete. (ORD-3, ORD-4, ORD-25)

**No intellectually blank assertions.** "There are three problems" is not a summary. Summarize actions by their direct effect, worded as an end product; summarize situations by the inference their similarity supports. You can't summarize a grouping that isn't MECE. (SUM-1, SUM-4, SUM-5, SUM-7, SUM-14)

**Group sizes.** Four or five items per grouping; regroup beyond that. (PYR-5, ORD-8, PAG-19)

**Problems before analysis.** Define the problem as a gap between an undesired result (R1) and a measurable desired result (R2) arising in a concrete situation; structure the analysis with MECE frameworks and yes/no questions before gathering data. (DEF-2, DEF-9, ANL-1, ANL-5, ANL-6)

**Make the structure visible.** Headings state ideas (never "Background" or "Findings"), are parallel within a group, never stand alone, and are introduced. Slides carry one message stated as the title. Sentences copy a picture of the relationship. (PAG-7, PAG-8, PAG-11, PAG-13, SCR-10, SCR-19, PRO-5)

## Part 2 — Review checklist

Run the passes in order and stop to fix the pyramid when an early pass fails: a fault at one level invalidates the review of everything below it. Each item is a yes/no question; "no" is a finding.

### Pass 1 — The governing thought
- [ ] Can the document's point be stated as one sentence (Subject + Answer)? (PYR-6, BLD-2)
- [ ] Does that sentence appear at the top (in the first 30 seconds of reading), not at the end? (PYR-3, BLD-14, PAG-3)
- [ ] Does it answer the question this reader actually has, rather than describing the subject? (PYR-17, BLD-3)

### Pass 2 — The introduction
- [ ] Are Situation, Complication, Question (stated or implied), and Answer all present, as one story? (INT-1)
- [ ] Does the Situation open with an accepted statement about the subject, anchored in time and place? (INT-4, INT-5, INT-7)
- [ ] Does the Complication lead to exactly one Question, and does the Answer answer it? (INT-8, INT-9, INT-20, BLD-6)
- [ ] Is everything in it already known to or accepted by the reader, with no proof or exhibits? (INT-2, BLD-19)
- [ ] Is history folded into the story rather than placed in a "Background" section or the body? (INT-14, INT-17, BLD-18)
- [ ] Is it the right length for this reader, not padded to match the document's length? (INT-15, INT-16)
- [ ] Long document: are the Key Line points listed after the main point? Short document: are the points underlined topic sentences? (INT-11, INT-12)
- [ ] If the reader is acting on a problem: does the Question match where they stand (no solution yet, proposed, chosen, failed, several options, R2 unclear, unsure whether there is a problem)? (DEF-13, DEF-14)
- [ ] Do alternatives appear only if the reader raised them? (DEF-17, PAT-9)
- [ ] Does the pattern match the question family (what to do / whether / how / why)? (PAT-1, `03b` catalog)

### Pass 3 — The Key Line
- [ ] Do the Key Line points, taken together, answer the question the top point raises, completely and with nothing extra? (PYR-7, PYR-12, BLD-7)
- [ ] Is the Key Line inductive? If deductive, is that justified (unexpected answer, or actions that need prior explanation), and is it at most four points? (DED-5, DED-7, DED-10)
- [ ] Do recommendations sit on the Key Line, rather than arriving last after findings and conclusions? (DED-6, DED-8)
- [ ] Is a choice argued by showing it meets R2, not by knocking down the alternatives? (DEF-18, PAT-12)
- [ ] Is the Key Line's plural noun right for the situation (steps, changes, reasons, criteria)? (BLD-7, DEF-19, PAT-3)

### Pass 4 — Every grouping: kind, MECE, order
- [ ] Can each grouping be named with one plural noun? (PYR-8, DED-11)
- [ ] Is it MECE: no overlaps, no gaps? (ORD-15, SUM-4, ANL-5)
- [ ] Is its order identifiable and matched to its source (time / structure / degree)? (ORD-3, ORD-4)
- [ ] Are cause and effect on separate levels, and actions separate from outcomes? (ORD-9, ORD-11, ORD-14)
- [ ] Are there four or five items at most? (ORD-8)
- [ ] Is no item at a different level of abstraction from its siblings? (ORD-21)
- [ ] For ranked classes: is the strongest first, and is the implied priority intended? (ORD-25, ORD-26)
- [ ] Is no list "news", meaning true facts that share nothing? (DED-17)

### Pass 5 — Every summary
- [ ] Does every grouping have a summary that states an idea, not the number and kind of items below? (SUM-1)
- [ ] Action groupings: is every action worded as an end product, and is the summary the direct, specific effect? (SUM-7, SUM-8, SUM-14)
- [ ] Does the summary avoid overclaiming ("will achieve" where the steps only enable)? (SUM-15)
- [ ] Situation groupings: does the summary state what the shared subject, predicate, or judgment implies, without going beyond these items? (SUM-3, SUM-20, DED-12, DED-13)
- [ ] Can you say "why these ideas and no others" for each grouping? (SUM-29)

### Pass 6 — Reasoning
- [ ] Is each grouping purely deductive or purely inductive? (PYR-14, DED-15)
- [ ] Are deductive chains short and low in the pyramid, with no step the reader won't supply? (DED-4, DED-9, DED-10)
- [ ] Is no causal chain disguised as a parallel list? (DED-14)
- [ ] Are hypothesized causes labelled as possibilities until tested, with tests that give keep/drop answers? (ABD-4, ABD-9)

### Pass 7 — Problem-solving documents (reports, proposals, plans)
- [ ] Is the problem defined as R1 → R2 within a concrete Opening Scene, with R2 measurable? (DEF-2, DEF-4, DEF-9)
- [ ] Was the analysis structured by a diagnostic framework before data were gathered, with yes/no questions? (ANL-1, ANL-6, ANL-15)
- [ ] Does every grouped list map onto a MECE tree, with no orphans or gaps? (ANL-18)
- [ ] Is there no "Key Issues" list or "Benefits" section standing in for the argument? (ANL-21, PAT-15)
- [ ] Are problems always paired with solutions? (PAT-19)

### Pass 8 — On the page
- [ ] Does the formatting device fit the length and audience? (PAG-1, `10` P2)
- [ ] Do heading levels map to pyramid levels, and do headings state ideas so that the TOC reads as a summary of the argument? (PAG-4, PAG-13)
- [ ] Are there never single subsections or stacked headings without text, and are sibling headings parallel? (PAG-7, PAG-8, PAG-11)
- [ ] Does the text stand without the headings? (PAG-10)
- [ ] Does each Key Line section open with a mini-story or backward reference, then preview its points? (PAG-26 to PAG-30, INT-18)
- [ ] Does the ending avoid restating, with Next Steps only for self-evident actions? (PAG-32, PAG-33)

### Pass 9 — On screen
- [ ] Was the introduction written and the deck storyboarded before slides were made? (SCR-20, SCR-21)
- [ ] Is every slide title a statement of its point? (SCR-10, SCR-24)
- [ ] One idea per slide, about 6 lines or 30 words at most? (SCR-9, SCR-11)
- [ ] Mostly exhibits, each with its form chosen after its question? (SCR-5, SCR-18, SCR-19)
- [ ] Are transitions spoken rather than shown, with no slide read verbatim? (SCR-3, SCR-6)

### Pass 10 — Prose
- [ ] Could a reader sketch the relationship each key sentence describes? (PRO-5, PRO-6)
- [ ] Do concrete nouns act through plain verbs, with the action attributed to the right noun? (PRO-8, PRO-9)
- [ ] Are missing specifics flagged for the author rather than invented? (PRO-10)

## Part 3 — The author's summary, paraphrased (Appendix C)

Appendix C condenses each chapter into numbered points plus boxed "key concepts", "key processes", and "key thinking techniques". Paraphrased:

| Chapter | Key points |
|---|---|
| 1 Why a pyramid | Ideas must be grouped to be communicated; grouped ideas form a pyramid of levels of abstraction; top-down is the most efficient delivery; the three rules; slot ideas into the pyramid and test them before writing. |
| 2 Substructures | A box holds an idea, meaning a statement that raises a reader question; vertical = question/answer dialogue; horizontal = deductive or inductive, not both; the top point answers a question arising from what the reader knows; the introduction tells the story that recalls that question (S → C → Q → A). |
| 3 Building | Top-down: Subject, Question, Answer, check that S and C lead to Q, verify the Answer, fill in the Key Line. Bottom-up: list the points, work out their relationships, draw conclusions, work back to the introduction. |
| 4 Introductions | Introductions remind rather than inform; always S, C, Q, A; length depends on the reader and subject; each Key Line grouping gets a mini-introduction. |
| 5 Deduction and induction | In deduction the second point comments on the first and the third states what follows from both holding at once; summarize by lifting the last point and adding "because"; induction infers from a set of like conclusions; prefer induction at the Key Line. |
| 6 Logical order | Ordering exposes "news" lists and omissions; order reflects source (time ← process, structure ← commenting on a structure, importance ← building a class); no order means no logical relationship or incomplete thinking. For action ideas: state each so specifically it implies a tangible end product, group by shared effect, order by the underlying process or structure, check for missing steps. For situation ideas: group by similar kind, identify the structure or class, rewrite as full sentences and order, check for missing points. |
| 7 Summarizing | No blank assertions; only MECE groupings can be summarized; actions go in time order and are summarized by their direct effect; situation ideas share a kind of subject, predicate, or judgment. Sorting actions: pare to essence, separate levels (before / in order to), word as end products, derive the effect. Sorting situations: find structural similarities, name the narrow category that fits only these, state the inference. |
| 8 Defining the problem | Lay out Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, R2, what has been done so far, and the Question; convert to an introduction by reading left to right and down; the last thing the reader knows is always the Complication. |
| 9 Structuring the analysis | Show the problem area with diagnostic frameworks (system interactions, cause-and-effect activities, classified causes); gather data to prove or disprove which elements cause the problem; use logic trees to generate and test solutions and to expose the relationships hidden in lists. Key process: define, frame, hypothesize, gather data to prove or disprove. |
| 10 On the page | Highlight the structure with headings, indentation, underlining, and numbering; show transitions between major groups. |
| 11 On a screen | Text slides short and direct; exhibit slides with their message stated across the top; storyboard the structure; rehearse repeatedly. |
| 12 In prose | Visualize the image of what you mean, then copy the image in words. |

### PYR — Fundamentals (`01-pyramid-fundamentals.md`)

- **PYR-3** — Put the summarizing idea ahead of the ideas it summarizes, at every level of the document. — pp. 5–7
- **PYR-4** — Never present a grouping without stating the idea that unites it. — p. 4
- **PYR-5** — Keep each grouping within short-term memory limits (no more than about seven; fewer is easier; three is handy); once a set passes four or five items, sort it into categories under summaries. — pp. 3–4
- **PYR-6** — Make the whole document rest on exactly one top thought, with every idea below explaining or supporting it. — pp. 8–9
- **PYR-7** — Make every idea at every level a summary of the ideas grouped directly beneath it. — pp. 9–10
- **PYR-8** — Make every idea within a grouping the same kind of idea — nameable by one plural noun. — p. 10

### BLD — Building the pyramid (`02-building-the-pyramid.md`)

- **BLD-1** — Try the top-down approach first; never dictate or draft the whole document "to get it down" and structure it afterward. — pp. 21, 31
- **BLD-2** — Start from what you already know: the top will be one sentence whose subject is the document's Subject and whose predicate is the Answer to an existing reader Question that arose from a known Situation and Complication. — p. 21
- **BLD-3** — Fill the top box in the order Subject → Question → Answer, skipping ahead whenever one is unknown, and at least confirm that you *can* answer. — p. 22; Exh. 4
- **BLD-4** — Begin the introduction with the first uncontroversial statement about the Subject that the reader will accept — because it is familiar to them or a matter of verifiable record. — pp. 22–23, 25
- **BLD-5** — Derive the Complication by imagining the reader responding "Fine — and?": state what occurred within the Situation (a setback, a new problem, an inconsistency) that triggers the Question. — p. 23
- **BLD-6** — Recheck that the Complication leads straight to the Question you wrote and that your Answer answers it; if not, adopt the question it does raise, or revise the Complication, and rethink. — p. 23
- **BLD-7** — Find the Key Line by writing down the New Question the Answer raises, deciding whether to answer it inductively or deductively, and (if inductively) naming the plural noun. — p. 25; Exh. 4
- **BLD-8** — Confirm the Key Line points are the right ones and logically ordered, then repeat the question/answer routine at each lower level; for a very short document you may stop structuring and write. — p. 26
- **BLD-9** — If you can't settle the top (Subject, Question or reader knowledge unclear), drop to the Key Line; if no Key Line points come, work bottom-up: list all points, work out how they connect, derive conclusions. — p. 26
- **BLD-10** — When restructuring someone else's document, treat it as a closed universe: clarify what it says without judging whether it is true or right. — p. 26
- **BLD-11** — In bottom-up work, sort the listed points by type (e.g. problems vs. solutions) and examine the action ideas first, merging any that say the same thing. — p. 28
- **BLD-12** — Lay out cause-and-effect relationships among situation points visually, looking for separate chains of reasoning and for missing links. — p. 28
- **BLD-13** — When the points could support more than one conclusion, choose by thinking through the introduction — what the reader already knows determines their Question, and so which structure answers it. — p. 29
- **BLD-14** — End the introduction by stating the Key Line points, so the reader has your whole thinking within about the first 30 seconds; if not, rewrite. — p. 29
- **BLD-15** — Word headings as ideas (the point of the section), not as categories; never use headings like "Findings" or "Conclusions". — p. 30; see Ch. 10
- **BLD-16** — Begin thinking about the introduction with the Situation, even though S, C, Q, A may later be written in any order for effect. — p. 31
- **BLD-17** — Never skip thinking through the introduction, even when the main point and its Question feel obvious. — p. 31
- **BLD-18** — Put historical chronology ("what happened") in the introduction; in the body, mention events only when spelling out cause-and-effect relationships. — pp. 31–32
- **BLD-19** — Limit the introduction to what the reader will accept as true (known, or readily verifiable by a neutral observer); keep anything new to them out of it, and keep anything they already know out of the pyramid. — p. 32
- **BLD-20** — Given a choice, make the Key Line inductive rather than deductive, even if the ideas were developed deductively. — p. 32; full treatment in DED-5
- **BLD-21** — Treat a deductive Key Line step that raises no question (typically the "therefore" restating the top point) as a sign of overstructuring, and recast the Key Line inductively. — p. 33

### INT — Introductions (`03-introductions.md`)

- **INT-2** — Put in the introduction only what the reader already knows or will accept as true. Nothing that needs proof, and no exhibits. — pp. 42, 48; cf. Ch. 3 p. 32
- **INT-3** — Tell a story the reader already knows, so that agreeable points come before contestable ones. — p. 36
- **INT-4** — Open the Situation with a statement *about the subject* that the reader will endorse. — p. 36
- **INT-5** — Begin at the earliest point where a self-sufficient, noncontroversial statement can be made. — p. 36
- **INT-6** — For broad-audience documents (widely circulated reports, articles, books), plant the question by narrating generally accepted knowledge, assuming moderately well-informed readers. — pp. 36–37
- **INT-7** — Tie the opening Situation to a specific time and place. — p. 37
- **INT-8** — Write the Complication as the next development in the story, growing from the established Situation and leading unavoidably to one Question. It need not be a problem. — p. 37
- **INT-9** — Make the Answer (the story's resolution) the main point, the apex of the pyramid. — p. 36
- **INT-10** — Reorder S, C, Q, A only deliberately, to set a tone (standard, direct, concerned, aggressive), and never omit an element. — pp. 40, 48

- **INT-12** — In a short document (a paragraph or two per point), skip the listed points and headings. Make each point the underlined topic sentence of its paragraph. — p. 41

- **INT-14** — Never include a section headed "Background" or "Introduction". — p. 42
- **INT-15** — Make the introduction long enough for writer and reader to stand in the same place, and no longer. — pp. 42–43
- **INT-16** — Set introduction length by the reader's needs and the subject's demands, not by the document's length. — pp. 43, 48
- **INT-17** — Blend necessary background (problem history, the writer's involvement, earlier studies and their findings, definitions, admissions) into the story rather than presenting it as a separate block. — p. 48
- **INT-18** — Open every Key Line section with a brief S-C-Q story, under a heading that conveys the substance of the point (not a one-word topic), before stating and supporting the point. — pp. 45–47; App. C p. 236
- **INT-19** — Fit each section introduction to the reader's current vantage point. — p. 48
- **INT-20** — Allow exactly one opening Question per document. If two appear, fold them so one contains the other (e.g. "whether and how" becomes "how", with "yes" as the top point). — pp. 48–49
- **INT-21** — When the Question won't surface from the introduction, reason backward from the body. Ask what question the points answer, then what situation would have prompted it. — p. 49
- **INT-22** — Write the Question down privately before drafting, even when the text will leave it unstated. — p. 50
- **INT-23** — Spend disproportionate effort on the introduction and rework it until it sounds right. — pp. 43, 59

### PAT — Introduction patterns (`03b-introduction-patterns.md`)

- **PAT-1** — Before drafting, place the reader's question in one of the four families (what to do / whether to go ahead / how / why) and pick the matching pattern. — pp. 49, 216–217
- **PAT-2** — In a directive, plant the question: S = the writer's aim; C = what the writer needs from the reader to reach it; Q (implicit) = how does the reader do that? Pin the Q down privately even though the text won't state it. — pp. 50–51
- **PAT-3** — When the reader's question is "how?", make the Key Line a set of steps (actions). — pp. 51, 53
- **PAT-4** — In a directive, check that the Answer roughly undoes the Complication. — p. 51
- **PAT-5** — In a funding request, leave "approve?" implicit, make the Answer "approve", and support it with the standard reasons. — pp. 51–52
- **PAT-6** — Mention extra benefits ("other goodies") only when facts back them, and never as the justification for acting. — p. 52
- **PAT-7** — In a how-to document, choose the introduction by whether the activity is new (S = activity now required; C = no capability yet) or existing but faulty (S = current system; C = it underperforms). — p. 53
- **PAT-8** — Before writing anything that changes a process, draw the present and recommended processes side by side, and derive the Key Line from their differences. — pp. 53–54, 226–233
- **PAT-9** — Treat options as genuine alternatives (S = want X; C = several routes; Q = which?) only when the reader already knows them. — pp. 54–55, 225; consistent with DEF-17, which places reader-raised alternatives in the Complication
- **PAT-10** — Having chosen among known options, organise the Key Line around the judgement criteria if the winner beats every rival on every criterion. Otherwise give one statement per option: the main reason for the winner, and the main reason each rival was dropped. — pp. 55–56
- **PAT-11** — When no option (or no possible action) gives the reader all they want, organise around alternative objectives (pick A if P matters most; B if Q does; …), not around alternative ways of solving the problem. — pp. 56, 225–226
- **PAT-12** — Judge options against the wanted result (R2), not against each other, and define R2 first. — p. 225
- **PAT-13** — If the reader insists on seeing alternatives despite a clear R2 and a clear solution, put them in the introduction (accepting some awkwardness) or in an appendix chart: options down the side, criteria across the top, check marks for matches. — pp. 225–226; cf. DEF-18
- **PAT-14** — Choose the proposal structure by context. — pp. 57–58, 222–223
  - New or competitive client: brief problem statement plus a "reasons to hire" Key Line (grasp of problem; method; experience; terms).
  - Known client or formality: full problem description in the introduction plus a Key Line of approach steps, with commercial terms in a covering letter.
- **PAT-15** — Don't build a proposal on the stock consulting headings, don't include a separate Benefits section, and keep qualifications and timing/staffing/fees outside the thinking structure. — pp. 221–222
- **PAT-16** — Organise every project plan around the process. — pp. 221–222
- **PAT-17** — In a "reasons" proposal, structure the content as follows. — p. 223
  - Open with a short paragraph previewing the parts (understanding, approach, experience, terms).
  - Explain the problem in the understanding section in Situation-R1-R2 form, aimed at the decision makers' hot buttons.
  - Use the experience section to spotlight specific or unique expertise.
- **PAT-18** — Open a progress review by recalling what was last reported or promised (S), then what the reader asked for or what was due next, now done (C), with the implicit "what turned up?" (Q). Lead with the finding. — pp. 58–59
- **PAT-19** — Never raise problems without also proposing solutions. — p. 230
- **PAT-20** — When the reader doesn't know the problem, give the introduction only enough for the reader to see that a problem could exist and that the fix could work. Explain the current system under each change, covering only the steps where faults occur. — pp. 230, 234
- **PAT-21** — When the reader knows both the old and the wanted process, state their question precisely (is it feasible? is it a good fix? should it be rolled out, and how?) and answer it, instead of offering a vague account of what was "examined". — pp. 227–229
- **PAT-22** — In a process-change document, make the Key Line the recommended changes, answering "what changes do you recommend?". Reason backward from them to the introduction, and expect each change to prompt "why?". — pp. 232–234

### DED — Deduction and induction (`04-deduction-and-induction.md`)

- **DED-1** — Decide, for every grouping, whether it is deductive or inductive — the only two ways ideas can be logically related. — p. 60
- **DED-2** — Build a deductive argument from three moves: an assertion about an existing state of affairs; a second assertion saying something about the first one's subject or predicate; what follows from both being true together. — p. 62
- **DED-3** — Summarize a deductive grouping with the weight on its final point (e.g. "Because X, Y" / "Since X, Y" / "If you want X, you must Y"). — pp. 60, 63
- **DED-4** — Skip steps or chain deductive arguments only when readers will readily supply and accept the missing steps. — p. 63
- **DED-5** — On the Key Line, avoid deduction and present the message inductively. — pp. 64–65, 67
- **DED-6** — Present the action before the argument — state *that* the reader should act and then justify each action, rather than explaining why and only then how. — pp. 65–66
- **DED-7** — Use a deductive Key Line only when (a) the top point clashes with what the reader expects the writer to say, or (b) the reader cannot understand the actions without the reasoning first. — pp. 66–67
- **DED-8** — To convert a findings → conclusions → recommendations analysis to inductive form, rotate it: put the recommendations on the Key Line and group each one's findings/conclusions beneath it. — p. 65
- **DED-9** — Keep deductive reasoning as far down the pyramid as possible — ideally within a paragraph. — p. 67
- **DED-10** — Keep any deductive argument to at most four points and at most two chained "therefore" points. — p. 68
- **DED-11** — Name each inductive grouping with one plural noun that covers every member, and remove the misfits. — pp. 68–69
- **DED-12** — Verify every inductive inference from the bottom up: from these items alone, could you reasonably reach the parent? — p. 70
- **DED-13** — Keep the inference inside the grouping: the parent must speak to its children specifically and only. — p. 70
- **DED-14** — When a claim rests on a single piece of evidence, or the items form a causal chain, treat the grouping as deductive — don't dress a cause-and-effect sequence up as a list of parallel items. — p. 71
- **DED-15** — Tell the forms apart with one test: if the second point says something about the first one's subject or predicate, the grouping is deductive; if not, it must share the first one's plural noun (inductive); if neither, the points aren't logically related. — p. 71
- **DED-16** — Build inductive groupings by fixing the subject and varying the predicate, or fixing the predicate and varying the subject, and base the inference on that shared element. — pp. 71–72
- **DED-17** — Cut points that share neither subject nor predicate with their siblings: they are news, not thinking, however true. — p. 72
- **DED-18** — State the top point before the ideas grouped under it. — p. 72

### ORD — Logical order (`05-logical-order.md`)

- **ORD-1** — Draft to one level below the Key Line, write, then go back and scrutinize every grouping in the prose. — p. 73
- **ORD-2** — Never let a list stand just because its items fit a plural noun ("steps," "problems"). Confirm the items share an internal logic. — p. 73
- **ORD-3** — Every inductive grouping must show one of three orders: time, structural, or degree. — pp. 75–77
- **ORD-4** — Choose the order that matches how the grouping was produced: causes of an effect → time; parts of a whole → structure; class of like things → degree. — pp. 75–77
- **ORD-5** — Don't agonize over deductive groupings' order; the argument fixes it. — p. 75
- **ORD-6** — Use order to test completeness. If a set of "steps" can't be put in one-two-three sequence, assume something is missing or doesn't belong. — p. 75
- **ORD-7** — For a set of actions that jointly produce a result, use time order and make the summary the effect of doing them. — p. 76
- **ORD-8** — Cap each grouping at four or five points. With more, look for items more closely related to each other and subgroup them. — p. 78
- **ORD-9** — Separate cause from effect in step lists. Pull out mini-processes so each level holds steps of the same level of abstraction. — pp. 78–79
- **ORD-10** — Picture yourself doing each action and name the tangible thing you'll hold when it's finished. — p. 79
- **ORD-11** — Don't mix actions and outcomes in one list of steps. Convert outcomes into an action (e.g., "assess the reaction") or remove them. — pp. 79–80
- **ORD-12** — Remove items that are attributes of another item (or of a stage) rather than members of the sequence. — p. 80
- **ORD-13** — When conclusions rest on an implicit process, surface the process and order the points by it. — pp. 80–81
- **ORD-14** — If one point in a group is the result of the others, move it up to be their summary. — p. 81
- **ORD-15** — When dividing a whole into parts, make the parts MECE. — p. 82
- **ORD-16** — Order structural parts by the principle of division: by activity → time order; by location → geographic/structural order; by product, market, or customer → degree order on a relevant measure (sales, investment). — p. 83
- **ORD-17** — When creating a new structure, force an order on its parts (e.g., the order needs arise) to check it's exhaustive for your purpose. — p. 83
- **ORD-18** — For a thing (not an organization) analyzed by how it works, divide by functioning part and present parts in the order they function. — pp. 83–84
- **ORD-19** — Describe a structure either top-down and left-to-right, or along a process the reader would naturally follow (such as the path the eye takes over a map). — pp. 84–85
- **ORD-20** — When recommending changes to a structure that are equally important and simultaneous, order them as you'd draw the new structure on a blank page, one element at a time. — p. 86
- **ORD-21** — Set aside any item that refers to all the other items (e.g., "identify strengths and weaknesses in each area"). It's not a peer. — p. 86
- **ORD-22** — Faced with a messy list, ask why each item is (good/bad/included), then lay the answers on a known structure (a process, ROI tree, EPS tree) to find the message. — pp. 87–89
- **ORD-23** — Only after the message is visible, scrutinize each supporting point and look for what the structure says is missing (e.g., an upside never assessed). — p. 89
- **ORD-24** — For a class grouping, define the shared characteristic precisely and confirm every known item with it is included. — pp. 89–90
- **ORD-25** — Rank degree-ordered items strongest first. Reverse to weakest-first only as a deliberate stylistic choice for emotional effect. — p. 90
- **ORD-26** — Treat the sequence of a degree-ordered list as a claim about relative importance, and make sure it's the claim you intend. — p. 90
- **ORD-27** — If items labeled as a class were really drawn from one process or one structure, say so and use that source's order. — pp. 90–91
- **ORD-28** — To expose an improper class grouping, reword the items in a common form (e.g., as results of the subject) and see whether a process or structure emerges. — p. 91
- **ORD-29** — For a long class list, (1) name the type of each point, (2) group same-type points, (3) find the order the set of groups implies. — p. 92
- **ORD-30** — Choose the order of subgroups by the process relevant to the question the document answers (producing vs reading vs fixing). — p. 92
- **ORD-31** — Don't attempt the summary until the grouping is shown valid and complete via its order. — p. 93

### SUM — Summarizing grouped ideas (`06-summarizing-grouped-ideas.md`)

- **SUM-1** — Never top a grouping with an intellectually blank assertion, i.e., one that only names the kind and number of ideas below ("four risks," "six initiatives," "key findings"). — pp. 94–95
- **SUM-2** — Summarize a deductive grouping by restating the chain with the weight on its final conclusion. — p. 94
- **SUM-3** — Summarize an inductive grouping by stating what the relationship among its ideas implies. The summary must complete the thinking. — p. 94
- **SUM-4** — Before summarizing, confirm the grouping is MECE through a valid order (process, structure, or classification). — pp. 94, 97
- **SUM-5** — Classify each grouping as action ideas (do) or situation ideas (about). Summarize actions by their effect; summarize situations by what their similarity implies. — p. 97
- **SUM-6** — Judge whether actions belong together only against the effect they're meant to produce. Never by how the actions look. — pp. 98–99
- **SUM-7** — Word every action, at every level of the hierarchy, as an end product: what a real person would hold when the step is done. — pp. 99–103
- **SUM-8** — State effects specifically enough to judge whether the steps beneath would achieve them (amount, deadline, or a concrete completion test). — p. 99
- **SUM-9** — Reject action statements you can't visualize: if you can't tell someone who has done it from someone who hasn't, or can't answer "How?" beneath it, it has no intellectual content. — p. 100
- **SUM-10** — Don't phrase action steps as a list of questions to avoid specific wording. Convert them into a sequence of end-product steps toward one deliverable. — pp. 103–104
- **SUM-11** — Separate levels of action: steps done *before* the next are siblings; steps done *so that* the next can be produced go beneath it. Keep each level to five or fewer. — p. 104
- **SUM-12** — Don't organize actions into horizontal category bins such as Tasks / Objectives / Benefits. Organize vertically around end-product actions. — pp. 105–107
- **SUM-13** — To untangle repetitive or category-labeled action lists, pare each item to its barest essence, mark repetitions and levels, then rebuild one pyramid. — pp. 106–107
- **SUM-14** — Summarize actions directly: with a MECE group beneath, name the *immediate* result of performing the actions, phrased as an end product. — p. 107
- **SUM-15** — Test the action summary against the points: reject any summary that a trivial result would satisfy (one extra sale "improves sales"; one paid bill "reduces receivables"). Don't overclaim: steps may put you "in a position to" achieve something without guaranteeing it. — pp. 102, 107–108
- **SUM-16** — Find an action summary by asking "I do these in order to make what happen?" and "If I don't, what won't happen?" — p. 108
- **SUM-17** — When action sentences are complex, extract their essence first: isolate the subjects and ask what they share; isolate how each acts on its subject and ask what's alike; summarize from those commonalities. — p. 108
- **SUM-18** — Phrase the summary so it raises the question the grouping answers (How? Why? What would we do?). — pp. 108, 113, 116
- **SUM-19** — For situation ideas, go past listing: prove the common link that separates these items from all others, then state its wider significance as a new idea. — p. 111
- **SUM-20** — Find the structural similarity by comparing subjects, predicates, and implied judgments: same subjects → compare predicates; same predicates → compare subjects; neither → compare implied judgments. — p. 111
- **SUM-21** — Strip polished, framework-style language down to the bare structure before judging a grouping. — pp. 111–112, 114
- **SUM-22** — Don't accept a grouping just because each point is true. If no clear relationship emerges, the grouping is defective; rethink it. — p. 112
- **SUM-23** — For longer situation lists, find closer links (subgroups) among the similarities, then ask why *these* subgroups and no others to reach the top inference. — p. 113
- **SUM-24** — After stating the summary, use it plus the order of the points to look for missing members. — p. 113
- **SUM-25** — When the implication of a similarity isn't obvious, visualize the source of the relationship (draw it) and read conclusions off the picture. — pp. 114–115
- **SUM-26** — If the conclusions you reach share nothing in common, they relate deductively. Carry the chain to its explicit "therefore." — p. 116
- **SUM-27** — Detect action ideas disguised as situations ("variables," "areas," "issues," "characteristics"). Restate them specifically; if you can picture the effect they jointly produce, switch to action form with the effect on top. — p. 116
- **SUM-28** — Don't assume situation ideas must be in degree order. They can follow time order when they describe stages of an activity. — pp. 116–117
- **SUM-29** — Ask of every grouping "why these ideas and no others?" If the answer is a shared characteristic, the summary is an insight about its significance. If the answer is joint necessity for a goal, the summary is the direct effect. — p. 118
- **SUM-30** — Use each real summary as a springboard: ask what follows from it (deductive comment) and what else is like it (inductive extension). — pp. 95–96

### DEF — Defining the problem (`07-defining-the-problem.md`)

- **DEF-1** — For any long problem-oriented document, work in four stages (define the problem → structure the analysis → conduct it → form the pyramid), and design stages 1–2 so they pre-structure the pyramid. — p. 120
- **DEF-2** — Define a problem as a gap between an Undesired Result (R1) and a Desired Result (R2) arising from a specific Situation, not as a topic or area of concern. — p. 121
- **DEF-3** — Lay out all four elements (Starting Point/Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, R2) before looking for a solution. — pp. 122, 127
- **DEF-4** — Describe the Opening Scene as a concrete, easily visualized structure or process at a specific time and place, pitched at a general business reader (what a friend would need to "see"). — pp. 127–128
- **DEF-5** — Keep the Opening Scene sketch simple and its description short during definition; expand the prose only when writing the introduction. — p. 128
- **DEF-6** — Identify the Disturbing Event as the actual or potential change that threatens the stable situation and surfaces R1, and classify it as external, internal or recently recognized. — p. 129
- **DEF-7** — If you don't know what triggered recognition of the problem (common in proposals), do not invent a Disturbing Event; go directly to R1, which you can always identify as what the reader dislikes about the structure/process. — p. 129
- **DEF-8** — State R1 as briefly as possible; list several R1s if the disturbance produced several. — p. 130
- **DEF-9** — State R2 as specifically and quantifiably as possible, in end-product terms (a number or a definite end state). — p. 130
- **DEF-10** — If R2 can't be stated specifically (or at all), write the general desired state and make "determine the specific R2" the first step of the problem solving. — p. 130
- **DEF-11** — Treat the definition as revisable scaffolding; update the elements as data arrives but keep the relationships between them. — pp. 130–131
- **DEF-12** — Look for the causes of the gap, and thus the solution, in the structure or activities of the Opening Scene. — p. 123
- **DEF-13** — Establish explicitly whether the reader has already acted on the problem (suggested, chosen, implemented or abandoned a solution) before choosing the question. — p. 131
- **DEF-14** — Classify the reader into one of the seven problem situations and use the matching S-C-Q pattern (Exhibit 34). — pp. 131–132
- **DEF-15** — Convert the framework to S-C-Q by reading it left to right and down. The Complication is the most recent development the reader already knows. — pp. 124, 126, 133
- **DEF-16** — In a layered history, fold all earlier rounds (R1-a, R2-a, Solution-a, R1-b…) into the Situation; only the latest development is the Complication. — pp. 124–126
- **DEF-17** — Place alternatives in the Complication, and raise them only if the reader already knows them (the reader identified them and wants them weighed). — p. 135
- **DEF-18** — Never justify a recommendation by knocking down rejected alternatives ("A fails, B fails, so C"); justify it by showing it solves the problem. — p. 135
- **DEF-19** — Match the Key Line's plural noun to the question: "changes" when fixing a system already running, "steps" when creating a new capability. — p. 133
- **DEF-20** — Use the answers to Sequential Analysis questions 1–2 (is there a problem; where) for the introduction, and the answers to 3–5 (why; what could; what should) for the pyramid's points. — p. 122
- **DEF-21** — When reviewing a document, confirm that the introduction mirrors the problem definition and that the pyramid's top answers the question the introduction raises. — p. 127
- **DEF-22** — For a strategy question (Case 6), put the strategy itself at the top; the Key Line either gives steps to achieve it or a deductive argument for it, with the steps under the final box. — p. 136

### ANL — Structuring the analysis (`08-structuring-the-analysis.md`)

- **ANL-1** — Structure the analysis before gathering data; never collect whatever is available and postpone thinking. — pp. 140–142
- **ANL-2** — Derive likely causes (hypotheses) from the structure of the Opening Scene, the situation that produced R1, not from thin air and not from the client's question (which states R2). — pp. 142, 166
- **ANL-3** — Keep diagnostic frameworks (find causes), logic trees (generate or evaluate solutions) and decision trees/PERT charts (plan action) distinct, and use each for its own job. — pp. 142, 150, 167
- **ANL-4** — Build diagnostic frameworks using one or more of the three structuring methods: divide (physical structure), trace cause and effect (financial, task or activity structure), or classify (causes, choice structures). — p. 143
- **ANL-5** — Make each level of a diagnostic tree MECE, especially the top branches. — pp. 143, 148–149
- **ANL-6** — Convert every branch into a yes/no question, and plan data gathering by asking what must be learned to answer each one yes or no. — pp. 145, 149–150, 156
- **ANL-7** — Test candidate causes in order of ease of elimination, and use industry experience to choose where to look first. — pp. 143, 148
- **ANL-8** — For a physical-structure framework, draw the system's units and flows in their actual or intended working state, and read the questions off the picture. — pp. 143–144
- **ANL-9** — Quantify a financial-structure tree (put numbers on nodes) to locate where the shortfall arises before drilling down. — p. 145
- **ANL-10** — In a task structure, phrase every node as a managerial task (Increase…/Reduce…), working down from EPS through the P&L and balance sheet. — pp. 146–147
- **ANL-11** — For an activity structure, start from the undesirable effect, hypothesize the MECE reasons at the next level, and keep asking "why would this happen?" until you reach gatherable facts. — pp. 147–148
- **ANL-12** — In a choice structure, mirror the real sequential process with dual (either/or) forks, and in sequential structures fix upstream (left) weaknesses before downstream (right) ones. — pp. 149–150
- **ANL-13** — If no line of inquiry in a sequential marketing structure reveals the problem's source, go back and recheck the definitions of target market and consumer benefit. — p. 151
- **ANL-14** — Choose which frameworks to build from the Opening Scene of the problem definition, and decide depth using subject-matter knowledge. — p. 153
- **ANL-15** — Gather first only the data needed to draw the diagnostic framework; then form hypotheses; then gather only the data that proves or disproves them. — p. 154
- **ANL-16** — Before fieldwork, identify each data item's source, owner, schedule and cost. — p. 156
- **ANL-17** — Use a logic tree to lay out the MECE set of possible actions (or strategic opportunities), then weigh each option's payoff against its risk to choose the final set. — pp. 156–158
- **ANL-18** — Test any grouped list in a draft (issues, findings, recommendations) by mapping each item onto the analytical tree it should come from; drop items that don't map and look for empty branches. — pp. 159–162
- **ANL-19** — Reserve "issue" for questions answerable yes or no; call topic lists "concerns"; rephrase open questions ("What level is needed?") as yes/no issues ("Is the present level too high?"). — pp. 160, 163
- **ANL-20** — Before judging whether R1 really holds, establish the R2 benchmark (e.g., the "right" inventory level). — p. 160
- **ANL-21** — Don't write an "Issues" section in a proposal; present the analytical process and its end products as the pyramid. — p. 161
- **ANL-22** — Don't treat "devise hypotheses" as a separate step from formulating yes/no issues; derive both from the same analytical tree. — pp. 166–167
- **ANL-23** — Use a completed diagnostic framework to explain the situation to the client in three views: today, what must logically have been happening, and the ideal. — p. 150
- **ANL-24** — Make the analytical structures the backbone of the final pyramid: definition → introduction; confirmed causes → support; chosen actions → Key Line. — p. 167

### ABD — Abduction (`09-abduction.md`)

- **ABD-1** — First classify the problem. Is the outcome unwelcome (structure known → analytical abduction, Ch 8–9) or unexplained (structure non-existent, hidden or contradicted → scientific abduction)? — pp. 210, 212
- **ABD-2** — Identify your reasoning mode from your starting point. Rule-first is deduction (necessary conclusion); Case-first is induction (probable rule); Result-first is abduction (possible case to check). — pp. 211–212
- **ABD-3** — In analytical problem solving, go from the unwelcome outcome (R1), through your knowledge of the structure (Rule), to a specific candidate cause (Case), and then verify it. — p. 211
- **ABD-4** — Treat any abductive conclusion as only *possibly* true until verified. — p. 211
- **ABD-5** — When the structure is unknown, propose candidate structures *before* reasoning toward cases. — p. 212
- **ABD-6** — Generate hypotheses from the components of the problem situation: list them, then form one hypothesis per component or relation (as with weight / distance / time), and use analogy where you can. — pp. 212–214
- **ABD-7** — Support hypothesis generation by getting complete knowledge of the situation and by writing down and challenging every assumption about it. — p. 213
- **ABD-8** — For each hypothesis, derive a consequence that would have to follow automatically, and test that consequence. — p. 213
- **ABD-9** — Design every experiment or test to deliver a clear yes/no that lets you keep or drop the hypothesis; never just vary conditions to "see what happens". — p. 214
- **ABD-10** — After each test, iterate: form narrower or follow-on hypotheses for the remaining possibilities, and repeat. — p. 212
- **ABD-11** — Make each step of the five-step pattern produce a concrete, picturable output (a picture of the gap, of the structure, of the alternatives). Use those pictures to steer the next step and later to anchor the write-up. — p. 214
- **ABD-12** — Expect to cycle among deduction, induction and abduction in complex problems, switching deliberately according to what you know. — p. 212
- **ABD-13** — Aim for a representation of the problem in which the answer is obvious. — p. 215

### PAG — On the page (`10-pyramid-on-the-page.md`)

- **PAG-1** — Choose the presentation form from message length and audience size: short/few → prose memo or report read alone; short/many → dot-dash memo (lap visual) discussed at a table; long/large audience → slides. — pp. 168–169
- **PAG-2** — Make the visual layout reinforce the pyramid's logic, whatever the form. — p. 169
- **PAG-3** — Arrange the document so the reader has the Introduction, Main Point, and all Key Line points within roughly the first 30 seconds of reading. — p. 170
- **PAG-4** — Apply formatting levels to match the pyramid's levels of abstraction: title = apex; sections = Key Line; subsections = next level; numbered paragraphs, then dash points, below. — p. 171, Exh. 53
- **PAG-5** — If each Key Line point has fewer than two supporting paragraphs, just underline (or otherwise highlight) the points in the text; if more than a paragraph or two, list the Key Line points in the introduction and give each its own heading. — pp. 171–172, Exh. 54–55
- **PAG-6** — Indent increasingly subordinate ideas further to the right and give every idea at the same level the same visual form. — p. 174, Exh. 56
- **PAG-7** — Never use only one of any element at a level: no lone section, subsection, numbered paragraph, or dash point; never add a heading for visual relief. — p. 174
- **PAG-8** — Word same-level headings in the same grammatical form within each group; parallelism need not carry across groups with different parents. — p. 174
- **PAG-9** — Keep headings to the essence of the thought. — p. 174
- **PAG-10** — Treat headings as outside the text: the first sentence under a heading must itself announce the new topic, and no sentence may lean on the heading for its meaning; numbered paragraphs are the exception. — p. 175
- **PAG-11** — Introduce every group of headings by stating the point the group supports and the ideas to come; never put a section heading directly under the title or a subsection heading directly under a section heading. — p. 175
- **PAG-12** — Don't overdo headings; use them only where they help the reader hold the subdivisions; often stop at section headings. — p. 175
- **PAG-13** — Make headings state real ideas so the table of contents reads as a précis; drop generic labels such as Introduction, Background, Findings, Conclusions, Recommendations. — pp. 175–176
- **PAG-14** — Under a chapter heading or title, open with paragraphs that state the major idea, put reader and writer in the same place, and preview how the idea will be developed (optionally listing divisions with ¶ points). — p. 173
- **PAG-15** — Develop a numbered paragraph's point in no more than about three paragraphs; underline its first sentence or opening phrase if that helps show the points are alike. — p. 173
- **PAG-16** — Use ¶ points only for lists of fewer than five items or to flag a single paragraph for emphasis; keep ¶ paragraphs short and in block form. Use three centered stars to signal that a long section's wrap-up follows. — p. 173
- **PAG-17** — In the underlined-points format, each point must answer precisely the question its parent provokes, and no more; put all amplification and background in each section's introductory or concluding paragraphs. — p. 177
- **PAG-18** — In the underlined-points format, keep each point to about a dozen words and one subject-predicate. — p. 177
- **PAG-19** — In the underlined-points format, limit the points to the outline of a deductive (at most four points in a chain) or inductive (at most five points) argument. — p. 177
- **PAG-20** — When the document (or a section) runs longer than about seven paragraphs, state its Key Line (or section) points right after the main point. — p. 176, Exh. 57
- **PAG-21** — If you use decimal numbering, use it with hierarchical headings, not instead of them, and cite sections by number plus topic. — pp. 177–178
- **PAG-22** — Number only true divisions of thought; never number introductions, concluding summaries, linking comments, or introductions to subpoints. — p. 179
- **PAG-23** — In short memos, set off a group of supporting points by indenting/numbering them, and write all of them in the same grammatical form. — pp. 179–180
- **PAG-24** — Use only one set of indented groupings per memo. — p. 180
- **PAG-25** — In dot-dash outlines: short direct statements without grace notes or liaisons; one statement per level; parallel construction at a level where possible; every idea explains or supports the one above. Don't use the format unless you will follow these rules. (The two-of-every-level rule is relaxed.) — pp. 180–181
- **PAG-26** — Give every Key Line point a brief introduction of its own; in long documents pause at the start or end of each major grouping to show where the reader has been and is going. — p. 182
- **PAG-27** — Make transitions relate what sections *say* (their ideas), not what they *do*; look back and forward at once; never write "this chapter covered X, the next covers Y". — p. 182
- **PAG-28** — When a Key Line section needs to be led into, tell a mini Situation–Complication–Question story, scoped to where the reader now stands, whose answer is that section's point; include only facts the reader knows or will accept. — pp. 182–183, Exh. 59
- **PAG-29** — Otherwise link sections by referencing backward: pick up a key word, phrase, or idea from the preceding part in the new part's opening sentence. — pp. 183–185, Exh. 60
- **PAG-30** — After a backward-reference link, still preview the ideas the section contains and show how they back up its point. — p. 185
- **PAG-31** — End a very long or complex section with a full summary that restates its principal content and tone, drawn from the pyramid. — p. 185
- **PAG-32** — Default to no concluding paragraph. If you add one, don't merely restate; put the message's significance in perspective and leave the reader wanting to act, via a philosophical insight and/or a prescription for immediate action, pitched to subject and reader. — pp. 185–187
- **PAG-33** — When the reader is likely to act on a recommendation, add a Next Steps section listing only immediate, self-evident actions; any action the reader might question goes in the body, fitted into the logic. — p. 187
- **PAG-34** — Use transition devices gracefully and only where needed. — pp. 187–188

### SCR — On screen (`11-pyramid-on-screen.md`)

- **SCR-1** — Treat a presentation as a show for a live audience, not a report in slide form: plan the script, storyboard, visuals, timing, and pacing. — pp. 189–190
- **SCR-2** — Never put a list on a slide where a group of related ideas summarized by an insight belongs, and never show seven items. — p. 190
- **SCR-3** — Don't read slide text aloud word for word, and don't say something different from what the slide says. — p. 190
- **SCR-4** — Deliver at least the basic minimum: text slides holding only the key ideas, grouped, summarized, and brief; clear supporting exhibits; an underlying storyboard and script. — p. 191
- **SCR-5** — Aim for about 90% exhibits and 10% text slides; use text slides only to clarify the presentation's structure or to emphasize key thought groups (conclusions, recommendations, next steps); use exhibits for relationships words can't show. — p. 191
- **SCR-6** — Make yourself, not the slides, the star: put transitions and introductory remarks in what you say, not on the screen. — pp. 191–192
- **SCR-7** — If the deck must also work as a handout, pair each slide with its script on the facing page, with the script written in outline form and transitions omitted. Don't bloat slides to make them self-explanatory. — p. 192
- **SCR-8** — Confine text slides to the pyramid's major points, in the pattern Situation → Complication → Main Point + Key Line → each Key Line point with its supports → each support point as a sentence over a graphic. — pp. 192–193, Exh. 61
- **SCR-9** — Present and support one idea per slide; the only exception is a slide enumerating a set of points (a summary, or a preview of points to come). — p. 193
- **SCR-10** — Title every slide with a statement, not a caption. — p. 194
- **SCR-11** — Keep text slides to about 6 lines or ~30 words; split if more. — p. 194
