# Pyramid Fundamentals: Why a Pyramid, and the Substructures Inside It

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Preface (unnumbered pages); Introduction to Part One "Logic in Writing" (unnumbered); Ch. 1 "Why a Pyramid Structure?", book pp. 1–11; Ch. 2 "The Substructures Within the Pyramid", book pp. 12–20. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `PYR`.

## When a skill needs this

- Reviewing a memo, email, report or deck whose main point is buried, missing, or arrives only at the end.
- Explaining *why* top-down ordering matters (the reader-cognition argument), e.g. when a user objects that "the reader needs the background first".
- Auditing whether a set of points is a legitimate grouping: does the parent summarize the children, are the children one kind of thing, is their order defensible?
- Deciding what belongs on the line beneath a point (the question/answer dialogue) and whether a grouping is deductive or inductive.
- Locating the reader's question through Situation–Complication–Question–Answer before any drafting starts.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Pyramid (of ideas) | A hierarchy of grouped ideas under one governing thought; each level summarizes the level beneath. |
| Single thought / top point / main point | The one idea the whole document exists to explain or defend; it is the Answer to the reader's Question. |
| Key Line | The row of ideas immediately under the top point; jointly they answer the question the top point provokes. |
| Leg | One branch running from a Key Line point down to its finest supporting detail. |
| Level of abstraction | How general an idea is; a summary sits exactly one level above what it summarizes. |
| Grouping | A set of sibling ideas sitting under one summarizing idea. |
| Idea (Minto's definition) | A statement that is news to the reader and so prompts a question. |
| Magical number seven | G. A. Miller's finding that short-term memory holds roughly seven items (range about five to nine). |
| Top-down ordering | Delivering a summarizing idea before the ideas it summarizes. |
| Bottom-up thinking | Forming summaries by grouping lower-level ideas (sentences → paragraphs → sections → document). |
| Vertical relationship | Point-to-subpoint link: a statement provokes a question, the next line down answers it. |
| Question/answer dialogue | The chain of reader questions and writer answers that the vertical relationship creates. |
| Horizontal relationship | The logical link among siblings — deductive or inductive, never a blend. |
| Deductive grouping | Argument steps: an assertion, a remark about its subject or predicate, then a "therefore". |
| Inductive grouping | Ideas alike enough to share one plural noun; the parent is an inference about what they share. |
| Plural-noun test | Quick check that one plural noun (reasons, steps, problems...) labels every idea in a grouping. |
| Four logical orders | The only possible sequences within a grouping: deductive, chronological, structural, comparative. |
| Introductory (narrative) flow | The introduction told as a story: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. |
| Situation (S) | The when-and-where starting point the reader already knows and accepts. |
| Complication (C) | A development inside the Situation that gives rise (or would give rise) to the reader's Question. |
| Question (Q) | The question the reader already has — or would have on brief reflection — that the document answers. |
| Answer (A) | The document's top point; stating it provokes the new question the Key Line answers. |

## Concepts

### Preface: the core claim

- Minto's founding claim (first published as booklets in 1973): you can spot clear writing because its ideas form a clean pyramid; muddled writing always strays from that shape.
- Because pyramid ideas can relate in only a few logical directions — up, down, sideways — rules about those relationships can be written down. So clear writing comes from arranging your thinking as a pyramid and testing it against those rules **before** drafting.
- How she got there: books on writing at the time dealt with sentences and paragraphs, not with organizing the thinking behind them. The usual advice ("be logical", "make a logical outline") offered no way to tell a sound outline from an unsound one. Answering that question led her to the pyramid.
- Scope: any document meant to convey someone's thinking clearly. (Illustrated with a short note about moving a meeting — see Worked examples.)
- Reach beyond writing: the pyramid also extends back into how problems are defined and analysed, and forward into how the entire writing process is managed. The book's four parts follow this:
  - Part One, Logic in Writing — the principle itself and how to build a simple pyramid; by itself enough to handle simple documents.
  - Part Two, Logic in Thinking — scrutinizing each point so it states the actual insight carried by the ideas grouped under it; Minto calls this discipline "Hard-Headed Thinking" (Chs 6–7).
  - Part Three (Logic in Problem Solving): analytical frameworks that pre-shape an analysis so it slots into a pyramid; written mainly for consultants and analysts whose conclusions drive someone else's action.
  - Part Four, Logic in Presentation — keeping the structure visible when it becomes prose or slides.
  - Appendices: how analytical problem solving differs from scientific; common introduction patterns; a full outline of the book.
- Promised payoff of thinking first and writing second: less time to a final draft, more clarity, fewer words. Minto admits the method still demands real discipline.

### Introduction to Part One: Logic in Writing

- Professionals generally dislike writing and are often told to make it "clearer"; most assume that means simpler, more direct sentences.
- Minto separates two sources of unclear writing:
  1. **Style** — long sentences, jargon or abstraction, clumsy paragraphs. A genuine weakness, but hard to change once schooling ends; like acquiring typing skill, it takes drills that busy professionals don't have time for.
  2. **Structure** — the sequence in which sentences appear, independent of how good each sentence is. This cause is both more widespread and much easier to repair.
- The likely diagnosis for any unclear document: its sequence of ideas fights the way readers' minds take in information.
- Two premises yield the pyramid: (a) readers find it easiest to receive major, abstract ideas before minor, supporting ones; (b) major ideas are always derived from minor ones. Combined, the ideal form is clusters of ideas nested under one overarching thought.
- Two relationships hold inside that shape: **vertical** (a point at any level sums up the ideas beneath it) and **horizontal** (siblings belong together because jointly they make a logical case).
- Delivery: start at the apex and work down each leg. Each major statement makes readers wonder what justifies it; the level beneath supplies that; the exchange repeats until every idea has been delivered. Minto believes this ask-then-answer reflex is universal, whatever the reader's nationality.
- A related fact: people can't pin down what they think until they express it (aloud or on paper). The structure that sharpens your own thinking turns out to be the same pyramid — so writers who force their thinking into one find that clear, direct sentences follow easily.
- What Part One covers: why the pyramid suits readers; how its substructures interact; how to work out which ideas a document needs and how they connect; the logic of introductions; and the difference between deductive and inductive argument. Later parts use the pyramid rules to confirm that points are valid, coherent and complete, and to surface ideas the writer hasn't yet articulated.

### Ch. 1 opening: the reader's job (p. 1)

- Even about two single-spaced pages contain on the order of 100 sentences; readers must take in, digest, connect and retain them all. That load is always lighter when the material arrives top-first as a pyramid.
- Minto rests this on three observations about the mind:
  1. To understand information it automatically sorts it into pyramid-shaped groups.
  2. Any set of ideas is easier to understand if it arrives already sorted that way.
  3. So documents should be deliberately built as pyramids of ideas.

### Sorting into Pyramids (pp. 2–3)

- Minds impose order on what they perceive: items encountered together are presumed to belong together, and a pattern gets imposed (as when ancient observers saw figures, not scattered points, in the night sky).
- Grouping follows "common fate" — shared traits or physical closeness. Six unevenly spaced dots are read as two trios because some gaps are narrower.
- Logical units pay off hugely in recall: in a memory exercise using unrelated noun pairs (from Köhler's *Gestalt Psychology*), picturing a scene that links each pair lets most people retrieve every partner word.
- The same happens with ideas: readers presume that ideas arriving in sequence belong together and try to find a pattern. That pattern is always pyramidal, because only a pyramid meets two needs of the mind — capping the number of items at about seven, and making the relationship between them explicit.

### The Magical Number Seven (pp. 3–4)

- Illustration: someone stepping out for a newspaper is asked, item by item while getting a coat and heading to the car, to pick up nine groceries. Most return with the paper and the first item only.
- Miller's research: short-term memory tops out around seven items — nine for some people, five for others (Minto places herself at five). Three is handy; one is easiest of all.
- When the count climbs past four or five, the mind starts sorting items into logical categories so they can be retained — in this case, probably by supermarket aisle.
- Categorize the same nine items as you read them and most people recall all nine. Drawn out, the result is three small pyramids: dairy (milk, eggs, butter, sour cream), fruit (grapes, oranges, apples), vegetables (potatoes, carrots).

### The Need to State the Logic (p. 4)

- Sorting alone doesn't help unless the logic of each cluster is named. Nine items split into four, three and two is still nine items. The point is to climb **above** them to three.
- What gets remembered is then three categories, not nine items — thinking has moved up a level of abstraction, and each higher-level idea brings its members to mind. Because the links are natural rather than invented (unlike the word-pair exercise), they are easy to keep.
- Grouping-and-summarizing appears to underlie every mental process — thinking, remembering, solving problems — so a person's knowledge can be pictured as an enormous web of interlocking pyramids. To communicate is to fit your message into the reader's existing pyramids.
- The practical snag: the writer can "see" the groupings, but can only hand them over one item at a time. The efficient way to make the reader see them too is to name each category before its items — that is, to order top-down.

### Ordering from the Top Down (pp. 5–7)

- Minto's most emphatic point in the book: summary first, then the ideas it summarizes. She ranks sequencing as the most important thing a writer controls for clarity, and says it can't be stressed too much.
- The mechanism:
  - Readers process one sentence at a time and presume adjacent ideas belong together.
  - Without advance notice of the relationship, readers hunt for one themselves.
  - Readers differ, so their reading seldom matches the writer's; often they find no connection at all. Even a reader who happens to think exactly like the writer works harder than necessary, because they must supply what was never said.
- **Expectancy.** A reader who receives a statement treats it as the first member of a group still to come and guesses its purpose. That guess saves effort — each later idea is checked only for what it shares with earlier ones — but a wrong guess leads them astray (see the Zurich example).
- **Announce the frame up front.** Readers search for a connecting structure no matter what; left to themselves they land on the wrong one or on none at all — wasted effort for writer and reader alike.
- Opening with an assertion does not by itself make a passage top-down. In the equal-pay example the author believed the piece began at the top, yet the ideas that follow have no visible link to it or to one another; the reader searches, finds nothing, and quits.
- **The reader's energy budget.** Attention is a fixed budget split three ways: decoding words, seeing how ideas relate, and grasping significance. Only the third is where a writer wants readers' effort to go; good ordering keeps the first two small. A sequence that sends readers back and forth to make connections is discourteous, and most readers won't comply.
- The chain of argument in brief: readers group and summarize in order to remember; they understand faster when material is already grouped, summarized and ordered top-down; so the clearest documents present top-down pyramids — **even though the thinking behind them was built bottom-up.**

### Thinking from the Bottom Up (pp. 8–11)

- Writers build their larger ideas from the bottom: single-idea sentences are clustered into paragraphs, paragraphs into sections, sections into the whole document, which is then represented by one thought at the apex.
- Membership criterion for a paragraph: every sentence must be needed to explain or defend the paragraph's single idea, and that idea in turn summarizes them. A paragraph of five finance sentences plus one about tennis fails the criterion — no single summary sentence could connect them.
- Writing that summary lifts you one level of abstraction; the paragraph can now be handled as a single point instead of six. The same move turns a few paragraphs into a section (each needed to support the section's idea, which summarizes them) and sections into the document.
- Grouping and summarizing continue until no further relationships can be formed, so every document ends up resting on **one** thought — the summary of its last grouping. That should be the writer's main message; everything beneath, if correctly built, explains or supports it in ever finer detail.
- **Exhibit 1 (p. 9)** — the canonical shape: a single apex box; three Key Line boxes under it; each Key Line box with two to four children, each of which has two to four children of its own.
- Soundness can be checked **before** writing, by testing whether the ideas relate in ways that allow pyramid groupings. Three rules apply:
  1. An idea at any level must summarize the ideas grouped beneath it.
  2. The ideas within any grouping must all be of one kind.
  3. The ideas within any grouping must follow a logical order.
- **Rule 1, why.** The central act of thinking and writing is abstraction — deriving a fresh idea from a set of grouped ones (a paragraph's point from its sentences, a section's point from its paragraphs' points). That only works if the group was correctly formed to begin with, which is what rules 2 and 3 guarantee.
- **Rule 2, why.** Rising exactly one level above a group requires the members to be logically alike. Apples and pears go up one level to fruit; tables and chairs to furniture. Apples with chairs can't use that next level (fruit and furniture already occupy it) and must climb to something like "objects", which is too vague to reveal the logic. Writing needs the idea the grouping directly implies, so its members must share a logical category:
  - a group that opens with a reason for some action must consist only of reasons for that same action;
  - a group that opens with a step must contain only steps of that same process;
  - a group that opens with a company problem must contain only related problems.
  - Shortcut: one **plural noun** (recommendations, reasons, problems, changes to make...) must cleanly label the group. Any subject matter is allowed; each group just has to hold a single kind. (Method in detail: Part Two, Chs 6–7.)
- **Rule 3, why.** Each member's position must be justified — there has to be a reason the second item cannot trade places with the first or third. Minto recognizes only four orders:
  - deductive — the sequence of an argument (major premise, minor premise, conclusion);
  - chronological — the sequence of time (first, second, third);
  - structural — the sequence of an existing structure (e.g. a list of cities along a route);
  - comparative — the sequence of importance or degree (most important first, and so on).
  - Which order applies depends on how the group was produced: by deduction → argument order; by tracing cause and effect → time order; by commenting on an existing structure → that structure's order; by classifying → order of importance. In Minto's view the mind has only these four analytical operations (deducing, tracing causes and effects, breaking a whole into parts, classifying), so only these four orders exist. (Detail: Ch. 6.)
- The working principle: fit ideas into the pyramid and test them against the rules before writing. A violated rule points to faulty thinking, ideas not yet developed, or a relationship not yet expressed in a form readers can grasp at once. Fix it at this stage and you spare yourself extensive rewriting.

### Ch. 2 opening: why the substructures matter (p. 12)

- A clear document locks its ideas into a tight set of relationships that form a single pyramid, then delivers them from the apex down, one leg at a time.
- Writers who already know their ideas can build the pyramid fairly easily, because the rules are so specific. Most writers, though, start with only a vague sense of what they think — and that's normal: thought isn't precise until it has been expressed, and a first expression is usually rougher than a later one.
- So ideas have to be **found** before they can be arranged. The pyramid supplies three substructures that accelerate the search:
  1. how a point connects to the subpoints beneath it (vertical);
  2. how the subpoints in a set connect to one another (horizontal);
  3. how the introduction's story unfolds (narrative flow).
- Ch. 3 (→ `02-building-the-pyramid.md`) applies them to finding, sorting and arranging ideas.

### The Vertical Relationship (pp. 13–16)

- **The hidden dimension.** Linear prose hides the fact that ideas sit at different levels of abstraction. Every idea under the apex therefore has two kinds of neighbour: those above and below it (vertical) and those beside it (horizontal).
- The vertical link is a strong attention device: it creates a question/answer exchange that draws readers through the reasoning, because they can't help responding logically to each statement.
- **What a box holds.** Minto's definition of an idea: any statement that tells readers something new to them and therefore prompts a question. Nobody reads to learn what they already know, so communicating your thinking always means informing readers of things they don't yet know.
- **The exchange.** A new statement automatically triggers a logical question — Why? How? On what grounds? The writer must answer it on the line below, horizontally. That answer is itself new information, so it prompts further questions, answered another line down.
- **Stopping point.** Keep going until readers would have no logical questions left. They may still disagree, but they will have followed the reasoning — which is all a writer can reasonably expect. Then climb back to the Key Line and continue answering the question the apex raised.
- **Attention rule.** Never prompt a question you aren't ready to answer, and never answer one that hasn't yet been prompted. Classic violation: an "Our Assumptions" section placed ahead of the main points answers questions readers haven't had the chance to form, so the material will need repeating — or rereading — later, where it actually matters.
- Built this way, a pyramid naturally delivers each piece of information at the moment readers need it.
- Exhibit 2 (Chesterton on pigs — the exchange in pure form, without business logic to distract) and Exhibit 3 (a franchise-purchase memo — the business version): see Worked examples.
- Recap: the pyramid makes the vertical question/answer link visible while the writer is still thinking. Every point provokes a question, and the line beneath must answer it.

### The Horizontal Relationship (pp. 17–18)

- The points on the line below must do more than respond to the question above — they must respond **logically**, as a clean deductive argument or a clean inductive one. A grouping can't be both, and no third kind of logical relationship exists.
- **Deductive grouping** — an argument built in steps:
  1. an assertion about some existing state of affairs;
  2. a second assertion that refers back to the first one's **subject or predicate**;
  3. what follows from both being true together.
  - Minto illustrates with the textbook mortality syllogism (a rule about all members of a class → an individual belongs to the class → so the rule applies to the individual). Same shape, invented: *Every contract over $50k needs legal review → This vendor contract is $80k → So this contract needs legal review.*
  - Moving up a level: summarize the whole argument, putting the weight on its conclusion (*Because this contract exceeds $50k, it needs legal review*).
- **Inductive grouping** — ideas linked simply because one plural noun fits them all (reasons for, reasons against, steps, problems...).
  - Minto's illustration: three different nations' tanks reported massing at one country's border; what they share is that each is a hostile military move against that country; the inference is that an invasion is imminent. Same shape, invented: *Our largest client's procurement team has asked for our cost breakdown; their CFO has asked for references; their CTO has requested a security audit → They are evaluating us for a renewal decision.*
  - Moving up a level: infer from what the items have in common.
- What the choice commits you to: picking deduction means the second point must refer back to the first one's subject or predicate and the last must be a "therefore" drawn from them; picking induction means the points must be logically alike and nameable by a plural noun.
- Knowing this, you could in principle start a pyramid from any single idea and add others wherever they are needed — above, below or alongside. One piece is still missing: the opening question the document must answer, which comes from tracing the introduction's narrative. (Full treatment of both forms: Ch. 5 → `04-deduction-and-induction.md`.)

### The Introductory Flow (pp. 18–20)

- **Relevance test for the opening statement:** it must directly answer a question you have confirmed the reader already holds; nothing else guarantees the exchange will engage them.
- **Need drives questions.** Writing exists to supply new information, but readers seek it only when their circumstances require it: no need, no question — and a real question signals a real need.
- So point the document at a question the reader already has, or would have after a moment's thought about their own circumstances. The introduction surfaces that question by recounting how it came about.
- That account is a story, so it should follow the classic shape of a story:
  - **Situation** — sets the scene in time and place;
  - **Complication** — something happens within that scene;
  - **Question** — which leads (or would lead) the reader to ask something;
  - **Answer** — which the document supplies.
- What S-C-Q-A achieves:
  - writer and reader share a common starting point before the reasoning begins;
  - the apex point gets a sharp focus, which in turn gives a test of whether the right message is being delivered as directly as possible.
- Board-of-directors memo example: see Worked examples.
- Recap: the introduction retells, as a story, what readers already know or can fairly be assumed to know, and in doing so reminds them of their question. The story lays out the Situation, the Complication that arose in it, and the Question it triggered; the document then gives the Answer (the apex point). Stating that Answer prompts a fresh question, which the line below answers — this is how the introduction hands over to the Key Line.
- **How the three substructures help find ideas:**
  - The **vertical** link tells you what the ideas below must accomplish: answer the question above them.
  - The **horizontal** link lets you check that the ideas you've assembled do so logically (a proper deductive or inductive argument).
  - The reader's **opening question** — the most important of the three — ensures relevance: every idea included is there only because it helps answer that question.

## Rules

- **PYR-1** — When writing is unclear, fix the order of ideas before polishing sentences. *Why:* structure is the more common cause of unclarity and far easier to fix than style; the rewritten TTW memo (p. 30) reads clearly with nearly unchanged wording. *Check:* before suggesting any sentence-level edit, confirm the draft has a single top point delivered first and groupings that obey PYR-7 to PYR-9. (Part One intro; p. 30)
- **PYR-2** — Shape the thinking into a pyramid and test it against the rules before writing prose. *Why:* the rules can be checked in advance, and fixing structure early prevents heavy rewriting. *Check:* an outline (top point, Key Line, supports) exists and has been checked against PYR-7 to PYR-9 before drafting. (Preface; p. 11)
- **PYR-3** — Put the summarizing idea ahead of the ideas it summarizes, at every level of the document. *Why:* readers take ideas one at a time and invent their own grouping if not told; they usually guess wrong or find none, and even a right guess costs effort. *Check:* for every heading, paragraph and group, the first sentence states the point the rest supports; no group ends with its "so what". (pp. 5–7)
- **PYR-4** — Never present a grouping without stating the idea that unites it. *Why:* splitting items into groups doesn't lighten the load unless readers can rise above them to the summary, which then calls up the items. *Check:* every list or set of paragraphs has an explicit parent statement; remove the children and the parent still says something specific. (p. 4)
- **PYR-5** — Keep each grouping within short-term memory limits (no more than about seven; fewer is easier; three is handy); once a set passes four or five items, sort it into categories under summaries. *Why:* memory holds about seven items and starts categorizing around four or five. *Check:* count children under each node; above ~5, look for natural sub-categories. (pp. 3–4)
- **PYR-6** — Make the whole document rest on exactly one top thought, with every idea below explaining or supporting it. *Why:* grouping-and-summarizing continues until no relationships remain, so a sound document has one apex. *Check:* the document's point fits in one sentence; each Key Line point answers the question that sentence raises. (pp. 8–9)
- **PYR-7** — Make every idea at every level a summary of the ideas grouped directly beneath it. *Why:* abstracting a new idea from grouped ideas is the core act of thinking and writing. *Check:* read each parent, then its children: do the children, together, establish exactly that parent? (pp. 9–10)
- **PYR-8** — Make every idea within a grouping the same kind of idea — nameable by one plural noun. *Why:* only logically alike ideas let you rise exactly one level; mixed kinds force a uselessly broad label ("things"). *Check:* name the plural noun (reasons, steps, problems, recommendations, changes); any child that doesn't fit is a misfit to move or remove. (p. 10)
- **PYR-9** — Order the ideas in each grouping so every position is justified, using one of the four orders — deductive, chronological, structural, comparative — matched to how the grouping was formed. *Why:* these four mirror the only analytical operations the mind has (deducing, tracing cause and effect, dividing a whole, classifying). *Check:* name the order in use and explain why item 2 can't swap with item 1 or 3. (pp. 10–11)
- **PYR-10** — Treat any rule violation as a signal about the thinking, not the wording. *Why:* a broken rule means flawed thinking, undeveloped ideas, or relationships not yet made instantly clear. *Check:* when PYR-6 to PYR-9 fail, go back to the content (what is the real point? what kind of ideas are these?) before rewording. (p. 11)
- **PYR-11** — Put only ideas in the pyramid's boxes: statements that are news to the reader and therefore provoke a question. *Why:* people read to learn what they don't know; a statement that provokes no question is either already known (it belongs in the introduction) or unnecessary. *Check:* for each point, write the question it provokes (Why? How? Which ones? On what grounds?); if there is none, relocate or cut it. (p. 14)
- **PYR-12** — Answer each point's question on the line directly beneath it, and keep descending until the reader would have no logical question left; then return to the Key Line. *Why:* the vertical exchange draws readers through the reasoning and lets them follow it even when they disagree. *Check:* for each parent, the children read as a direct answer to its question; no leg stops while an obvious question remains open. (p. 14)
- **PYR-13** — Never provoke a question you aren't ready to answer, and never answer one the reader hasn't yet had a chance to ask. *Why:* premature answers (e.g. an "Our Assumptions", "Background" or "Methodology" section ahead of the main points) must be repeated or reread later; open questions lose attention. *Check:* for each section, find the point above it whose question it answers; a section answering nothing yet asked is misplaced. (p. 14)
- **PYR-14** — Make each grouping either a deductive argument or an inductive one — never a mixture. *Why:* those are the only two logical relationships a grouping can have, and each has its own way of moving up a level. *Check:* classify every grouping as D or I (see DED-15); a grouping that is neither isn't logically related. (p. 17)
- **PYR-15** — In a deductive grouping, make the second point refer back to the first one's subject or predicate and the last point draw the "therefore"; summarize it with the weight on the conclusion. *Why:* that is what makes deductive points derive from one another. *Check:* the parent reads like "Because [premise], [conclusion]". (p. 17)
- **PYR-16** — In an inductive grouping, make all points share one plural noun and make the parent an inference about what they share. *Why:* the summary of an inductive group comes from judging what its members have in common. *Check:* name the plural noun; the parent states something about that common quality. (p. 17)
- **PYR-17** — Aim the top point at a question the reader already has (or would have, on reflecting about their circumstances). *Why:* the exchange engages only if its opening statement is relevant, and readers want information only when they need it. *Check:* you can name the reader and write their question in one line; the top point answers it directly. (p. 18)
- **PYR-18** — Build the introduction as a story — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer — so writer and reader start from a common point and the top point has a clear focus. *Why:* tracing how the question arose identifies it reliably and gives a test for the top point. *Check:* label the introduction's sentences S, C, (Q, often implicit), A; the Complication must naturally raise the Question, and the Answer must be the top point. (pp. 18–19)
- **PYR-19** — Limit the introduction to what readers already know or can fairly be assumed to know, and let the stated Answer raise the question the Key Line answers. *Why:* the introduction's job is to remind readers of their question, not to inform them; the Answer is the hinge to the body. *Check:* nothing in S or C would surprise the reader; the question the Answer raises matches the Key Line's plural noun. (p. 19; detail in BLD-18, BLD-19)
- **PYR-20** — Include an idea only if it helps answer the reader's opening question. *Why:* the opening question is the main guarantee of relevance. *Check:* for each point, trace a chain of Q/A links up to the top point; points with no chain are cut. (p. 20)

## Procedures

### Procedure 1 — Audit an existing pyramid (or outline) against the three rules

1. Write the top point as one sentence. If you can't, stop: the document has no single thought (PYR-6). Go to `02-building-the-pyramid.md` (top-down or bottom-up procedure).
2. For each node, from the top down:
   1. List its children.
   2. **Summary test (PYR-7):** do the children, taken together, establish exactly this parent — no more, no less? If the parent claims more than the children support, narrow the parent or add support; if the children say more than the parent, sharpen the parent.
   3. **Same-kind test (PYR-8):** name one plural noun covering every child. If you can't, find the misfit(s) and move them to the grouping where they belong or drop them.
   4. **Form test (PYR-14):** decide whether the grouping is deductive (second point refers back to the first one's subject/predicate; last is a "therefore") or inductive (shared plural noun). If neither, the grouping isn't logically related.
   5. **Order test (PYR-9):** name which of the four orders the children follow and why each sits in its slot.
   6. **Count test (PYR-5):** if more than ~5 children, look for sub-categories.
3. Any failure → revisit the thinking (PYR-10) before rewording.

### Procedure 2 — Run the vertical question/answer dialogue on a draft

1. Take the top point. Write the single question it provokes in the reader (usually Why?, How?, or Which ones?).
2. Check that the Key Line, read as a set, answers exactly that question.
3. For each Key Line point, write the question it provokes; check that its children answer it.
4. Continue down each leg until a point provokes no further logical question for this reader. That is where the leg ends.
5. Flag: (a) any point whose question goes unanswered below it (gap); (b) any material answering a question no point above has raised (premature answer — PYR-13), such as up-front "Assumptions", "Background" or "Methodology" sections.
6. After finishing a leg, return to the Key Line and repeat for the next point.

### Procedure 3 — Find the reader's opening question (brief form)

1. Name the reader.
2. Situation: state something about the subject that the reader already knows and accepts.
3. Complication: state what happened within that Situation to create a need — something went wrong, a problem appeared, a discrepancy surfaced.
4. Question: the question the Complication raises for the reader.
5. Answer: your top point. Confirm it answers that Question directly.
6. Write down the new question the Answer raises; the Key Line must answer it.
(Full step-by-step method with decision points: `02-building-the-pyramid.md`.)

### Procedure 4 — Convert a discovery-order draft to top-down order

1. Strip the draft to its points (one line each), in their existing order.
2. Identify what the writer is ultimately requesting or concluding (often the last line, e.g. a closing "Does that work for you?").
3. Make that the top point and phrase it as a full statement or request.
4. Regroup the remaining points as answers to the question the top point raises; label the grouping with a plural noun.
5. Rewrite: top point first, then the supporting points.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Main point appears only in the last paragraph, or as a closing question | Ideas delivered in the order the writer discovered them (bottom-up) | Move the summary to the top (PYR-3, PYR-6); Procedure 4 |
| Reader can't say what the document is about after the first paragraph | No single top point, or top point not stated first | PYR-6, PYR-3 |
| A list of 8–12 bullets with no sub-structure | Grouping exceeds short-term memory; logic not stated | Regroup under summaries (PYR-5, PYR-4) |
| Items grouped, but the group has no stated point (or a heading like "Findings", "Issues", "Other") | Logic of the grouping not stated | Write the summarizing idea (PYR-4, PYR-7) |
| A grouping mixes reasons, steps and problems | Children are not the same kind | Plural-noun test; move misfits (PYR-8) |
| The only label that fits a grouping is very broad ("things to consider", "points") | Mixed-kind grouping, forced up too many levels | PYR-8; regroup |
| No reason why items appear in their order; reordering changes nothing | Grouping not logically ordered | Pick deductive/chronological/structural/comparative (PYR-9) |
| "Our Assumptions", "Background", "Methodology" section ahead of the main points | Answering questions before they are raised | Move material to where a point raises its question (PYR-13, PYR-12) |
| A claim with no support beneath it, or support that answers a different question | Vertical Q/A broken | PYR-12 |
| A grouping that is half argument steps, half parallel examples | Deductive and inductive mixed | PYR-14; split or recast (see `04-deduction-and-induction.md`) |
| Opening paragraph is "The purpose of this memo is to discuss..." followed by a topic list | No introductory story; reader's question not identified | Rebuild as S-C-Q-A (PYR-17, PYR-18) |
| Introduction contains points the reader would dispute or has never heard | Introduction is informing, not reminding | PYR-19 (see BLD-18, BLD-19) |
| Content true but unconnected to the reader's question | Relevance not tested against the opening question | PYR-20 |
| Writer polishes sentences but reviewers still find it unclear | Style being fixed instead of structure | PYR-1 |

## Templates and patterns

**Generic pyramid (Exhibit 1 shape)**

```
[Top point — one sentence answering the reader's Question]
  └─ (question the top point raises: Why? / How? / Which?)
     Key Line (one plural noun, logically ordered):
     ├─ Point 1
     │    └─ (question Point 1 raises)
     │       ├─ Support 1.1
     │       ├─ Support 1.2
     │       └─ Support 1.3
     ├─ Point 2
     │    ├─ Support 2.1
     │    └─ Support 2.2
     └─ Point 3
          ├─ Support 3.1
          └─ Support 3.2
```

**Introduction-to-Key-Line hinge**

```
S: [What the reader knows and accepts about the subject]
C: [What happened in that situation that creates a need]
Q: [The question this raises — often left implicit in the text]
A: [Top point]
   → New question raised by A: [Why? / How?]
   → Key Line answers it: [plural noun] 1, 2, 3
```

**Deductive grouping (horizontal)**

```
[Because <statement 2>, <conclusion>]          ← parent leans on the final point
  Assertion about the world  →  Remark on its subject or predicate  →  Therefore <implication>
```

**Inductive grouping (horizontal)**

```
[Inference about what the items share]
  ├─ Item 1  ┐
  ├─ Item 2  ├─ all describable by one plural noun: <noun>
  └─ Item 3  ┘
```

**Grouping audit card**

```
Parent: ______________________
Question parent raises: ______
Children's plural noun: ______
Form: deductive / inductive
Order: deductive / chronological / structural / comparative — reason: ______
Child count: __ (regroup if > ~5)
```

## Worked examples

**Preface example — moving a meeting (Preface).** Before: a note relaying facts in the order the writer collected them — each of three attendees' constraints (one can't do today; one can't do tomorrow before mid-morning; one is away until late tomorrow) and the room's availability (taken tomorrow, open Thursday) — ending with a question asking whether Thursday at 11:00 is acceptable. Drawn as three unconnected boxes: people's availability / room availability / the closing question. After:

```
Move today's meeting to Thursday at 11:00
├─ Better for two of the attendees
├─ Lets the travelling attendee take part
└─ Only other day this week the room is open
```

Lesson: the closing question was really the top point; the scattered facts become the reasons beneath it.

**Shopping list (pp. 3–4).** Before: nine groceries named one at a time; recall falls to one or two. After:

```
(Groceries)
├─ Dairy: milk, eggs, butter, sour cream
├─ Fruit: grapes, oranges, apples
└─ Vegetables: potatoes, carrots
```

Lesson: categorize once past four or five items, and hold the three categories rather than nine items.

**Zurich and facial hair (pp. 5–6).** Before: over a drink, the speaker mentions, one city at a time, how much facial hair she noticed among people in a conservative European city, then among New York office workers, then that it has long been common in London. The listener tries a series of wrong theories (the city is loosening up; cities are being compared; she disapproves; London is ahead) and finally commits to a wrong one. After:

```
Facial hair has become broadly accepted in business life
├─ Zurich
├─ New York
└─ London
```

Lesson: give the frame first; otherwise the reader's expectancy steers them to an unintended relationship.

**Equal pay article (pp. 6–7).** Before: five ideas from an article's opening — a claim that equal pay might leave women relatively worse off (a wider earnings gap); two competing definitions of equal pay; two possible consequences of applying either definition. No after is given. Lesson: although the author believed the piece opened with its top point, nothing shows how the ideas connect, so the reader gives up — opening with a claim isn't enough if the following points don't visibly support it.

**Finance and tennis (p. 8).** Five sentences on finance plus one on tennis can't make a paragraph, because no single summary sentence can express how they relate. Lesson: a paragraph exists because every sentence in it is needed to support one summarizing idea.

**Apples and chairs (p. 10).** Apples + pears → fruit; tables + chairs → furniture; apples + chairs → only a label like "objects". Lesson: mixed-kind groupings force a label too broad to say anything about the logic.

**Exhibit 1 (p. 9; repeated p. 13) — a pyramid under one thought.** One top box; three Key Line boxes; each with 2–4 children; each of those with 2–4 children. Lesson: the generic target shape; the Key Line is the row directly under the apex.

**Exhibit 2 (p. 15) — Chesterton argues for keeping pigs as pets.** Chosen to show the vertical exchange without the distraction of business logic.

```
Pigs should be kept as pets
└─ Why?
   ├─ They are beautiful
   │  └─ In what way?
   │     ├─ They are wonderfully fat
   │     │  └─ How is fat beautiful?
   │     │     ├─ Pleasing curves for the viewer
   │     │     └─ Modesty in the pig itself
   │     └─ They are quintessentially English
   │        └─ How is English beautiful?   (a 3-step deductive chain)
   │           ├─ They are tied to the land
   │           │  └─ How? — likened to two classic English landscape features
   │           ├─ → That tie shows strength can coexist with gentleness
   │           └─ → So English that they merit being the national emblem
   └─ They could be bred into fascinating variations
      └─ How? — by type; by size; by personality; by function
```

Lesson: a reader who rejects the argument can still see exactly what it is and why the author holds it, because each grouping sticks to answering the question raised above it. Note the mix: most groupings are inductive, but the "English" leg is a deductive chain.

**Exhibit 3 (p. 16) — 20-page memo recommending purchase of a car-dealer franchise (British Leyland).**

```
Buy a large British Leyland franchise
└─ Why?
   ├─ It will outgrow the industry
   │  └─ Why? — big market share; few retail competitors
   ├─ Its financial effect will be positive
   │  └─ Why? — low cost; sales growing; profits rising
   └─ It will be easy to integrate
      └─ Why? — stand-alone business; current managers stay; simple controls
```

Lesson: every document should show the Q/A exchange; laid out this clearly, the reader can see where they disagree and ask pointed questions.

**Illustrations of the two horizontal forms (p. 17).** Deductive: the mortality syllogism (class rule → individual in the class → rule applies), summarized by leaning on the conclusion. Inductive: three nations' tanks on one country's border, summarized as an impending invasion. Lesson: the forms move up a level differently — deduction summarizes with weight on the last step; induction infers from what the items share. (Invented same-shape examples appear in the Horizontal Relationship concept above.)

**Board of directors memo (p. 19).** Before: an opening saying the memo gathers thoughts for reflection and discussion, followed by five governance topics (board makeup and size; board vs. executive committee roles; making outside directors effective; selection and tenure; how to get from the current to the desired arrangement). After, recast as a story:

```
S: A recent reorganization handed day-to-day running of the divisions to their
   managers, leaving the board free for policy and planning.
C: The board has spent so long on short-term operating issues that it isn't yet
   equipped to concentrate on long-range strategy.
Q: (implicit) What must the board change to do so?
A: The board should:
   ├─ Delegate operating matters to the executive committee
   ├─ Add outside members
   └─ Formalize its internal procedures and policies
```

Lesson: casting the opening as a story exposes the reader's question and turns a topic list into an answer with a Key Line.

## Nuances and exceptions

- **Top-down delivery ≠ top-down thinking.** Minto stresses that the thinking is assembled bottom-up (sentences into paragraphs, etc.) while delivery runs top-down. Don't read the principle as "decide the conclusion first and backfill".
- **Leading with a claim can be an illusion.** A writer may believe the opening sentence is the top point while the following points don't visibly support it (equal-pay example). What matters is whether readers can see the relationship, not whether an assertion comes first.
- **Agreement isn't the goal.** The exchange ends when the reader has no further *logical* question; they may still disagree. Both Chesterton and the TTW memo are offered as clear even if unpersuasive.
- **Seven is a ceiling, not a target.** Capacity varies (about five to nine), three is handy, one is easiest; the trigger for grouping is around four or five items.
- **Two relationships vs. four orders.** Ch. 2 says a grouping relates either deductively or inductively; Ch. 1 lists four orders (deductive, chronological, structural, comparative). Read together: a deductive grouping takes argument order; the other three orders apply to groupings produced by cause-and-effect, by dividing a structure, or by classifying. Ch. 6 (`05-logical-order.md`) elaborates.
- **No restriction on content.** Any kind of idea can be grouped (reasons, steps, problems, changes); the only constraint is one kind per grouping.
- **Finding precedes arranging.** Most writers don't know precisely what they think when they begin, and first formulations are rough. The substructures are tools for discovering ideas, not just for ordering known ones.
- **A question can be latent.** The reader's question may not yet be conscious; it counts if they would ask it after briefly considering their circumstances.
- **The introduction reminds; it doesn't inform.** Its content is what readers know or can fairly be assumed to know (elaborated in `02-building-the-pyramid.md`, caveat 5, and `03-introductions.md`).
- **Mixed forms within one pyramid are normal.** Exhibit 2 has inductive groupings and a deductive chain in different places; "never both" applies within a single grouping.

> **Skill note:** For agent use, "the reader's question" anchors every structural judgment. A reviewing skill should first ask (or infer) who the reader is and what they asked or must decide, then judge the top point against that question, before checking groupings.

> **Skill note:** The plural-noun test is the cheapest, most mechanical check an agent can run on any bullet list or set of sections; failing it reliably signals a mixed grouping.
