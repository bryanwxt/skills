# Introduction Patterns

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 4 "Fine Points of Introductions", book pp. 37–39 (Exhibits 10–11) and 49–59 (the business and consulting pattern sections); Appendix B "Examples of Introductory Structures", book pp. 216–234. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `PAT`. Pattern codes (W1, S7, H6, …) are this file's own lookup labels, not the book's.

## When a skill needs this
- Choosing the right S-C-Q skeleton for a document type: directive, budget request, how-to, options memo, proposal, project plan, progress report, process-change recommendation.
- Converting a request like "and show me the alternatives" into a defensible structure, or reviewing an options memo built as pros/cons lists.
- Structuring a consulting proposal (steps vs reasons) or a phase-end progress review.
- Writing a process-change memo, especially when the reader knows the old and new processes, or knows almost nothing about the process.
- Checking that a draft's Key Line has the shape its question demands ("how?" → steps; "approve?" → standard reasons; "which?" → criteria or per-option statements).

### Which pattern? (decision procedure)
1. **Reporting on a phase of work the reader commissioned?** → Y2 (first review) or Y3 (later ones).
2. **Proposing to do work for the reader?**
   - Known client or formality → H8.
   - Competitive or new client → S8.
   - Internal project plan → H9.
3. **Asking the reader to approve spending?** → S7.
4. **Need the reader to do something for the writer?** → H6.
5. **Recommending a change to a process?** Decide by what the reader knows:
   - Knows the process and its faults → X1/H5.
   - Knows the old and the new → X2.
   - Knows little or nothing → X3.
6. **Reader already holds a plan or solution and wants a verdict?** Pick by the doubt involved:
   - Right action? → S1.
   - Implementation trouble? → S2.
   - Tested, is it OK? → S3.
   - Will it reach objective Y? → S4.
   - Will a threat force change? → S5.
7. **Reader unsure whether a change under way is even a problem?** → S6.
8. **Reader knows *what* to do but not *how*?**
   - Prerequisite → H1.
   - Known fix → H2.
   - New activity → H4.
   - Broken process → H5.
   - System being installed → H7.
   - Account of past work → H3.
9. **Reader already aware of two or more options?** → W3.
   - "Options" are just "keep the routine" vs "change it" for a practice nobody questioned → W5.
   - No option gives the reader everything → W3, alternative-objectives variant.
10. **Task blocked, fix failed, or problem without a fix?** → W1.
    - The required result is known → W2.
    - Triggered by an audit → W4.
11. **Reader wants the cause of a failure?** → Y1.
12. **Still unclear?** → reason backward from the points to be made (see `03-introductions.md`, INT-21).

### The "four questions" across the book (reconciliation)
Minto lists the standard questions slightly differently in three places. Same four families, different order and wording:

| Family | Exhibit 10 (p. 37) | Ch. 4 prose (p. 49) | App. B prose and Exhibit B-1 headings (pp. 216–217) |
|---|---|---|---|
| W (what to do) | What should we do? | What should we do? | What should we do? |
| H (how) | How do we implement the solution? | How should/will/did we do it? | How do/did we do something? |
| S (whether to go ahead) | Is it the right solution? | Should we do it? | Should we go ahead with what we plan? |
| Y (why) | Why not? | Why did it happen? | Why did it happen? (only "occasionally") |

- Appendix B calls three of them standard and the fourth occasional.
- Ch. 4 (p. 49) adds that most documents tell readers what action to take. Readers seldom want an explanation of *why* without also wanting to know what to do, except when early findings in an analysis are being reported.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Directive | A document that asks or tells someone to do something; the commonest business memo. |
| Planting the question | Creating a question the reader didn't have (directives), rather than recalling one they did. |
| Implied question | A Question left unstated because the flow makes it obvious; the writer still pins it down privately. |
| Standard reasons (for spending) | Can't wait; fixes the problem (or is the best option); numbers justify it; extra benefits. |
| "Other goodies" | Incidental extra benefits. Mention them only if facts support them; never the reason for acting. |
| Present vs recommended process | Side-by-side pictures of how a task is done now and how it should be done. Their differences become the Key Line. |
| Genuine alternatives | Options the reader identified or knew before the writer began. The only kind a document should weigh. |
| Alternative objectives (alternative R2s) | Conditional recommendations ("pick A if you value P") used when no single action delivers everything. |
| R1 / R2 | From Ch. 8: R1 = the unwanted current result; R2 = the wanted result. A solution moves the reader from R1 to R2. |
| Situation-R1-R2 | Ch. 8's problem-definition structure, used to set out the client's problem in proposals. |
| Opening scene / Disturbing event | Ch. 8 framework parts: the setting or process where the problem lives, and what upset it. |
| Proposal (A) / (B) | Exhibit B-1's labels: (A) asks how the consultant would help (steps); (B) asks whether to hire this consultant (reasons). |
| Progress review | Formal phase-end communication to a client or superior, often leading up to a final report. |
| Hot buttons / agendas | Specific concerns of client decision makers that will sway consultant selection. |
| Before-after analysis | Picturing the old and new process step by step to pin down exactly which changes are needed. |

## Concepts

### Some Common Patterns (pp. 49–50)
- **Recurring shapes.** With practice a writer notices that introductions fall into a handful of recurring shapes, nearly always answering one of the four question families (see the reconciliation table).
- **Most documents are about action.** Wanting to know why something happened, without also wanting to know what to do about it, is rare. The exception is reporting early analytical findings.
- **Which patterns dominate depends on the line of business.** Minto describes the four business patterns she meets most:
  - *directives*: the reader must act (what or how);
  - *requests for funds*: whether to approve;
  - *how-to explanations*: how;
  - *choices between known options*: what to do.

### Giving Direction (pp. 50–51)
- **The commonest business memo.** A directive asks or tells someone to do something, and it may be the most frequent business memo anywhere. It *plants* the question rather than recalling one.
- **Field-sales example:**
  - Background: the writer plans to train field salespeople, at an upcoming meeting, to present a new shelf-space program for grocery chains, and needs input from each of them about one troublesome chain locally.
  - S = the meeting will train you to present the program; C = that training depends on details from each of you about a local problem chain; Q = (how do I supply those details?).
  - Bare form: S = our aim is X; C = reaching it requires Y from you; Q = how is Y to be done?
- **The question stays implicit** because the text flows without it. The writer must still state it privately before drafting, or risk losing track of what is being answered.
- **Exhibit 14.** Top point: profile one problem chain. Under "how?": pick a suitable chain (first deadline); gather the data needed (second deadline); organise and send back the data (final deadline).
- **A "how?" question is always answered with steps.**
- **The Complication and the Answer roughly mirror each other.** Doing the steps produces the result, and that result removes what the Complication said was missing (details are needed → here's how to supply them).
- **Procedures-manual example:**
  - S = a manual governs activities where inconsistent practice would harm the company, and it needs periodic revision; C = consistency requires a common method for entering changes; Q = (what's the method?).
  - Bare form: S = you do X; C = X must be done in a particular way; Q = which way? Again implicit.
  - This memo opening also appears among Ch. 4's length samples (p. 43).

### Seeking Approval to Spend Money (pp. 51–52)
- **The question never varies.** Another very frequent type. The reader's question is always whether to approve, and it stays implicit.
- **Rough shape:** S = a problem exists; C = a fix is available at cost $N; Q = (approve?).
- **Worked case:**
  - S = departmental workload has risen 20% a year for four years while headquarters policy has frozen staff at 14, producing overtime, weekend work, and a growing backlog.
  - C = the backlog now stands at 22 weeks, unacceptable to the field, with no hours left to add; analysis shows a particular computer system at a stated price would cut both backlog and overtime.
  - Q = (approve?); A = please approve.
- **Standard supporting reasons** (three, sometimes four) for "approve, because…":
  1. the problem can't wait;
  2. this action fixes it (or, where options were weighed, is the best of them);
  3. projected savings outweigh the cost (or some other financial case);
  4. extra benefits come with it.
- **Each reason has a job.**
  - (1) is where the problem gets described in full.
  - (2) is where the solution gets described in full.
  - (3) holds the usual financial analysis.
  - (4) isn't always borne out by the facts (e.g. "it opens new service opportunities"), so include it only when it is. Nobody would act *for* that reason, but since the action is happening anyway, the bonus is worth noting.
- **Pyramid:** Approve this expenditure → *why?* → action can't wait / this fixes the problem (or: best of the options, if options were examined) / the numbers justify it / we gain extra benefits too.

### Explaining "How to" (pp. 53–54)
- **The Key Line of every how-to document is a set of steps:** do X → how? → step 1, step 2, step 3. This is common, particularly in consulting, where someone has a problem and the writer explains the cure.
- **The introduction depends on whether the activity is new or existing.**
  - *New* (e.g. the Role of the Board memo, p. 19): S = activity X is now required; C = the organisation isn't equipped for it; Q = how to get equipped?
  - *Existing but faulty:* a client's forecasting system produces unreliable forecasts, and the client wants it fixed. S = here is the current system; C = it doesn't perform as it should; Q = what changes would make it perform?
- **Technique for the faulty-process case:**
  1. Draw today's process exactly as it is actually performed.
  2. Draw the process as it ought to run.
  3. Where the two pictures differ, those differences are the Key Line steps.
- **Make both processes visible before writing.** Familiarity breeds false confidence: someone who has worked on a process for a long time may feel sure of its every step, yet without drawing and comparing both versions, important pieces are very likely to be missed. Minto has seen so much incomplete thinking of this kind that she expands on it in Appendix B. The Big Chief memo (Ch. 3, p. 23) is another case.
- **Exhibit 15 (p. 54), market forecasting:** see Worked examples.

### Choosing Among Alternatives (pp. 54–56)
- **A well-defined problem has no alternative solutions.** Managers often ask for a solution "plus the alternatives". But (see Ch. 8) once a problem is properly defined, a recommendation either solves it or doesn't.
- **What the manager is really asking** is what options exist if no fully satisfactory solution can be found.
- **Weigh genuine alternatives only when the reader already knows them**, usually because they've been debated internally. Then the introduction is simple: S = we want to do X; C = several routes exist; Q = which is best?
- **Motor example:**
  - S = a ruling has named a certain horsepower range as most efficient for cold-weather drilling, so the largest customer plans to drop our 10 HP motor for a competitor's smaller one.
  - C = three options are on the table: match our 10 HP motor's price to our smaller model's; re-engineer our smaller model to equal the competitor's; design a new motor in the favoured range.
  - Q = which is best?
- **Two Key Line shapes after choosing:**
  - *By criteria* (cleanest, whenever possible): Pick C → quicker than A or B; cheaper than A or B; simpler to put in place.
  - *Option by option* (when C doesn't win on every criterion): Pick C → C meets every need except…; A fails because…; B fails because…. That is, the main reason for C plus the main reason each rival was dropped.
- **When nothing delivers everything the reader wants**, whether because none of the known options does or, with no options known beforehand, because no recommendable action does:
  - the question remains "which?" or "what should we do?";
  - the answer becomes *it depends on your priorities* → A, if steady sales matter most; B, if quick profits do; C, if labour peace does.
  - That is organised around **alternative objectives**, not alternative ways of solving the problem, and Minto insists these are quite different things.

### Some Common Patterns—Consulting (p. 57)
- **Consulting documents are longer and aim mainly at action.** This holds whether they are memos, reports, presentations, or proposals, so consultants usually answer only the first three of Exhibit 10's four questions.
- Chapters 8–9 treat consulting documents fully.
- **The two commonest consulting introductions** are proposals and phase reviews.

### Letters of Proposal (pp. 57–58)
- **Proposals are the lifeblood of consulting.** Firms have refined them heavily, and most follow this shape:
  - S = the client has a problem (described in a sentence or two); C = the client has opted for outside help; Q = (are you the firm to engage?).
  - The answer is always yes, typically backed by four parts: (1) grasp of the problem; (2) a sound method for solving it; (3) deep experience applying that method; (4) sensible commercial terms.
- **C and Q usually go unstated in the text.** Sample opening, paraphrased: thanks for the meeting about your difficulty choosing how to attack the automotive aftermarket amid conflicting internal opinions; this document is our proposal to help you sift the options and build a strategy for rapid share gains.
- **This form suits new clients**, where the consultant wants to spend real effort explaining the problem so that expertise shows.
- **For a well-known client, or when the proposal is a formality,** it's cleaner to put the problem description into the introduction (see Ch. 8).
  - S = the problem, explained over three or four paragraphs; C = the client wants consultants to help fix it; Q = what would that help consist of?
  - The body is organised around the **approach**, on the theory that clients choose on the strength of the approach (Minto adds wryly that they don't always).
  - This shape invites weaving past-experience examples into the explanation of how and why the approach is taken.
  - Commercial terms go in a covering letter.

### Progress Reviews (pp. 58–59)
- **What they are.** Formal communications scheduled at the end of each project phase with a client or superior, frequently leading toward a final report.
- **Once past the first, every review follows one fixed shape.**
- **First review:** S = the team has been working on problem X; C = it said its first step would be checking whether Y holds, and that check is finished; Q = what turned up?
- **Between reviews, the recipient reacts.** The recipient may ask for an anomaly to be investigated, or approve the work and move the team to phase two.
- **Next review, example:**
  - S = the previous review reported a capacity shortfall.
  - C = the client expected it to be brief, believing a rival was about to fold, and asked for that to be verified; verification is done.
  - Q = (what turned up?); A = the shortfall will persist, and get worse.
- **Bare form:** S = previous message X; C = requested follow-up Y is complete; Q = what turned up?
- Real consulting introductions appear in Appendix B.

### Difficult Introductory Forms (p. 221)
Every introduction has the simple S-C-Q structure, but some need extra thought. Minto picks out the two most frequent: documents that **propose how a problem will be solved** (consulting proposals, project plans) and documents that **handle alternatives**.

### Proposing Steps to Solve Problems (pp. 221–224)
- **These documents come before the solution.** Ordinarily a document follows the solving of its problem. Consulting proposals and project plans instead set out how the writer intends to *find* the solution.
- **Both put the problem definition up front** and usually organise the body around the analytical steps.
  - Both convey the writer's understanding of the reader's problem and the plan for solving it.
  - If accepted, the analysis follows, then a report of conclusions and recommendations.
- **A consulting proposal is also a contract.** It covers what the client buys, the price, the finish date, and who does what. To make sure these get covered, most firms use a stock heading set, which falls into three groups:
  - *problem-definition headings:* Introduction · Background · Objectives & Scope;
  - *solution-step headings:* Issues · Technical Approach · Work Plan & Deliverables;
  - *others:* Benefits · Firm Qualifications & Related Experience · Timing, Staffing & Fees.
- **Why the stock set is a trap:**
  - Writing under those headings invites a list under each, and the lists overlap, hiding the actual thinking.
  - The first group is all really problem definition; the second is all really solution steps.
  - A separate Benefits section adds nothing, since the benefit is solving the client's problem, which was the objective from the start.
- **Recommended instead:**
  - The introduction explains the problem.
  - The body is organised either around the **approach** (Exhibit B-2, steps) or around **reasons to hire** the firm (Exhibit B-3).
  - **Project plans are always organised around the process.**
  - Qualifications and timing/staffing/fees are included but sit **outside the structure of the thinking**.
- **Exhibit B-2 (steps):** S = client's problem (Situation, R1, R2); C = client wants consulting help; Q = how would you help? → "Our plan for solving your problem:" → steps one to four. Qualifications and timing/staffing/fees sit off to one side.
- **Steps vs reasons turns on competition.**
  - For a past client, where the proposal merely confirms what's been agreed this time, organise by steps.
  - In a competitive bid, organise by reasons to hire.
- **Exhibit B-3 (reasons):** S = client's problem (1–2 sentences); C = client wants consulting help; Q = should we hire you? → "Hire us to solve your problem" →
  - we grasp your problem (S-R1-R2);
  - we know how to solve it (→ steps one to three);
  - we've applied our problem-solving technique extensively;
  - our timing, staffing, and fees are sensible.
- **The reasons version opens with a short preview paragraph.** Paraphrased: thanks for meeting about marketing your software in developing countries; here is our proposal to help you shape a fitting marketing strategy; it covers the opportunity as we see it, how we'd help, our track record, and our terms. Then:
  - Section one explains the problem in detail through Situation-R1-R2, deliberately addressing the particular concerns ("hot buttons", agendas) of the client decision makers expected to influence the choice. Minto footnotes Romano & Freed, *Writing Winning Proposals* (1995), on gauging client concerns.
  - Section two lays out the approach.
  - Section three spotlights the specific or unique expertise brought to the problem.
- **Telephone-company software example (p. 224):** the problem-definition framework and resulting steps pyramid are under Worked examples. The text labels these "Exhibits B-4 and B-5", but those numbers belong to the process exhibits that follow; the telco diagrams are unnumbered.

### Dealing with Alternative Solutions (pp. 225–226)
- **The R1/R2 restatement.** This is the third cell of Exhibit B-1's first section (W3). Ch. 4's point recurs: strictly, a problem has no alternative solutions. A recommendation either carries the reader from R1 to R2 or it doesn't.
- **So-called alternatives appear when R2 is stated vaguely**, so that a solution can't be recognised on sight.
- **Comparing options with each other is irrelevant.** With R2 vague, people pick three or four plausible courses fairly arbitrarily and set them against *each other* (strengths and weaknesses, pros and cons). What counts is how each measures up *against R2*. Lacking an R2, they are really trying to back into one.
- **Define R2 at the outset.** Defining R2 is often the first step of problem solving. Backing into R2 through options can work but is gruelling, especially as people feel obliged to balance the pros/cons lists and print them all in full, without summarising or fitting them into a pyramid.
- **Discuss alternatives only if the reader knew them beforehand**, having identified them as possible courses. The reader's question is then "which?".
- **Otherwise the writer is raising options just to shoot them down:** three routes A, B, C → A fails because… → B fails because… → so do C.
  - Yet C's justification is that **it solves the problem**, not that A and B fail. Why mention A and B at all?
- **"The reader asked for them" doesn't settle it.** Logically, a reader can only *need* alternatives if the problem is badly defined, meaning R2 is unclear. In that case the reader really wants **alternative R2s**, and those can be supplied.
  - Structuring the document around alternative R2s is perfectly clear.
  - It fits when no solution the writer can devise gives the reader the whole of the desired R2: X, if earnings stability is the priority; Y, if rapid growth is; Z, if labour peace is.
- **If the reader wants "alternatives" but not alternative R2s,** despite a clear R2 and a solution that clearly meets it, two options remain:
  - include them in the introduction, which can get unwieldy; or
  - relegate them to an appendix, ideally a chart with the options listed down the side, the judgement criteria across the top, and check marks showing which option met which criterion.

### Describing Changes to Processes (pp. 226–227)
- **Usual case:** the reader understands both the process and what's wrong with it. The introduction can sketch them briefly and the body is organised around the changes: S = process X is in use; C = it isn't working; Q = how should it change?
- **The trick (from Ch. 4)** is to **picture clearly the steps "before" and "after"** so the required changes become clear to the writer.
- **Two further situations demand the same before-after analysis** for a short, clear introduction:
  1. The reader is familiar with the flawed current process *and* with the replacement they'd like, so the question is how to roll it out, or whether to.
  2. The reader knows nothing of how the process works, nor that it has faults, and wonders both what change is proposed and why any change is needed.
- **Typical failure in these two cases:** saying nothing about the processes, or saying far too much. Appendix B shows a weak example of each and rebuilds it with before-after analysis.

### The Reader Knows the Old and the New (pp. 227–229) — DDT (Exhibit B-4, "the process is not described")
- **The reader's real request.** The DDT document was written for a reader who wanted to know whether an existing process could be changed in a particular way.
- **Its structure, approximately:**
  - S = the team did an earlier study on computer storage and transmission of documents; a research institute studied delivery problems on the European network; the committee then asked for more technical work.
  - C = the team has been examining technical, cost, and management aspects of digitising and electronically delivering documents, given fast-moving technology.
  - Q = (none identifiable).
  - A = workable at reasonable cost on a European scale; the team designed a system (DDT) building on the existing network; market forces won't produce it, so a demonstration project is needed; more technical studies are needed; significant non-technical issues must be settled.
- **Diagnosis.** Apart from an ungainly title, the writer is unsure what to say and so says it opaquely. The writer hasn't clarified what problem the commissioning committee has or what it expects, and "examining the aspects" is not an illuminating purpose.
- **Remedy.** A reviewer can use Ch. 8's Problem Definition Framework to tidy the structure and sharpen the wording. Step one is to sketch the present process and the change the committee wants:
  - *Now:* the user scans a televised listing to find a document, phones a library to request it, and the library locates, copies, and posts it. 7–10 days.
  - *Wanted:* documents digitised and stored centrally; the user scans the listing, phones, and sees the document on screen within the hour.
- **The real question.** Seen this way, the reader plainly **understands the problem and already has a solution in mind**. So the question belongs to the "is it a good solution?" family; more exactly, *can an inexpensive electronic-delivery system be built?*
- **Rebuilt introduction:** see Worked examples.

### The Reader Knows Little or Nothing (pp. 230–234) — Period Graph Books (Exhibits B-5, B-6)
- **The temptation.** Writers often need sign-off on a fix when the reader **doesn't know the problem**, so the problem must be explained at length. The temptation is to narrate how the system now works, and everything wrong with it, before reaching the proposed changes.
- **The introduction rule still binds, with a refinement.**
  - The introduction may contain nothing the reader doesn't know or wouldn't accept.
  - But it *may* give the reader enough to "see" that a problem could exist and that the proposed fix could be credible.
  - Doing that requires the writer to **see the problem clearly first**.
- **Exhibit B-5 (over-described).** A memo about a system producing monthly graph books (sales, cost, and profit for five subsidiaries) that underpin top-management presentations. It catalogues the problems exhaustively.
  - Structure: S = we've taken over producing the graph books; C = issues have emerged in the handover; Q = (blank).
  - Key Line: our process and its faults / the other group's process and its faults / the whole thing needs streamlining, shall we discuss next steps?
- **It offers no remedy beyond "streamlining".**
  - Minto's rule of thumb: **never raise problems without also proposing solutions**.
  - In any case, properly stated problems contain their own solutions.
- **Steps:**
  1. draw the present processes;
  2. note what's wrong with each;
  3. draw the system that would remove the faults;
  4. list the changes that get from old to new;
  5. state the problem briefly in the introduction.
- **Exhibit B-6** draws the pictures: two current systems and a proposed one (see Worked examples). With everything laid out, the core problem is obvious: graphs are unreliable because errors enter at three points (data entry, calculation of graph points, and presenters altering graphs).
- **The changes become the Key Line**, answering "what changes do you recommend?". The writer then **reasons backward** to decide what the introduction must say to make the reader ask exactly that.
- **Each Key Line point prompts "why?"** Beneath each goes a detailed account of where the current system goes wrong at that point and how the change cures it.
- **Only the faulty steps need describing, not the whole system.** That shrinks the memo considerably. The index files this under handling "extended problem descriptions", p. 234.

## Rules
- **PAT-1** — Before drafting, place the reader's question in one of the four families (what to do / whether to go ahead / how / why) and pick the matching pattern. *Why:* most introductions follow a few recurring patterns, and the crucial thinking happens while the introduction is being worked out. *Check:* the writer can name the family and pattern code, and the draft's S/C/Q matches that pattern's skeleton. (pp. 49, 216–217)
- **PAT-2** — In a directive, plant the question: S = the writer's aim; C = what the writer needs from the reader to reach it; Q (implicit) = how does the reader do that? Pin the Q down privately even though the text won't state it. *Why:* the reader had no such question beforehand, and an unpinned Q leaves the writer unsure what is being answered. *Check:* S states the writer's goal, C states the request, and the body explains how. (pp. 50–51)
- **PAT-3** — When the reader's question is "how?", make the Key Line a set of steps (actions). *Why:* "how?" is always answered by steps. *Check:* every Key Line point under a "how?" is an action, and together they form a sequence. (pp. 51, 53)
- **PAT-4** — In a directive, check that the Answer roughly undoes the Complication. *Why:* the Answer is the outcome of performing the actions, and that outcome resolves the problem. *Check:* restate C as a gap, then confirm that executing A closes it. (p. 51)
- **PAT-5** — In a funding request, leave "approve?" implicit, make the Answer "approve", and support it with the standard reasons. *Why:* these are the arguments an approver needs, and they give natural homes to the problem description, the solution description, and the financial analysis. *Check:*
  - The Key Line holds 3–4 reasons answering "why approve?":
    - it can't wait;
    - this action fixes the problem (or is the best option, where options were weighed);
    - the numbers justify it;
    - any extra benefits.
  - The detail of the problem sits under "can't wait".
  (pp. 51–52)
- **PAT-6** — Mention extra benefits ("other goodies") only when facts back them, and never as the justification for acting. *Why:* nobody would act for that reason; it's a by-product worth noting. *Check:* the extra-benefits point comes last, is factual, and isn't the lead argument. (p. 52)
- **PAT-7** — In a how-to document, choose the introduction by whether the activity is new (S = activity now required; C = no capability yet) or existing but faulty (S = current system; C = it underperforms). *Why:* the two raise different questions (how to get equipped vs what changes would make it work). *Check:* C clearly says either "not equipped" or "not performing". (p. 53)
- **PAT-8** — Before writing anything that changes a process, draw the present and recommended processes side by side, and derive the Key Line from their differences. *Why:* writers who skip this almost always leave out something important, even when sure they know the processes. *Check:* the writer can produce both pictures, and each Key Line point corresponds to a difference between them. (pp. 53–54, 226–233)
- **PAT-9** — Treat options as genuine alternatives (S = want X; C = several routes; Q = which?) only when the reader already knows them. *Why:* a properly defined problem has no alternative solutions, and options the reader didn't know only get raised to be knocked down. *Check:* is there evidence the reader identified or debated these options earlier? If not, don't build the document on them. (pp. 54–55, 225; consistent with DEF-17, which places reader-raised alternatives in the Complication)
- **PAT-10** — Having chosen among known options, organise the Key Line around the judgement criteria if the winner beats every rival on every criterion. Otherwise give one statement per option: the main reason for the winner, and the main reason each rival was dropped. *Why:* criteria are the cleanest argument; per-option statements are the fallback when the winner isn't best across the board. *Check:* with criteria, each point compares the winner against all the others; without, there's exactly one point per option. (pp. 55–56)
- **PAT-11** — When no option (or no possible action) gives the reader all they want, organise around alternative objectives (pick A if P matters most; B if Q does; …), not around alternative ways of solving the problem. *Why:* the reader is then really asking for alternative R2s, which is legitimate. *Check:* each Key Line point is conditional on a named objective. (pp. 56, 225–226)
- **PAT-12** — Judge options against the wanted result (R2), not against each other, and define R2 first. *Why:* pros/cons comparisons among options say nothing about whether any of them solves the problem, and they are a painful way to back into R2. *Check:* the document states R2 explicitly and every option's assessment refers to it. No balanced pros/cons lists in the text. (p. 225)
- **PAT-13** — If the reader insists on seeing alternatives despite a clear R2 and a clear solution, put them in the introduction (accepting some awkwardness) or in an appendix chart: options down the side, criteria across the top, check marks for matches. *Why:* this honours the request without distorting the main argument, whose reason for the recommendation is that it solves the problem. *Check:* rejected options aren't Key Line points. (pp. 225–226; cf. DEF-18)
- **PAT-14** — Choose the proposal structure by context. *Why:* competitive bids are won on reasons, confirmations only need the plan, and clients largely choose on approach. *Check:* the proposal's apex reads either "hire us…" (reasons) or "our plan for solving your problem…" (steps), consistent with the context.
  - New or competitive client: brief problem statement plus a "reasons to hire" Key Line (grasp of problem; method; experience; terms).
  - Known client or formality: full problem description in the introduction plus a Key Line of approach steps, with commercial terms in a covering letter.
  (pp. 57–58, 222–223)
- **PAT-15** — Don't build a proposal on the stock consulting headings, don't include a separate Benefits section, and keep qualifications and timing/staffing/fees outside the thinking structure. *Why:* the stock set breeds overlapping lists that obscure the thinking; several headings are really problem definition and several are really steps. *Check:* the proposal's sections map onto one pyramid (problem in the introduction; steps or reasons in the body), with qualifications and fees as attached material. (pp. 221–222)
- **PAT-16** — Organise every project plan around the process. *Why:* a plan's job is to show the steps that will find the solution. *Check:* the Key Line of a project plan is a sequence of steps. (pp. 221–222)
- **PAT-17** — In a "reasons" proposal, structure the content as follows. *Why:* these concerns drive the selection, and the problem section is where expertise shows. *Check:* the problem section names the decision makers' concerns and has an explicit R1 and R2.
  - Open with a short paragraph previewing the parts (understanding, approach, experience, terms).
  - Explain the problem in the understanding section in Situation-R1-R2 form, aimed at the decision makers' hot buttons.
  - Use the experience section to spotlight specific or unique expertise.
  (p. 223)
- **PAT-18** — Open a progress review by recalling what was last reported or promised (S), then what the reader asked for or what was due next, now done (C), with the implicit "what turned up?" (Q). Lead with the finding. *Why:* after the first review the shape never varies, and the reader wants the result. *Check:* the review doesn't narrate activities in sequence, and its main point is a finding. (pp. 58–59)
- **PAT-19** — Never raise problems without also proposing solutions. *Why:* a properly stated problem implies its remedy, so a problems-only memo signals unfinished thinking. *Check:* any document listing problems ends in recommended changes, not "shall we discuss next steps?". (p. 230)
- **PAT-20** — When the reader doesn't know the problem, give the introduction only enough for the reader to see that a problem could exist and that the fix could work. Explain the current system under each change, covering only the steps where faults occur. *Why:* the introduction may hold only what the reader knows or accepts, and narrating every step bloats the document. *Check:* the introduction doesn't walk through the current process, and only faulty steps are described under each Key Line change. (pp. 230, 234)
- **PAT-21** — When the reader knows both the old and the wanted process, state their question precisely (is it feasible? is it a good fix? should it be rolled out, and how?) and answer it, instead of offering a vague account of what was "examined". *Why:* a writer who hasn't defined the reader's problem ends up writing opaquely. *Check:* the main point delivers a yes/no or how verdict on the specific proposed change. (pp. 227–229)
- **PAT-22** — In a process-change document, make the Key Line the recommended changes, answering "what changes do you recommend?". Reason backward from them to the introduction, and expect each change to prompt "why?". *Why:* the changes fall out of the before-after comparison, and the introduction needs only enough to provoke the question. *Check:* Key Line points are changes (actions), and each section explains the current fault and how the change removes it. (pp. 232–234)

## Procedures

### A. Pick and fill an introduction pattern
1. Identify the reader and what they already know about the subject (see `03-introductions.md`, INT-2/INT-6).
2. Run the *Which pattern?* decision procedure above to choose a code.
3. Fill that pattern's S, C, and Q in the case's own terms. Write the Q down even if the text will leave it implicit (PAT-2).
4. Test the C → Q link: given S and C, would the reader ask exactly this Q? If not, re-pick the pattern or rewrite C.
5. State A, then the new question A raises, then shape the Key Line as the card specifies (steps / reasons / criteria / changes / findings).
6. Check against the pattern's specific rules (e.g. PAT-5 for funding, PAT-9–13 for alternatives, PAT-14–17 for proposals).
7. Decide what to state and what to leave implicit. Commonly implicit: the Question in directives, funding requests, and proposals, and the Complication in proposals.

### B. Before-after process analysis (process-change documents: H5, X1–X3)
1. Draw the **present** process as boxes in sequence, as it is actually done. Where several systems feed one output, draw each.
2. Under each step, note **what goes wrong** there.
3. Draw the **process needed** to remove those faults.
4. List the **changes** that get from old to new. These are the Key Line points. Find the summary point above them (e.g. "remove the points where errors creep in").
5. Decide what the reader knows:
   - Knows the process and its faults → brief S ("process X in use") + C ("not working") + Q "how should it change?" (X1).
   - Knows old and new → state the current pain and the wanted process. Q = is it feasible / sound / how or whether to roll out. Give a verdict (X2).
   - Knows little or nothing → reason backward from the changes. Put in S and C only enough for the reader to see a problem could exist and the fix is plausible. Q = what changes do you recommend? (X3).
6. Under each change, answer "why?" by describing only the faulty current steps and how the change cures them (PAT-20).
7. Confirm the document doesn't raise problems without solutions (PAT-19).

### C. Handling a request for "alternatives"
1. Did the reader know these options beforehand (identified or debated them)?
   - **Yes** → W3 introduction (S = want X; C = several routes; Q = which?). Go to step 3.
   - **No** → go to step 2.
2. Is R2 (the wanted result) clearly defined?
   - **Yes, and a solution achieves it** → recommend that solution on its merits (it solves the problem). If the reader still insists on alternatives, put them in the introduction or an appendix chart (PAT-13).
   - **No / ambiguous** → define R2 first (PAT-12). If no action achieves the full R2, organise around alternative objectives (PAT-11).
3. Does the choice beat the rivals on every criterion?
   - **Yes** → Key Line by criteria (quicker / cheaper / simpler than the others).
   - **No** → Key Line option by option (why C; why not A; why not B).
   - **None meets what the reader wants** → "depends on your priorities" Key Line (alternative objectives).

### D. Structuring a consulting proposal or project plan
1. Define the problem with the Situation-R1-R2 framework (Ch. 8; see `07-defining-the-problem.md`): opening scene, disturbing event, R1, R2, and any second layer (e.g. "decided to do X, but don't know Y").
2. Decide on the context:
   - **Project plan** → organise around the process steps (PAT-16).
   - **Known client or formality** → the introduction holds the full problem description (3–4 paragraphs). Q = how would you help? Key Line = approach steps ("our plan for solving your problem…"). Terms go in a covering letter.
   - **New client or competitive** → the introduction gives a 1–2-sentence problem plus "you want help". Q (implicit) = should we hire you? Key Line = grasp of problem (S-R1-R2, aimed at hot buttons) / approach (with steps beneath) / experience (unique expertise) / terms (timing, staffing, fees). Open with a short paragraph previewing these parts.
3. Keep qualifications and timing/staffing/fees outside the logical structure as attached material (PAT-15).
4. Don't use the stock heading set and don't add a Benefits section (PAT-15).

### E. Writing a funding request
1. S = the problem, told as a story the approver recognises (growth, constraints, consequences).
2. C = the problem has hit a breaking point, plus the fix and its price.
3. Q (implicit) = approve? A = please approve.
4. Key Line (why?):
   - can't wait (full problem detail);
   - fixes it, or is the best option (full solution detail);
   - numbers justify it (cost vs savings);
   - [only if supported] extra benefits.

### F. Writing a progress review
1. First review: S = the problem under study (or the proposal's first step); C = the planned first check of Y is done; Q = what turned up? A = the finding.
2. Later reviews: S = what the last review reported; C = what the reader asked to be investigated or done next, now complete; Q = what turned up? A = the finding (e.g. "the shortfall persists, and worsens").

## Templates and patterns
Pattern cards. Each gives **When** it applies, **S/C/Q/A**, the **Key Line** shape, and a **skeleton**. Where the book gives no Key Line for a pattern, the card says so, and any suggestion offered is marked as a Skill note.

### Family W — what to do

**W1 · Solve a problem / decide how to proceed** (B-1 first cell; Exhibit 10 row 1; pp. 37, 217, 218, 220)
- *When:* a task, goal, or existing approach is blocked or threatened, or a fix already tried has failed, and the reader doesn't know what to do.
- *S/C/Q:* S = a goal, situation, or past action; C = it failed, is blocked, or a problem arose; Q = how to proceed?
- *Variants in the book:*
  - task blocked (Exhibit 10);
  - current approach won't hold up (Simmons & Smith, Q = how to change?);
  - fix implemented but failed (Jackson Foods, Q = now what?).
- *Key Line:* not given by the book; the examples give a one-line recommended direction as A.
- > **Skill note:** derive the Key Line from the question the recommendation raises ("how?" → steps per PAT-3; "why?" → reasons).
```
S = Our [current approach / goal / situation] is X
C = [It failed / it's blocked / problem P has emerged]
Q = (What now? / How should we change?)
A = Recommended course: <…>
```

**W2 · Get the desired action** (B-1; p. 217)
- *When:* the reader has a problem and can say what result a fix must produce.
- *S/C/Q:* S = a problem exists; C = the reader needs a fix that delivers X; Q = what action yields that fix?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Problem P exists
C = Any fix must deliver X
Q = What action produces such a fix?
A = <action that delivers X>
```

**W3 · Choose among alternatives** (B-1; Ch. 4 pp. 54–56; Colefax p. 220; App. B pp. 225–226)
- *When:* only when the reader already knows the options (identified or debated them). Otherwise see PAT-9–13.
- *S/C/Q:* S = aim to do X (or X was planned); C = several routes exist (or Y has been floated as better); Q = which?
- *Key Line (three shapes):*
```
(a) Winner best on every criterion        (b) Winner not best on every criterion
Pick C                                     Pick C
 ├─ quicker than A or B                     ├─ C meets every need except …
 ├─ cheaper than A or B                     ├─ A fails because …
 └─ simpler to put in place                 └─ B fails because …

(c) No option gives everything wanted (alternative objectives / R2s)
Depends on your priorities
 ├─ A, if <objective 1> matters most
 ├─ B, if <objective 2> does
 └─ C, if <objective 3> does
```
- *Unknown or insisted-on alternatives:* put them in the introduction (unwieldy) or an appendix chart:
```
              Criterion 1   Criterion 2   Criterion 3
Option A          ✓              –             ✓
Option B          –              ✓             –
Option C          ✓              ✓             ✓
```

**W4 · Audit** (B-1; p. 217)
- *When:* the reader uses a process that has been audited for needed changes.
- *S/C/Q:* S = process P is used to reach X; C = P was audited for needed changes; Q = are changes needed?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
- > **Skill note:** a "yes" naturally leads to the changes as the Key Line, as in the process-change patterns.
```
S = We use process P to reach X
C = P has been audited for needed changes
Q = Do we need changes?
A = <Yes: these changes / No: P is sound>
```

**W5 · Change a practice the reader never questioned** (B-1; p. 217)
- *When:* the writer wants to alter a routine the reader takes for granted.
- *S/C/Q:* S = activity X is coming up; C = choice between carrying on as before and changing; Q = which is wiser?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
- > **Skill note:** structurally a two-option W3, so the W3 shapes apply.
```
S = Activity X is due shortly
C = We can (a) repeat the usual approach or (b) change it in way Y
Q = Which is wiser?
A = <Switch to Y / keep the usual approach>
```

### Family S — whether to go ahead with a plan

**S1 · Is it the right action?** (B-1; Exhibit 10 row 3; pp. 37, 217; DDT p. 229)
- *When:* the reader has a situation or problem, and an action is planned or proposed.
- *S/C/Q:* S = a situation or problem exists; C = an action is planned or proposed; Q = is that action right?
- *Key Line:* not given in B-1. Book examples:
  - Levitt introduction: C = the presumed cause; Q = is the presumption right?; A = no, it's something else.
  - DDT rebuild: A = workable but not yet practical, plus three supporting points.
```
S = Situation/problem P
C = Action A is planned (or proposed)
Q = Is A right?
A = <Yes / No / Yes-but> <one-line verdict>
     ├─ supporting point
     └─ supporting point
```

**S2 · Will there be a problem?** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = problem and solution in hand; C = fear that implementing the solution will cause trouble; Q = will it?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Problem P; fix A in hand
C = Concern that implementing A may cause trouble T
Q = Will it?
A = <verdict>
```

**S3 · Does the solution work?** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = problem and solution in hand; C = the solution has been tested; Q = is it OK?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Problem P; fix A
C = A has been tested
Q = Does A pass?
A = <verdict from the test>
```

**S4 · Will it achieve the objective?** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = an action is being planned; C = it's only worth doing if it achieves Y; Q = will it achieve Y?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Action A planned
C = Only worthwhile if it achieves Y
Q = Will A achieve Y?
A = <verdict>
```

**S5 · Must I change?** (Diffraction Physics, pp. 218–219)
- *When:* a possible threat might force a change to how the reader operates.
- *S/C/Q:* S = our position may be threatened; C = a real threat would force change; Q = is change unavoidable?
- *Example A:* commit now to a major unbundled offering.
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Our position is X; a possible threat T is emerging
C = If T is real we'll have to change; we assessed T
Q = Is change unavoidable?
A = <Yes, do Z now / No>
```

**S6 · Is there a problem at all?** (Anielski Airlines, p. 219)
- *When:* a change is under way and the reader wants to know its impact in order to soften it.
- *S/C/Q:* S = a change is under way; C = the reader wants to blunt its likely harm; Q = what will the impact be?
- *Example A:* far from a problem, the change will be a catalyst for something positive.
- *Placement note:* Appendix B gives this its own heading, and Exhibit B-1 has no matching cell. Filing it under family S is this file's choice.
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
```
S = Change C is under way
C = We want to limit its likely downside
Q = What will the effect really be?
A = <assessment, possibly reframing the change as an opportunity>
```

**S7 · Approval to spend money** (Ch. 4 pp. 51–52)
- *S/C/Q/A:* S = a problem exists; C = a fix is available at cost $N; Q = (approve?); A = please approve.
- *Key Line:* the standard reasons (PAT-5).
```
S = <Problem, told as a story the approver recognises>
C = <It has hit a breaking point>; fix A would solve it at cost $N
Q = (Approve?)
A = Approve this expenditure
     │ why?
     ├─ Action can't wait                 (full problem detail here)
     ├─ This fixes the problem            (or: best of the options examined)
     ├─ The numbers justify it            (cost outweighed by savings, etc.)
     └─ [Only if supported] We gain extra benefits too
```

**S8 · Proposal (B): new or competitive client** (Ch. 4 p. 57; B-1; Exhibit B-3 p. 223)
- *S/C/Q:* S = client's problem in 1–2 sentences; C = client has opted for outside help; Q = (should we hire you?); A = yes, hire us. C and Q are usually left implicit.
- *Key Line:* four reasons, with the approach broken into steps.
```
[Opening paragraph: thanks for meeting about <problem>; here is our proposal to help you <objective>;
 it covers the problem as we see it, how we'd help, our track record, and our terms]

Hire us to solve your problem
 ├─ We grasp your problem                 (Situation-R1-R2, aimed at decision makers' hot buttons)
 ├─ We know how to solve it
 │    ├─ Step one
 │    ├─ Step two
 │    └─ Step three
 ├─ We've applied this technique extensively   (specific/unique expertise)
 └─ Our timing, staffing and fees are sensible
```

### Family H — how something is or was done (Key Line = steps whenever the question is "how?", p. 51)

**H1 · Do a prerequisite action** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = X must be done to solve a problem; C = X depends on first doing Y; Q = how to do Y?
```
S = Solving P requires X
C = X depends on first doing Y
Q = How is Y done?
A = Do Y via: ├─ step 1  ├─ step 2  └─ step 3
```

**H2 · Implement a known solution** (B-1; Exhibit 10 row 2; p. 37; Herzberg HBR p. 38; San Sebastiano p. 219)
- *S/C/Q:* S = a problem exists; C = the fix is known but implementing it is unclear or hard; Q = how to implement?
```
S = Problem P exists
C = Fix A is known, but putting it in place is unclear or hard
Q = How should A be put in place?
A = <implementation approach> → steps
```

**H3 · Explain how it was done** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = a problem existed; C = it was fixed via X; Q = how was X carried out?
```
S = Problem P existed
C = It was resolved through X
Q = How was X carried out?
A = X was accomplished by: steps
```

**H4 · Do something new** (B-1; Ch. 4 p. 53; Role of the Board memo p. 19)
- *S/C/Q:* S = activity X is now required; C = no capability for it yet; Q = how to get set up?
```
S = Activity X is now required of us
C = We lack the capability for it
Q = How do we get equipped?
A = To get equipped:  ├─ step/change 1  ├─ step/change 2  └─ step/change 3
```

**H5 · Do something properly (fix an existing process)** (B-1; Ch. 4 pp. 53–54; = X1)
- *S/C/Q:* S = here is the current system; C = it performs badly; Q = what changes would make it work?
- *Key Line:* the changes revealed by comparing the present and recommended processes (Procedure B).
```
Present process:      step → step → step
Recommended process:  step → step → step → step
S = Today, <task> is done like this: X
C = It isn't delivering (<symptom>)
Q = What should change?
A = <summary of change>
     ├─ change 1 (difference #1 between the processes)
     ├─ change 2
     └─ change 3
```

**H6 · Give direction** (B-1; Ch. 4 pp. 50–51; Exhibit 14)
- *S/C/Q:* S = the writer's aim is X; C = reaching it requires Y from the reader; Q = (how do I deliver Y?). The Q is planted and implicit.
- *Variant:* S = the reader does X; C = X must follow a set method; Q = (what method?).
- *Key Line:* steps, often dated. A roughly reverses C (PAT-4).
```
S = Our aim is X
C = Reaching it requires Y from you
Q = (How do I deliver Y?)
A = Deliver Y
     ├─ Step 1 by <date>
     ├─ Step 2 by <date>
     └─ Step 3 by <date>
```

**H7 · Explain how it works** (B-1; p. 217)
- *S/C/Q:* S = there is an objective; C = a system or process is being installed to meet it; Q = how does it work?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book.
- > **Skill note:** a how-question, so stages or steps fit under PAT-3.
```
S = Our objective is X
C = System/process P is being installed to reach it
Q = How does P operate?
A = P runs in <n> stages: ├─ … ├─ … └─ …
```

**H8 · Proposal (A): known client or formality** (Ch. 4 p. 58; B-1; Exhibit B-2 p. 222; telco example p. 224)
- *S/C/Q:* S = the client's problem in 3–4 paragraphs (Situation-R1-R2); C = the client wants consultants to help fix it; Q = what would that help consist of?
- *Key Line:* the steps of the approach, with experience woven in. Qualifications and timing/staffing/fees sit outside the structure; commercial terms go in a covering letter.
```
S = <Full problem description: situation, R1, R2>
C = You want consultants to help fix it
Q = What would that help consist of?
A = Our plan for solving your problem:
     ├─ Step one
     ├─ Step two
     ├─ Step three
     └─ Step four
[Outside the structure:  Qualifications | Timing, staffing & fees]
```

**H9 · Project plan** (pp. 221–222)
- *When:* explaining to a requesting manager how a problem will be tackled.
- *S/C/Q:* the introduction defines the problem.
- *Key Line:* always organised around the process (steps).
```
S/C = <Problem definition>
Q   = (How will it be solved?)
A   = We will … → step 1 → step 2 → step 3
```

### Family Y — why it happened

**Y1 · Why didn't it work?** (Exhibit 10 row 4; Hayes & Abernathy, p. 39)
- *S/C/Q:* S = an action was taken (or an outcome observed); C = it failed (or the accepted explanation doesn't fit the facts); Q = why?
- *Key Line:* not given by the book. HBR example: A = a single underlying cause.
```
S = We did A   (or: outcome O has occurred)
C = It failed   (or: the usual explanations don't fit O)
Q = Why?
A = <cause>
```

**Y2 · First progress review** (Ch. 4 p. 58; B-1)
- *S/C/Q:* S = the team has been working on problem X (the proposal promised a first step); C = the first check, of Y, is finished; Q = what turned up?
```
S = We've been tackling problem X; our first step was to test Y
C = That test is complete
Q = What turned up?
A = <the finding>
```

**Y3 · Later progress reviews** (Ch. 4 pp. 58–59; B-1)
- *S/C/Q:* S = the last review reported X, and the reader asked for Y next; C = Y is now complete; Q = what turned up?
```
S = Our last review reported X; you asked us to look into Y
C = That work is finished
Q = (What turned up?)
A = <the finding, e.g. "the problem persists, and is worse">
```

### Family X — Describing changes to processes (App. B pp. 226–234)

**X1 · Reader knows the process and its faults** (p. 226) → use H5. Brief S/C; Key Line = changes.

**X2 · Reader knows the old and the new** (pp. 226–229)
- *When:* the reader is familiar with the flawed current process and has a wanted replacement in mind.
- *Q:* how to roll it out, or whether to (the DDT case: is it a sound fix, meaning is it feasible?).
```
S = <Who is concerned> about <current process>; it runs like <brief before-picture> and costs <pain metric>
C = A better way would be <brief after-picture>; you asked us to assess <feasibility / rollout>
Q = (Is it feasible / should we / how do we roll it out?)
A = <Verdict, e.g. "workable, but not yet practical">
     ├─ supporting point
     ├─ supporting point
     └─ recommended next step
```

**X3 · Reader knows little or nothing** (pp. 230–234)
- *When:* approval is needed for changes to a process whose workings, or whose faults, the reader doesn't know.
- *Q:* what changes do you recommend? (and, implicitly, why change at all?)
```
[Reason backward from the changes.]
S = <Accepted fact about who does what, e.g. "as you may know, we recently took over X">
C = <Enough for the reader to see a problem could exist and the fix is plausible,
     e.g. "despite computerisation, figures are often wrong; the cause appears to be Z">
Q = What changes do you recommend?
A = <Summary of what the changes achieve, e.g. "remove the points where errors creep in">
     ├─ Change 1   ── why? → only the faulty current step(s) + how change 1 cures them
     ├─ Change 2   ── why? → …
     └─ Change 3   ── why? → …
```

## Worked examples

**Exhibit 14 (p. 50) — field-sales directive.** S = the meeting will train you to present a new shelf-space program; C = that needs details on one problem chain from each region; Q = (how do I supply them?)
```
Profile one problem chain
 │ how?
 ├─ Pick a suitable chain (first deadline)
 ├─ Gather the data needed (second deadline)
 └─ Organise and send back the data (final deadline)
```
Lesson: directives plant the question. "How?" yields dated steps, and the Answer (produce the profile) cancels the Complication (details are needed).

**Procedures-manual directive (p. 51; text on pp. 43–44).** S = a manual governs activities where inconsistent practice would be harmful, and it needs regular revision; C = consistency demands a common method; Q = (what's the method?). Body = the method. Lesson: the bare form "you do X / it must follow a set method / which method?" makes a two-sentence opening.

**Funding request: departmental backlog (pp. 51–52).**
```
S = Workload up 20%/yr for 4 yrs; staff frozen at 14 by policy → overtime, weekend work, backlog
C = Backlog at 22 weeks, unacceptable to the field, no hours left to add;
    a new computer system costing $N would cut backlog and overtime
Q = (Approve?)
A = Please approve
     ├─ Action can't wait
     ├─ This fixes the problem (or: best of the options)
     ├─ The numbers justify it
     └─ Extra benefits (only if facts support)
```
Lesson: approval requests have a fixed question and a near-fixed Key Line.

**Role of the Board memo (Ch. 2 p. 19, cited p. 53) — doing something new.**
- S = the board must now concentrate on long-range strategy, since a reorganisation has relieved it of day-to-day operations.
- C = long habit of handling short-term operating matters leaves it unequipped for that.
- Q = how does it get equipped?
- Key Line = the changes it should make: pass day-to-day matters to the executive committee; add outside members; formalise internal operating policies.
- Lesson: the H4 pattern. Full analysis in `01-pyramid-fundamentals.md`.

**Exhibit 15 (p. 54) — market forecasting: differences in the processes give the Key Line.**
```
Present process:      July: market forecast → July: 6-month Master Schedule → adjust in monthly meeting
Recommended process:  July: trend forecast → Sept: set inventory-level policies + firm market forecast
                      → 6-month Master Schedule → expert adjusts before monthly meeting → fine-tune in meeting
Recommended structure:
Move forecasting later in the year
 ├─ Set inventory targets to steer scheduling
 ├─ Hold the Master Schedule until September
 └─ Decide monthly revisions through a formal process
```
Lesson: each Key Line point is a difference between the two process pictures.

**Motor options (p. 55) — known alternatives.**
- S = a ruling favours a certain horsepower range for cold-weather drilling, so the largest customer will drop our 10 HP motor for a competitor's smaller one.
- C = three options (price-match our 10 HP to our smaller model; re-engineer our smaller model to equal the rival's; design a new motor in the favoured range).
- Q = which is best?
- Key Line by criteria (quicker / cheaper / simpler than the others) or option by option (C meets every need except…; A fails because…; B fails because…).
- Lesson: options the reader knows can be weighed. Pick the Key Line shape by whether the winner dominates on every criterion. (The book prints the rival's horsepower inconsistently.)

**Alternative objectives (p. 56; p. 226).** "Depends on your priorities" → A for steady sales / B for quick profits / C for labour peace. In App. B: X for earnings stability, Y for rapid growth, Z for labour peace. Lesson: when no action gives everything, offer alternative R2s, not alternative solutions.

**Automotive-aftermarket proposal opening (p. 57) — proposal (B).** A short paragraph: thanks for the meeting about choosing how to attack the automotive aftermarket amid conflicting internal opinions; this document proposes how the firm would help sift the options and build a strategy for fast share gains. C and Q are implicit, and the body follows the four reasons. Lesson: in proposals, the Complication and Question go unstated.

**Capacity progress review (p. 58) — later progress review.**
- S = the previous review reported a capacity shortfall.
- C = the client expected it to be brief because a rival seemed about to fold, and asked for that to be checked; it has been.
- Q = (what turned up?); A = the shortfall remains, and will worsen.
- Lesson: recapping the last exchange in S/C sets up a finding-first answer.

**Simmons & Smith (p. 218) — what to do (W1).**
- S = three products sold into three markets through a small specialist sales force plus some dealers; strong in the core market, weaker elsewhere.
- C = the core market is set to double in three years and the others are growing fast; the small sales force may not cope; dealers are expensive (high commission) and starting to compete with a substitute product.
- Q = (how should the firm protect its core and grow the rest most profitably?).
- A = give each market its own distribution approach.
- Lesson: S = current approach; C = growth and threats make it unsustainable; Q = how to change?

**Diffraction Physics (pp. 218–219) — whether to proceed with what's contemplated (S5).**
- S = the top supplier of scanners into a major retail-systems maker's European installations, premium quality at a premium price.
- C = competitors are beginning to sell cheaper unbundled scanners, which could end the OEM channel and spark price cutting; a market survey gauged the threat and whether to sell direct.
- Q = (must we change?).
- A = commit now to a major unbundled offering to exploit long-term trends.
- Lesson: S = possible problem; C = if real, change is forced. The survey is folded into C.

**City of San Sebastiano (p. 219) — how to achieve what's wanted (H2).**
- S = the city can't generate enough jobs given slow regional recovery, defence cuts, and other drags, and knows it must foster economic development.
- C = it has strengths, but infrastructure gaps deter relocating firms; the client asked what the city can do.
- Q = how to implement the fix?
- A = start with measures local effort can launch.
- Lesson: S = a problem; C = the fix is known but hard to put in place.

**Anielski Airlines (p. 219) — is there a problem? (S6).**
- S = European transport is deregulating (access limits eased, protections removed, paperwork and border checks cut).
- C = debate continues on pace, scope, and mitigation.
- Q = (what will the impact really be?).
- A = far from a problem, deregulation will be the main catalyst for a genuine common market.
- Lesson: the answer can recast the feared change.

**Colefax Supermarkets (p. 220) — which option? (W3).**
- S = a new sales-based replenishment system was conceived as a central mainframe system.
- C = since data entry and use happen at branch level, someone asked whether a branch-based design would be more practical, economical, and flexible, and the client formed a committee.
- Q = which?
- A = branch-based is better.
- Lesson: S = planned X; C = Y floated as better. The reader knew both options.

**Jackson Foods (p. 220) — our fix failed, now what? (W1).**
- S = the supply chain costs $12M a year (14% of sales), high against competitors and inefficient, causing stock-outs, late or incomplete orders, backorders, and lost share.
- C = changed trading terms (larger orders, fewer drop points) barely helped, and carrying on this way will badly damage finances.
- Q = what should we do?
- A = start treating the supply chain as a competitive weapon, pursuing cost and service gains within a long-range aim of becoming the industry's most efficient supplier.
- Lesson: S = had a problem and applied a fix; C = it failed. (Jackson Foods reappears as a storyboard example in Ch. 11; see `11-pyramid-on-screen.md`.)

**Exhibits B-2 / B-3 (pp. 222–223) — proposal by steps vs by reasons.** See cards H8 and S8. Lesson: confirmations to existing clients use steps. Competitive bids use reasons, with the steps nested under "we know how to solve it".

**Telephone-company software proposal (pp. 223–224) — problem definition → steps pyramid.**
```
Problem-definition framework
  Opening scene: client (US telco); telcos in developing countries
  Disturbing event: policy shift — developing-country telcos will now buy software from other telcos
  R1: chance to supply them with in-house-built software → R2: become a profitable vendor of business/admin software
      → chosen route: a joint venture packaging the client's strongest capabilities into product families,
        sold where segments look attractive
  R1': can't tell which opportunities are most attractive → R2': become a profitable vendor
      → route: hire consultants to pinpoint the attractive segments

Pyramid (reading the framework left to right)
S = Software use by developing-world telcos is the quickest-growing part of the global telecom market;
    such telcos once built their own or bought from vendors, but will now buy from other telcos
C = You've chosen to serve this market but can't yet tell which segments are most attractive
    or how to deploy limited resources to win
Q = How would you help us find out?
A = We'll find the best strategy for serving this market
     ├─ Map the market segments and how each buys
     ├─ Forecast yearly spending per application area
     ├─ Establish how suppliers are positioned with prospective customers
     └─ Pinpoint the markets, customers and marketing approaches that best fit your products and capabilities
```
Lesson: the problem-definition framework supplies S and C, and the proposal's Key Line is the steps of the approach. (The book mislabels these as Exhibits B-4/B-5.)

**Exhibit B-4 (pp. 227–229) — DDT: reader knows old and new; "the process is not described".**
- *Before:* sections on the reason for the study and on conclusions. A history of studies and committees, a purpose of "examining the aspects", and conclusions (workable at reasonable cost on a European scale; DDT designed; market forces won't deliver it; a demonstration project is needed; must be an open, standards-based system; more technical studies; non-technical issues).
- *Problem picture:*
  - before = locate document → request it → library finds/copies → posts it (7–10 days);
  - disturbing event = complaints about the delay;
  - R1 = too slow; R2 = document in hand within an hour;
  - solution = deliver electronically (find reference on screen → phone central computer → read document on screen).
- *After:*
```
S = The EEC committee (CIDST) is unhappy with slow access to sci/tech documents over the network:
    finding sources online is quick, but copies by post take 7–10 days
C = Digitising, central storage and electronic delivery would be better; you asked us whether an
    inexpensive system could be built
Q = (Can an inexpensive electronic-delivery system be built?)
A = Feasible, but not yet practical
     ├─ Low unit costs require Europe-wide scale
     ├─ Europe-wide coverage faces many obstacles
     └─ Best route: a demonstration project to build enough demand to clear the obstacles
```
- Lesson: when the reader knows the problem and has a fix in mind, sketch before and after, name the precise "is it a good fix?" question, and answer it.

**Exhibits B-5 / B-6 (pp. 230–234) — Period Graph Books: reader knows little; "the process is over-described".**
- *Before (B-5 memo):*
  - opening: "as you know, we took over four graph books; this memo sets out issues from the handover";
  - a *Production* section walks through four activities;
  - an *Issues* section covers control, the other group's monitor-book process, data keyed in three or more times, and a list of errors;
  - an *Overall assessment* calls the process cumbersome and fragmented, says it needs streamlining that current staff can't manage, and asks to discuss next steps.
  - Skeleton: S = took over responsibility; C = issues emerged; Q = blank; Key Line = our process and its faults / their process and its faults / needs streamlining, let's discuss.
- *B-6 pictures:*
```
Financial Analysis system:
  gather data → generate graph data points → computer input sheet → key into graphics computer
  → computer draws graph → check graph points → place in graph book for presentations → file
  faults: mistakes and late/incomplete figures (gathering/generating); graphs come back too late
          to catch errors; presenter alters graph (unreported)
Monitor-book system (other group):
  download from division computers → key into the group's corporate computer → corporate computer
  calculates data points → (feeds the input-sheet step above)
  faults: data keyed into computers three times; data inconsistent across periods/regions;
          variable cost calculated wrongly; unexplained shifts in previously correct points
Proposed system:
  send data straight to corporate computer → computer generates data points → computer draws
  graphs → finance checks graphs → graph book → presenter edits (looped back for re-checking) → file
```
- *Diagnosis:* graphs are unreliable because errors enter at data entry, at graph-point calculation, and when presenters edit. Putting everything on one computer fixes the first two; disciplining presenters fixes the third.
- *After:*
```
S = As you may know, since Period 5 Corporate Analysis has produced four of the graph books
    (formerly Corporate Planning's job) behind top-management presentations; the other group still does the fifth
C = Though computer-calculated, the figures are often wrong or inconsistent; the root cause seems to be
    two separate, inefficient systems feeding in the data
Q = What changes do you recommend?
A = Remove the points where errors creep in
     ├─ Link divisional data straight into the corporate computer
     ├─ Computerise graph-point generation with a dependable routine
     └─ Require that any presenter edits be revalidated before use
     (each point prompts "why?" → describe only the faulty current steps and how the change cures them)
```
- Lesson: when the reader doesn't know the problem, find the changes through before-after pictures, make them the Key Line, reason backward to a minimal introduction, and describe only the broken steps.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **The Question is often implicit** in directives, funding requests, and proposals (the book parenthesises it throughout), and proposals often leave the Complication implicit too. Implicit in the text never means unknown to the writer (PAT-2).
- **Directives plant rather than remind,** an explicit exception to the "introductions remind" doctrine (p. 50). The planted content is still facts the reader accepts (the writer's goal and need).
- **The no-alternatives doctrine is conditional** (pp. 54, 225): it applies only *if the problem is properly defined*. Options surface when R2 is vague. Two legitimate cases:
  - the reader already knew the options;
  - no action reaches the full R2 (alternative objectives).
- **The approval pattern's second reason changes form** when options were examined: "the best of the options" replaces "this fixes the problem" (p. 52).
- **Proposal structure depends on context,** and Ch. 4 and App. B draw the same line in slightly different terms:
  - Ch. 4: the reasons version for *new clients* (where explaining the problem showcases expertise); the steps version for *well-known clients or formalities*.
  - App. B: *competitive* bids vs *past clients confirming agreed work*.
- **Problem description sits in different places in the two proposal forms.**
  - Reasons form: the introduction carries only 1–2 sentences, and the detailed problem goes in the first Key Line section ("we grasp your problem").
  - Steps form: the 3–4-paragraph description sits in the introduction.
- **Commercial terms move too.** They go in a covering letter in the steps form (p. 58) and appear as the last reason in the reasons form (p. 223). In the steps form, qualifications and fees are "outside the structure of the thinking" (p. 222).
- **Consultants usually answer only the first three questions** (p. 57), yet Exhibit B-1 files progress reviews under the "why did it happen?" family. The book doesn't reconcile the two.
- **Exhibit 10's fourth question is "why not?"** (an action failed), while elsewhere it is "why did it happen?". Both belong to the explanatory family.
- **Process-change memos fail in two opposite ways.** One is under-description: DDT never sketches the process, so the writer can't see the real question. The other is over-description: Period Graph Books narrates every step and offers no fix. The same before-after analysis cures both (p. 227).
- **The Appendix B example introductions put the Answer right after the Complication,** usually as a one-line "we believe…", and tend to fold the reader's request ("you asked us to…", "you formed a committee…") into the Complication.
- **Several B-1 cells come with no example or Key Line** (audit, unquestioned practice, implementation risk, tested solution, objective test, how it was done, how it works). Treat this file's Key Line suggestions for them as Skill notes, not doctrine.
- **Beginner warning (p. 53):** the more familiar a writer is with a process, the more tempting it is to skip drawing it, and the likelier it is that something important gets left out.

> **Skill note:** for a classifier, the strongest signals are: (1) who wants something from whom (writer→reader request = H6; reader→writer approval = S7); (2) whether the reader already holds a candidate solution (S family) or not (W/H families); (3) whether the reader's knowledge of the process is full, old-and-new, or none (X1–X3).
