# Reflecting the Pyramid on the Page

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Introduction to Part Four "Logic in Presentation" (book pp. 168–169) and Ch. 10 "Reflecting the Pyramid on the Page" (book pp. 170–188). Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `PAG`.

## When a skill needs this
- Deciding what physical form a finished pyramid should take (prose memo, dot-dash "lap visual", or slide deck) given message length and audience size.
- Formatting a memo or long report so that its headings, underlining, numbering, or indentation visibly mirror the levels of the pyramid.
- Reviewing a draft's headings and table of contents: single orphan subheadings, non-parallel wording, wordy headings, text that leans on the heading, headings stacked with no introduction, topic-label TOCs ("Background / Findings / Conclusions").
- Writing the connective tissue of a long document: introductions to each Key Line section, backward-referencing links, end-of-section summaries.
- Deciding whether a document needs a conclusion at all, how to write one that is not a lame restatement, and when to add a Next Steps section.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Visual presentation | Ideas shown as slides/graphics on a screen rather than as prose on a page (overheads, 35 mm slides, computer slides, video). |
| Dot-dash memo / lap visual | A page-by-page outline document that a small group reads and discusses together around a table; see dot-dash outline. |
| Highlighting the structure | Using visual devices so the reader's eye sees the pyramid's divisions of thought. |
| Hierarchical headings | Heading styles that move further right (and change form) as ideas become more subordinate; same-level ideas share one visual form. The author's preferred device. |
| Underlined points | Format in which each support point below the Key Line is written out in full and underlined, levels distinguished by numbering style, case, and indentation. |
| Decimal numbering | Numbering divisions (e.g. 5, 5.1, 5.1.1) instead of, or alongside, headings. |
| Indented display | In a short memo, setting a group of parallel points off visually (numbered and indented) from the surrounding prose. |
| Dot-dash outline | Indented outline using numbers, letters, dashes, and dots; used for consultant progress reviews; looser rules. |
| Paragraph point (¶) | A pilcrow used as a bullet for short lists (fewer than five items) or to flag one paragraph for emphasis. |
| Stars / "sunset" | Three asterisks centered on the page, signalling that a long section (or the document) is about to be wrapped up. |
| Invisible fences | The boundaries between groups under different parents; parallel wording is required within a group, not across groups. |
| Table of contents as précis | If headings state real ideas, the TOC alone summarizes the report's argument. |
| Telling a story | Introducing a Key Line section with a fresh, smaller Situation–Complication–Question that leads to the section's point. |
| Referencing backward | Opening a new section by picking up a key word, phrase, or idea from the preceding section. |
| Summarizing sections | Pausing at the end of a long or complex section to restate its main matter and tone before moving on. |
| Making full conclusions | An optional closing that puts the message in perspective and moves the reader to act; usually better omitted. |
| Next Steps | A closing section listing immediate, self-evident actions the reader will take once they accept the recommendation. |
| Grace notes / liaisons | Connective or decorative language; banned in underlined-point and dot-dash formats. |

## Concepts

### Introduction to Part Four: Logic in Presentation (pp. 168–169)
- Once the pyramid's logic is settled, the ideas must be arranged so the reader can *see* the divisions of thought in the hierarchy. This applies equally to prose on a page and to bullets/graphics on a screen.
- Historical note: business documents were once all prose memos or reports. Printing and graphics technology created the "visual presentation" (overhead transparencies, then 35 mm slides with a remote, now computer-made slides and even full-motion video).
- **Choice of form turns on two variables: how long the message is and how many people will receive it.**
  - Short message, one or a few people → written prose memo or report, sent to recipients to read alone.
  - Short message, many people → a "dot-dash memo" or "lap visual", discussed around a table.
  - Long message, large audience → slides, shown by overhead projector or computer.
- Whatever the form, the display must visually reinforce the logic and the relationships among the pyramided ideas. The rationale: the eye perceives the logic before the mind understands it, so what the eye sees should support what the mind takes in.
- Techniques differ depending on whether the reader reads alone from a page (Ch. 10) or sits with others watching a screen while listening to commentary (Ch. 11). Both have rules. Part Four ends with hints on making the sentences themselves convey meaning clearly (Ch. 12).

> **Skill note:** The book lists three length/audience combinations; it does not explicitly cover "long message, few readers". Ch. 10's opening (most documents are prose read alone, long or short) implies a prose report with heavier formatting for that case.

### Chapter opening: the reader alone with the page (pp. 170–171)
- Most documents you write will be prose on a page, read by one person alone. At any length, the goal is for that reader to spot and take in the major ideas as quickly as possible.
- **Target: the reader should have your entire thinking — Introduction, Main Point, and Key Line points — within the first 30 seconds of reading.** They should also be able to tell that the subordinate groups are related, and in what way.
- A long report can display its hierarchy in five usual ways: hierarchical headings; numbered, underlined points; decimal numbering; indented display; and dot-dash outlines. Practitioners disagree strongly about which of the first three best serves a whole report. The author prefers hierarchical headings but presents the others because their advocates have good reasons.
- Whichever device you pick, the objective is the same: make it as easy as possible to grasp the major points and all their grouped support, even in a very long document. That means (1) each formatting level must correspond to a level of abstraction in the argument (Exhibit 53), and (2) you must write transitional phrases that carry the reader smoothly from one grouping to the next where needed.
- **Exhibit 53 mapping (p. 171):** apex "major thought" = title or chapter heading; Key Line boxes = section headings (numbered 1.); next level = subsection headings (1.1); next = numbered paragraphs (1.1.1); next = dash points. The drawing shows sections with different numbers of children (one with three subsections, one with four), i.e. the formatting follows the pyramid's actual branching.

### Highlight the Structure (pp. 171–173)
- **Very short document** — fewer than two paragraphs supporting each Key Line point: simply underline the points (Exhibit 54). They will jump out at the eye.
- **Longer document** — more than a paragraph or two per Key Line point: state the Key Line points in the introduction, then echo each one with a heading over its section (Exhibit 55).

### Hierarchical Headings (pp. 173–176)
**Technique.** Put the marks for increasingly subordinate ideas further and further to the right, and give all ideas at the same level the same visual form (Exhibit 56). Major ideas get section headings at the left margin; their divisions get subsection headings; their divisions get numbered paragraphs; and so on. You need not copy the book's exact styles, but whatever style you use, every heading must stand for a division of thought.

**Exhibit 56 conventions (p. 173)** — the book's sample page, which describes its own rules:
- *Chapter heading*: numbered, centered, upper case, underlined. Worded to state the major thought the chapter develops. Directly beneath a chapter heading (or title), the opening paragraphs make the major idea plain and supply any other context, so that both parties are "standing in the same place" when the point arrives and its plan of development is announced. Later chapter headings are worded in parallel style. The major divisions to come may be listed with paragraph points (¶) or another mark.
- *Section heading*: upper case, underlined, flush left. Worded to state the idea the section develops; the first section heading parallels the others. A section divides either into subsections or, if its points are short, into numbered paragraphs. The principal ideas of the subsections are introduced beforehand, optionally as a ¶ list.
- *Subsection heading*: indented, upper and lower case, underlined. Worded to state its principal thought; parallel in style with its siblings. Further division uses numbered paragraphs.
- *Numbered paragraph*: indented further; its first sentence or opening phrase may be underlined to highlight that the numbered points are alike. A point may run more than one paragraph, but try to develop it in no more than three.
- *Dash-point paragraph*: indented further; divides the thought inside a numbered paragraph.
- *Dot points*: indented further still; you will rarely break an idea down this far.
- *Stars* (three asterisks centered): signal that a long section's closing remark comes next.
- *Paragraph point (¶)*: used to set out lists of fewer than five items (e.g. the section headings to come), or to make one paragraph carrying an important point stand out. ¶ paragraphs are written in block form and kept as short as possible.

**The six rules for headings (pp. 174–175):**
1. **Never use only one of any element.** Headings mark levels of abstraction in the pyramid, and a pyramid level can never hold just one item. So never one section, one subsection, one numbered paragraph, or one dash point. Don't drop in a heading for looks, the way newspapers and magazines break up print. A heading signals that its idea is one of a group whose members jointly explain or defend the idea above.
2. **Show parallel ideas in parallel form.** Every idea in a group belongs to one kind; stress that by giving each heading the same grammatical form. If the first section heading starts with a verb, all must; if the first subsection heading in a group starts with an "-ing" word, all in that group must. Parallelism applies *within* a group, not *between* groups: the subsections under one section may start with "To …" while those under the next section start with "-ing" words, because invisible fences separate the groups under different parents.
3. **Cut the wording to the thought's essence.** Headings are reminders, not the main event; keep them as concise as possible. (Lengthening a section heading with a "to provide …" purpose clause is given as too wordy.)
4. **Regard headings as outside the text.** Headings serve the eye more than the mind; readers skim them, so they can't carry the message. Don't write text that depends on the heading (e.g. starting the body with "This action will …" where "this action" is the heading). The first sentence under a heading should itself signal the turn to a new topic, and the whole document should read smoothly with every heading removed. **Exception:** numbered paragraphs, which the reader takes in as running prose.
5. **Introduce each group of headings.** Before a group of headings, state the point the group will explain or defend and name the ideas to come. Skipping this turns the section into a mystery story: the reader can't tell what the points add up to until the end, and by then may have forgotten how it began. Therefore a section heading must never come immediately after the title, and a subsection heading must never come immediately after a section heading.
6. **Don't overdo.** The author calls this perhaps the most important rule. Add a heading only when it genuinely helps the reader track how your thinking subdivides. Often nothing below the section-heading level is needed or useful.

**Table of contents as précis (pp. 175–176).** Well-formulated headings, collected in the TOC, become a précis of the report — another aid to the reader. This works only if the pyramid's boxes contain real ideas. A TOC of the form Introduction / Background / Findings / Conclusions / Recommendations communicates nothing. "Introduction" and "Background" headings are generally unnecessary anyway: they overlap (both hold introductory material) and they are redundant (the opening paragraphs could hardly be anything else). Headings exist to show divisions of thought, and in that sample the thought doesn't start until the Key Line, which there (in theory) begins at "Findings". The point is where the real thinking starts; it does not make "Findings" an acceptable heading, since the whole TOC is condemned as uninformative.

### Underlined Points (pp. 176–177)
- **Technique:** write out the whole statement of each support point below the Key Line and underline it. Lower-level points are also written in full and underlined, but distinguished by numbering form and indentation (Exhibit 57).
- **Exhibit 57 layout (p. 176):**
  - Title: centered, upper case, underlined, stating the main point.
  - One or two paragraphs of situation; one or two of complication plus question (the question may be left implicit).
  - The main point; then, if the whole document exceeds about seven paragraphs, the Key Line points as ¶ bullets.
  - A heading matching the first Key Line point (upper case, underlined, flush left).
  - A brief lead-in that arrives at and restates the section's point; if the section exceeds about seven paragraphs, list its points.
  - Level 1: `1.` at the margin; support point in UPPER CASE, underlined.
  - Level 2: `(1)` indented; Upper and Lower Case, underlined.
  - Level 3 (very long documents only): `1.` without parentheses, indented further; Upper and Lower Case, underlined.
  - Level 4: indented with a dot; only the first word capitalized.
  - Level 5: indented with a dash; only the first word capitalized.
- The author finds this form ugly. With computers, many writers put the major points in **bold** and keep underlining for the lower-level points, which looks better (p. 177 shows levels 1–2 bold and level 3 underlined).
- **Purpose:** speed and ease. The reader can skim only the major points and still get the whole message. Easy for the reader, but it imposes strict rules on the writer:
  1. **Absolute question/answer discipline.** Each point must answer precisely the question its parent point provokes, and go no further. There is no room for graceful verbal links or amplification; they spoil the clean, bare display of the logic. Any amplification or background must go in the introductory or concluding paragraphs of each section.
  2. **Word each point as sparely as possible.** If the reader must get through 30 words before reaching the point, the format's ease is lost. More than about a dozen words, or more than one subject and predicate, means rethink.
  3. **Limit the points strictly to the outline of your deductive or inductive argument.** Most people ignore this and just list points. A chained deductive argument never has more than four points; an inductive one never more than five. If you exceed that, you have probably missed a chance to group, so rethink.

### Decimal Numbering (pp. 177–179)
- Many companies and most government bodies number the subdivisions instead of (or as well as) using headings; some number every paragraph. The claimed advantage is precise reference to any topic or recommendation.
- **Drawbacks:** a page dense with index numbers keeps pulling the reader's attention off the substance; and any later edit that removes a paragraph may force renumbering of everything after it, an annoyance that word processors don't eliminate.
- **Recommendation:** if you want numbering as a quick guide, use it *with* hierarchical headings, not *instead of* them. Headings convey the gist during reading and jog the reader's memory on a return visit days later. Likewise, a cross-reference that names its topic ("Section 4.1 on manufacturing profits") beats a bare number ("Section 4.1"): with the topic named, the reader already knows roughly what to expect before flipping there, instead of finding out only on arrival.
- **Exhibit 58 (p. 178)** shows the target look, using the opening of Ch. 5 of Antony Jay's *Effective Presentation*: a centered, numbered chapter heading (5.) with introductory paragraphs; section headings in capitals with numbers (5.1, 5.2) at the margin, each followed by text; subsection headings in upper/lower case, indented, numbered 5.1.1–5.1.3.
- **Two numbering schemes (p. 179):**
  - Common: `I.` → `1.1` → `1.1.1` → `1.1.1.1`.
  - Probably simpler: `I.` → `1.` → `a.` → `i.`
  - (Both illustrated with one four-level chain: no other animal will suffer to the death for its master as a dog will → other animals run when danger nears → the dog stays → even if it means death.)
- **Number only real divisions of thought.** Do not number paragraphs in the opening introduction, in concluding summaries, in linking comments, or in the introductions to subpoints.

### Indented Display (pp. 179–180)
- For documents too short for headings or decimal numbering. You are still dealing with groups of ideas and should still make them visible.
- A cluster of points that together back up or explain one idea is always easier to take in when visibly set off as a group.
- **Main rule:** express the set-off ideas in the same grammatical form. This usually saves words, makes the ideas easier to grasp, and lets *you* check whether you said what you meant (in the book's example it exposed that the writer never said what kind of slides were wanted for one section).
- Whether the memo is long or short, visually grouping similar ideas makes them easier to understand. But, as with headings, use only one set of indented groupings per memo; more than that weakens the effect.

### Dot-Dash Outlines (pp. 180–181)
- A variant of indented display, also called the lap visual, used mainly by consulting firms for **progress reviews**: a small group of client executives sit around a table and go through the document together, a page at a time.
- Same technique: more subordinate ideas sit further to the right.
- **Looser rules:** you need not insist on at least two points at every level. The aim is to put the main points before the reader in an easy-to-see way without giving too much at once, so the group reads slowly and digests and discusses fully.
- The book's sample page "Format for Progress Reviews" (p. 181) is written in the format and states its rules:
  - Use it when you'll be present while the client reads and you want to provoke discussion, so you hear the client's reaction to the findings on the spot and can keep the work heading where it should; lay the ideas out so the client quickly picks up the main points, sees how they connect, and can separate the minor points from the major.
  - Rules: (a) short, direct statements at each level, without grace notes or liaisons; (b) one statement only per level; (c) parallel construction for same-level ideas where possible; (d) each idea relates directly to the level above it, either explaining it or supporting it.
  - Don't bother with the format unless you mean to obey the rules.
- **Why bother with any of these devices (p. 181):** they let the reader's eye see the logic that the reader's mind is working to untangle, which speeds understanding. Each device saves only seconds, but for an executive handling dozens of documents a day, those seconds accumulate.

### Show Transitions Between Groups (pp. 182–188)
- After the opening introduction, give **each Key Line point its own brief introduction**. In longer documents also stop now and then, as a major grouping opens or closes, to remind the reader what ground has been covered and what comes next.
- Make these moves smooth and non-mechanical. Avoid "this chapter covered X; the next chapter covers Y". Relate what the sections *say* (their major ideas), not what they *do*. The transition should look both ways at once: back to what was said and forward to what comes.
- At the start of a chapter, section, or subsection use one of two techniques: **telling a story** or **referencing backward**. If chapters or sections are long, also pause at the end and **summarize** before moving on.

#### Telling a Story (pp. 182–183)
- Lead the reader into each Key Line point with a new Situation–Complication–Question story whose Answer is that Key Line point. This is the same technique as in Ch. 4's Total Quality Management paper (Exhibit 59).
- The section stories reuse the SCQ form of the opening introduction but **shrink its scope to where the reader is standing at the start of each new story** (i.e. each story assumes everything said so far).
- **Wherever an introductory story appears, it must contain only things the reader already knows or will accept as true.**

#### Referencing Backward (pp. 183–185)
- Take a key word, phrase, or idea from the part of the pyramid you are leaving and reuse it in the first sentence of the part you are entering. This is the familiar between-paragraph technique (e.g. a paragraph saying no executive is responsible full time for group affairs is followed by one whose opening sentence names that lack of full-time leadership and adds that overlapping or unwieldy responsibility assignments make the resulting problems worse).
- The identical move works when opening a section, a subsection, or occasionally a fresh set of support points (Exhibit 60).
- Aim: transitions that are unobtrusive yet clear, mainly by carrying the key word or phrase forward. The word is carried toward the next section's main point, which the "explanation" part of the opening introduction has already flagged briefly. That is why no story is needed here: the reader has already been given enough to follow the point.
- You **still** have to preview the ideas each section will contain and show how they back up its point.

#### Summarizing Sections (p. 185)
- When a chapter or section is very long or complicated, stop and summarize fully before going on (the book cites its own recap closing Ch. 4's first section, p. 48).
- A closing summary should deftly re-express both the main substance *and the tone* of what preceded it. Both already sit in your pyramid, so the job is just to reassemble them for the reader.

#### Making Full Conclusions (pp. 185–187)
- In theory a document with a proper introduction and a body that obeys the pyramid rules needs no conclusion: the reader's question was stated at the start and fully answered. But writers often feel an urge to close with some grace instead of just stopping (hence "please don't hesitate to call if you have questions" on short memos).
- The obvious (perhaps too obvious) long-document ending: a centered line of asterisks (a "sunset"), then a last paragraph opening "In conclusion" that reminds the reader of the main point. If you do this, avoid a lame restatement of what is already clear (e.g. "this report has outlined our recommendations and the steps each department must take").
- Instead find compelling words that both sum up and stir an appropriate emotion (Aristotle's advice for conclusions). For a business document, the feeling to leave is a need and desire to act. So signal what the reader might now consider, or now act on, given what they have learned.
- The indication may take the form of a **philosophical insight**, a **prescription for immediate action**, or both; Lincoln's close to his second inaugural address does both.
- Calibrate subtlety and restraint to subject and reader. An airline president would bristle at emotional language urging a new planning system, but would be receptive to emotion on an issue they already care deeply about (e.g. re-regulation of the airline industry).
- In general, if you insist on a conclusion, write something that puts the significance of the message in perspective (see the European information-retrieval example below).
- The author discourages most people from writing concluding paragraphs at all, because they are so hard to do well; pragmatism says leave them out.

#### Stating Next Steps (p. 187)
- The one case where you definitely need a closing section: when you want the reader to take actions in the immediate future.
- Typical trigger: a long document recommending a course of action the reader will probably take. Once the reader decides to go ahead, certain things have to happen "Monday morning"; collect them under a heading **Next Steps**.
- **The only rule: Next Steps may contain only actions the reader will not question**, i.e. logically obvious ones. If an action would prompt a question, it belongs in the body of the document and must fit horizontally and vertically with the rest of the argument.

#### Why transitions matter (pp. 187–188)
- The purpose of all these placement devices is to reduce the thinking the reader must do. Readers seldom have analytical training, and they know the subject far less well than the writer, even when the subject is their own firm; they cannot be expected to decode your reasoning as an equal would.
- Assume, then, that the reader's attention has wandered by the time one long group of points ends and the next begins. Transitions catch that attention and steer it back. The author frames this as basic courtesy, with two conditions: do it with grace, and do it only at the points that need it.

## Rules
- **PAG-1** — Choose the presentation form from message length and audience size: short/few → prose memo or report read alone; short/many → dot-dash memo (lap visual) discussed at a table; long/large audience → slides. *Why:* each form suits a different reading situation. *Check:* name the length and audience; confirm the chosen form matches. (pp. 168–169)
- **PAG-2** — Make the visual layout reinforce the pyramid's logic, whatever the form. *Why:* the eye sees the logic before the mind grasps it. *Check:* does the layout alone (indentation, heading styles) reveal which ideas are major and which support them? (p. 169)
- **PAG-3** — Arrange the document so the reader has the Introduction, Main Point, and all Key Line points within roughly the first 30 seconds of reading. *Why:* the reader should take in your whole thinking at once and see how the groups relate. *Check:* time-box a skim of the opening; can you state the main point and every Key Line point? (p. 170)
- **PAG-4** — Apply formatting levels to match the pyramid's levels of abstraction: title = apex; sections = Key Line; subsections = next level; numbered paragraphs, then dash points, below. *Why:* format is meant to show the divisions of thought. *Check:* map each heading level to a pyramid level; no level should be skipped or mixed. (p. 171, Exh. 53)
- **PAG-5** — If each Key Line point has fewer than two supporting paragraphs, just underline (or otherwise highlight) the points in the text; if more than a paragraph or two, list the Key Line points in the introduction and give each its own heading. *Why:* the points must jump out at the eye at whatever length. *Check:* count paragraphs per Key Line point and confirm the device used. (pp. 171–172, Exh. 54–55)
- **PAG-6** — Indent increasingly subordinate ideas further to the right and give every idea at the same level the same visual form. *Why:* position and form are how the eye reads hierarchy. *Check:* any two same-level items formatted differently, or a subordinate item not indented further, is a fault. (p. 174, Exh. 56)
- **PAG-7** — Never use only one of any element at a level: no lone section, subsection, numbered paragraph, or dash point; never add a heading for visual relief. *Why:* a heading asserts that its idea is one of a group jointly supporting the idea above; a pyramid level can't hold one item. *Check:* count siblings under every parent; any count of one fails. (p. 174)
- **PAG-8** — Word same-level headings in the same grammatical form within each group; parallelism need not carry across groups with different parents. *Why:* sameness of form signals sameness of kind; invisible fences separate groups. *Check:* compare the first word/grammatical pattern of every sibling heading. (p. 174)
- **PAG-9** — Keep headings to the essence of the thought. *Why:* headings remind, they don't dominate. *Check:* strip purpose clauses and qualifiers; if the heading still identifies the idea, the extra words go. (p. 174)
- **PAG-10** — Treat headings as outside the text: the first sentence under a heading must itself announce the new topic, and no sentence may lean on the heading for its meaning; numbered paragraphs are the exception. *Why:* readers glance at headings without reading them carefully. *Check:* delete all headings (except numbered-paragraph lead-ins) and read; it must still flow and make sense. (p. 175)
- **PAG-11** — Introduce every group of headings by stating the point the group supports and the ideas to come; never put a section heading directly under the title or a subsection heading directly under a section heading. *Why:* otherwise the section is a mystery story whose point emerges only at the end. *Check:* look for two headings with no text between them, or a group with no preview of its members. (p. 175)
- **PAG-12** — Don't overdo headings; use them only where they help the reader hold the subdivisions; often stop at section headings. *Why:* the author calls this the most important heading rule. *Check:* for each heading level below sections, ask whether removing it would hurt comprehension. (p. 175)
- **PAG-13** — Make headings state real ideas so the table of contents reads as a précis; drop generic labels such as Introduction, Background, Findings, Conclusions, Recommendations. *Why:* headings show divisions of thought, and generic labels carry none (and "Introduction"/"Background" overlap and state the obvious). *Check:* read only the TOC; can you reconstruct the argument? (pp. 175–176)
- **PAG-14** — Under a chapter heading or title, open with paragraphs that state the major idea, put reader and writer in the same place, and preview how the idea will be developed (optionally listing divisions with ¶ points). *Why:* the reader must know the point and the plan before the detail. *Check:* the first paragraphs after the title contain the governing thought and a preview of its divisions. (p. 173)
- **PAG-15** — Develop a numbered paragraph's point in no more than about three paragraphs; underline its first sentence or opening phrase if that helps show the points are alike. *Why:* keeps numbered points digestible and visibly parallel. *Check:* count paragraphs per numbered point. (p. 173)
- **PAG-16** — Use ¶ points only for lists of fewer than five items or to flag a single paragraph for emphasis; keep ¶ paragraphs short and in block form. Use three centered stars to signal that a long section's wrap-up follows. *Why:* these marks have specific signalling jobs. *Check:* any ¶ list of five or more items fails. (p. 173)
- **PAG-17** — In the underlined-points format, each point must answer precisely the question its parent provokes, and no more; put all amplification and background in each section's introductory or concluding paragraphs. *Why:* connective language spoils the bare display of logic the format exists for. *Check:* for each point ask "does this directly answer the question its parent raises, and only that?" (p. 177)
- **PAG-18** — In the underlined-points format, keep each point to about a dozen words and one subject-predicate. *Why:* the reader must not wade through 30 words to find the point. *Check:* word count; count clauses. (p. 177)
- **PAG-19** — In the underlined-points format, limit the points to the outline of a deductive (at most four points in a chain) or inductive (at most five points) argument. *Why:* more points mean a missed grouping. *Check:* count points per group; above the limit, look for a regrouping. (p. 177)
- **PAG-20** — When the document (or a section) runs longer than about seven paragraphs, state its Key Line (or section) points right after the main point. *Why:* the reader needs the map before the territory. *Check:* long document/section without a stated list of its points fails. (p. 176, Exh. 57)
- **PAG-21** — If you use decimal numbering, use it with hierarchical headings, not instead of them, and cite sections by number plus topic. *Why:* numbers alone interrupt concentration, convey no gist, and complicate amendments. *Check:* every numbered division has a worded heading; cross-references name the topic. (pp. 177–178)
- **PAG-22** — Number only true divisions of thought; never number introductions, concluding summaries, linking comments, or introductions to subpoints. *Why:* numbering must mirror the actual divisions. *Check:* every numbered unit corresponds to a pyramid box. (p. 179)
- **PAG-23** — In short memos, set off a group of supporting points by indenting/numbering them, and write all of them in the same grammatical form. *Why:* groups are easier to absorb when visibly grouped; parallel form saves words and exposes gaps in what you meant. *Check:* every item in the set starts with the same grammatical construction; an item that won't fit the pattern signals unclear thinking. (pp. 179–180)
- **PAG-24** — Use only one set of indented groupings per memo. *Why:* repeated groupings dilute the visual effect. *Check:* count indented groupings. (p. 180)
- **PAG-25** — In dot-dash outlines: short direct statements without grace notes or liaisons; one statement per level; parallel construction at a level where possible; every idea explains or supports the one above. Don't use the format unless you will follow these rules. (The two-of-every-level rule is relaxed.) *Why:* the page is read together, slowly, to provoke discussion. *Check:* each line is a single short statement tied to its parent. (pp. 180–181)
- **PAG-26** — Give every Key Line point a brief introduction of its own; in long documents pause at the start or end of each major grouping to show where the reader has been and is going. *Why:* the reader's mind drifts between long groups and must be brought back. *Check:* every Key Line section opens with prose before any subheading. (p. 182)
- **PAG-27** — Make transitions relate what sections *say* (their ideas), not what they *do*; look back and forward at once; never write "this chapter covered X, the next covers Y". *Why:* mechanical signposting carries no ideas. *Check:* search for "this section/chapter has …", "the next section will …". (p. 182)
- **PAG-28** — When a Key Line section needs to be led into, tell a mini Situation–Complication–Question story, scoped to where the reader now stands, whose answer is that section's point; include only facts the reader knows or will accept. *Why:* it is the same reader-engagement mechanism as the opening introduction. *Check:* identify S, C, Q in the section opener; confirm S contains nothing contestable. (pp. 182–183, Exh. 59)
- **PAG-29** — Otherwise link sections by referencing backward: pick up a key word, phrase, or idea from the preceding part in the new part's opening sentence. *Why:* unobtrusive yet clear continuity; no story needed when the original introduction already flagged the point. *Check:* the opening sentence of each section/subsection echoes a term from the one before. (pp. 183–185, Exh. 60)
- **PAG-30** — After a backward-reference link, still preview the ideas the section contains and show how they back up its point. *Why:* the link connects; the introduction previews. *Check:* each section opener names its supporting points. (p. 185)
- **PAG-31** — End a very long or complex section with a full summary that restates its principal content and tone, drawn from the pyramid. *Why:* the reader needs consolidation before moving on. *Check:* long sections end with a paragraph restating their point and its basis. (p. 185)
- **PAG-32** — Default to no concluding paragraph. If you add one, don't merely restate; put the message's significance in perspective and leave the reader wanting to act, via a philosophical insight and/or a prescription for immediate action, pitched to subject and reader. *Why:* conclusions are hard to do well, and a lame restatement adds nothing. *Check:* delete the conclusion; if nothing is lost, leave it out; if kept, it should say what the reader can now think or do. (pp. 185–187)
- **PAG-33** — When the reader is likely to act on a recommendation, add a Next Steps section listing only immediate, self-evident actions; any action the reader might question goes in the body, fitted into the logic. *Why:* Next Steps is outside the argument and can't carry anything that needs defending. *Check:* for each step ask "could the reader reasonably ask why?" If yes, move it. (p. 187)
- **PAG-34** — Use transition devices gracefully and only where needed. *Why:* they are good manners toward a reader who isn't your peer on the subject; overuse is clutter. *Check:* each transition is at a group boundary where the reader would otherwise lose the thread. (pp. 187–188)

## Procedures

### P1. Choose the presentation form (pp. 168–169)
1. Estimate message length (short vs long) and audience (one/few vs many).
2. Short + one/few → prose memo or report, sent to be read alone → continue with P2.
3. Short + many, discussed together → dot-dash memo / lap visual (P2, dot-dash branch).
4. Long + large audience → slides → use `11-pyramid-on-screen.md`.
5. Whatever the form, verify the layout reveals the pyramid (PAG-2).

### P2. Choose the page-formatting device (pp. 170–181)
1. Count paragraphs supporting each Key Line point.
   - Fewer than two each → underline/highlight the Key Line points in the text (Exh. 54). Stop.
2. If the memo is short but contains a group of parallel support points → indented display (numbered, set off, same grammatical form; one grouping per memo).
3. If the document will be read page-by-page with you present, to provoke discussion (progress review) → dot-dash outline (PAG-25).
4. If it is a longer report:
   - Default (author's preference) → hierarchical headings (P3).
   - Organization requires numbering → decimal numbering *plus* worded headings (PAG-21, PAG-22).
   - Reader wants to skim support points only, and you can meet strict Q/A, brevity, and argument-limit rules → underlined points (PAG-17 to PAG-19), optionally bold for upper levels.
5. Whatever you pick, apply it consistently and only where it helps (PAG-12).

### P3. Put hierarchical headings on a pyramid (pp. 171–176)
1. Map levels: apex → title/chapter heading; Key Line → section headings; next level → subsection headings; next → numbered paragraphs; below that → dash points (rarely dot points).
2. Decide how deep to go: often stop at section headings (PAG-12). Use subsections only where a section has substantial divisions.
3. Write each heading as the idea in that pyramid box, cut to its essence (PAG-9, PAG-13).
4. Check sibling count ≥ 2 at every level (PAG-7).
5. Make each sibling group grammatically parallel (PAG-8).
6. Write an introduction under the title and under every heading that has child headings: state the point, then preview the child ideas (PAG-11, PAG-14).
7. Write the first sentence under every heading so it stands without the heading (PAG-10).
8. Read the TOC alone: it should summarize the argument (PAG-13).
9. Read the body with headings hidden: it should flow (PAG-10).

### P4. Write the transitions in a long document (pp. 182–188)
1. For each Key Line section, decide the opener:
   - Does the reader need context to see why this point arises (i.e. it wasn't already set up)? → tell a mini-SCQ story ending in the section's point (PAG-28).
   - Was the point already flagged in the original introduction's preview, so the reader already has enough to follow? → reference backward: pick up the key word/phrase of the previous section in the opening sentence (PAG-29).
2. In either case, then introduce the section's group of supporting ideas and how they support its point (PAG-30).
3. Apply the backward-reference move also between subsections and, where useful, between support points.
4. Check that no transition describes what sections *do*; rewrite to state what they *say* (PAG-27).
5. At the end of any very long or complex section, write a summary restating its content and tone (PAG-31).

### P5. Decide how to end the document (pp. 185–187)
1. Will the reader need to take immediate, obvious actions upon accepting the recommendation? → write a Next Steps section (PAG-33). Test each step: could the reader question it? If yes, move it into the body.
2. Otherwise, default to no conclusion (PAG-32).
3. If you still want one: optionally signal it with centered asterisks; write a closing that puts the significance in perspective and moves the reader toward action (insight and/or immediate prescription), pitched to how strongly the reader already feels about the subject. Reject any closing that only restates.

### P6. Audit a draft's on-page structure (whole chapter)
1. Skim the first 30 seconds' worth: are Introduction, Main Point, Key Line all visible? (PAG-3)
2. Extract the heading tree; verify level mapping, sibling counts, parallelism, concision (PAG-4, 7, 8, 9).
3. Look for stacked headings with no text between them (PAG-11).
4. Read text with headings removed (PAG-10).
5. Check numbering covers only real divisions (PAG-22).
6. Check each section opener for story/backward-reference and a preview of its points (PAG-26 to PAG-30).
7. Check the ending (PAG-32, PAG-33).

## Diagnostics
| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Reader must read several pages before learning the main point and the Key Line | Structure not surfaced at the top | State intro, main point, and Key Line points up front; list Key Line if long (PAG-3, PAG-20) |
| A section with exactly one subsection, or one dash point under a numbered paragraph | Heading inserted for looks; not a real grouping | Merge into parent or find the missing sibling (PAG-7) |
| Sibling headings mix verbs, "-ing" forms, and noun phrases | Ideas not recognized as the same kind | Recast siblings in one grammatical form (PAG-8) |
| Heading is a full sentence with purpose clause | Heading doing the text's job | Cut to the essence (PAG-9) |
| Body text starts "This will …" / "It is …" referring to the heading | Text depends on heading | Rewrite opening sentence to name the topic (PAG-10) |
| Title followed immediately by "1. …" heading, or section heading immediately followed by subsection heading | Group not introduced; mystery story | Insert introduction stating the point and previewing the subpoints (PAG-11, PAG-14) |
| Headings at four or five levels in a modest report | Overformatting | Drop lower levels (PAG-12) |
| TOC reads Introduction / Background / Findings / Conclusions / Recommendations | Boxes hold topics, not ideas | Rewrite headings as idea statements; drop Intro/Background headings (PAG-13) |
| Underlined points run 25–30 words with multiple clauses | Points not distilled | Cut to ≤ ~12 words, one subject-predicate (PAG-18) |
| Eight or nine underlined points under one parent | Missed grouping | Regroup into ≤5 inductive / ≤4 deductive (PAG-19) |
| Every paragraph numbered, including introduction and summaries | Numbering not tied to divisions of thought | Number only pyramid boxes; add worded headings (PAG-21, PAG-22) |
| Cross-reference says only "see 4.1" | Reader can't anticipate content | Add topic: "4.1 on …" (PAG-21) |
| Short memo buries four parallel requests in run-on paragraphs | Group not displayed | Indented display, parallel form (PAG-23) |
| Indented list items in mixed grammatical forms, or one item vague | Unclear thinking hidden by prose | Force parallel form; the misfit reveals the gap (PAG-23) |
| "This chapter looked at A. The next chapter looks at B." | Mechanical transition describing what, not saying what | Reference backward to the idea; preview the next idea (PAG-27, PAG-29) |
| Section opens straight into detail with no lead-in | Missing section introduction | Mini-SCQ story or backward reference plus preview (PAG-26, PAG-28, PAG-30) |
| Section-opening "story" contains new or contestable claims | Story misused | Restrict S to what the reader knows/accepts (PAG-28) |
| Long, complex section ends abruptly | No consolidation | Add summary of content and tone (PAG-31) |
| Ending: "This report has outlined our recommendations …" | Lame restatement | Delete, or replace with significance/call to act (PAG-32) |
| Next Steps include debatable actions (e.g. a new strategy) | Argument smuggled outside the pyramid | Move to body, fit into logic; keep only obvious actions (PAG-33) |

## Templates and patterns

### Heading hierarchy (Exhibit 56 style)
```
                      1  CHAPTER HEADING IN CAPS (centered, numbered, underlined)

Opening paragraph(s): state the chapter's major idea; give whatever the reader
needs so you are "standing in the same place"; say how the idea will be developed.
   ¶ First major thought to come
   ¶ Second major thought to come

SECTION HEADING IN CAPS (flush left, underlined)
Introductory paragraph: the section's point + the subsections to come.
   ¶ First subpoint ahead
   ¶ Second subpoint ahead

     Subsection Heading in Upper and Lower Case (indented, underlined)
     Introductory text for the subsection.

       1. Numbered paragraph. Opening phrase may be underlined. Develop in ≤ 3 paragraphs.
          – Dash-point paragraph dividing the numbered paragraph's thought
              • Dot point (rarely needed)
                               *   *   *
     (three centered stars → a long section's closing remark follows)
```

### Underlined-points layout (Exhibit 57 style)
```
              TITLE STATING THE MAIN POINT (centered, caps, underlined)
Situation paragraph(s).
Complication and question paragraph(s) (question may be implied).
Main point. If document > ~7 paragraphs:
   ¶ First Key Line point
   ¶ Second Key Line point
   ¶ Third Key Line point
HEADING MATCHING FIRST KEY LINE POINT (caps, underlined, flush left)
Brief lead-in arriving at and restating the section's point; if section > ~7
paragraphs, list its points.
1.   SUPPORT POINT IN CAPS, UNDERLINED, AT THE MARGIN
     (1)  Next-Level Point, Indented, Parenthesized Number, Upper/Lower Case, Underlined
          1.  Next Level (very long documents only), No Parentheses, Underlined
              • Next level with a dot, only first word capitalized
                 – Next level with a dash, only first word capitalized
(Modern variant: bold for levels 1–2, underlining for lower levels.)
```

### Decimal numbering with headings (Exhibit 58 style)
```
                    5. CHAPTER HEADING
Introductory paragraphs (unnumbered).
5.1 SECTION HEADING
Introductory paragraph (unnumbered).
     5.1.1 Subsection heading
     5.1.2 Subsection heading
     5.1.3 Subsection heading
5.2 SECTION HEADING
```
Numbering schemes:
```
Common:            Simpler:
I.                 I.
  1.1                1.
    1.1.1              a.
      1.1.1.1            i.
```
Never number: opening introduction · concluding summaries · linking comments · introductions to subpoints.

### Indented display in a short memo
```
<One or two sentences of context>. For <purpose>, I will need <noun>:
   1. <Parallel phrase, bold>. <Elaborating sentence(s).>
   2. <Parallel phrase, bold>. <Elaborating sentence(s).>
   3. <Parallel phrase, bold>. <Elaborating sentence(s).>
```

### Dot-dash outline (progress review)
```
1. Short direct statement (no grace notes or liaisons)
   a. Statement explaining or supporting 1
      – Statement explaining or supporting a
         • Statement explaining or supporting the dash
   b. Parallel to a
2. Parallel to 1
```

### Section-opening frames
```
Story opener (mini-SCQ, scoped to where the reader now stands):
  S: <What the reader now knows/has done, including the previous section's point>.
  C: <Why that is not yet enough>.
  Q: <implied or stated> → A: <this section's Key Line point / heading>.

Backward reference:
  "<Key phrase from previous section> … <new section's point>," e.g.
  "In addition to <previous point's action>, <subject> should <this section's point>."
  "Just as <previous point>, so <this point>."
  "The <problem from previous section> is compounded by <this section's problem>."
Then: preview the supporting points of this section.
```

### End-of-section summary
```
In summary, <what the recommendation/structure consists of>. <What it provides>.
Only by <the action> can <reader> <realize the larger goal from elsewhere in the report>.
```

### Next Steps
```
NEXT STEPS
If you agree that <recommendation>, then you should:
  1. <Obvious immediate action>
  2. <Obvious immediate action>
  3. <Obvious immediate action>
(Test each: would the reader ask "why?" → if so, it belongs in the body.)
```

## Worked examples

**Exhibit 53 (p. 171) — Headings mirror the pyramid.** A generic pyramid labelled level by level. After:
```
Title/chapter heading ............ Major thought
  Section headings (1.) .......... Key Line boxes (three)
    Subsection headings (1.1) .... children of a Key Line box (3 under one, 4 under another)
      Numbered paragraphs (1.1.1)  children of a subsection (four)
        Dash points .............. children of a numbered paragraph (three)
```
Lesson: each formatting level equals one level of abstraction.

**Exhibit 54 (p. 172) — Very short memo, points underlined.** A memo asking a colleague to review TV sweepstakes rules and copy, voicing three concerns. Structure:
```
Request to review sweepstakes rules/copy; "I have three concerns."
  1. [underlined question on how viewers get access to the rules] → rules printed only in the newspaper; possible lottery problem
  2. [underlined question on whether entrants will bother forecasting] → copy implies a random draw regardless of forecasts
  3. [underlined question on the ad's clarity] → fast-paced commercials may obscure the details
Closing courtesy.
```
Lesson: with under two paragraphs per point, underlining the points is enough to make them jump out.

**Exhibit 55 (p. 172) — Longer memo, Key Line listed then headed.** Field-sales-meeting memo: the meeting will train attendees to design a beverage department likely to pay off for a grocery chain and to pitch it to the chain's managers; each region must supply a problem chain's profile, so the reader must:
```
• choose a chain (deadline July 11)        → first section heading, on choosing the chain
• gather the data (deadline Aug 10)        → second section heading
• organize and send the data (Aug 15)      → third section heading
```
Lesson: when points need more than a paragraph or two, preview them in the introduction and echo each as a section heading. (Same memo appears in Ch. 4, p. 50.)

**Exhibit 56 (p. 173) — The heading conventions page.** A self-describing page showing chapter → section → subsection → numbered paragraph → dash point → dot point, plus stars and ¶ usage (see Templates). Lesson: indent and change form as ideas subordinate; same level, same form.

**Parallel headings example (p. 174).**
Book's shape (a hotel-group reorganization): two section headings, each opening with an imperative verb; under the first, two subsection headings each opening with "To …"; under the second, three subsection headings each opening with an "-ing" word. An invented example of the same shape:
```
Consolidate Purchasing
   To Cut Unit Costs
   To Simplify Supplier Management
Clarify Regional Accountability
   Merging the Smallest Regions
   Naming One Head per Region
   Ending Dual Reporting Lines
```
Lesson: section headings both start with verbs; each subsection group is internally parallel ("To …" vs "-ing"), but the two groups need not match each other.

**Bad heading dependence (p. 175).** A heading recommending a full-time chief executive, followed by body text that opens by calling it "this action" and saying what it will clarify. Lesson: the text must name its own topic; headings are not read as part of the prose.

**Bad TOC (p. 175).** Introduction 1 / Background 2 / Findings 3 / Conclusions 15 / Recommendations 23. Lesson: topic labels convey no thinking; the TOC should be a précis of the ideas.

**Exhibit 57 (p. 176) — Underlined-points layout.** Self-describing page (see Templates). Lesson: a full-statement, skimmable hierarchy; ugly in pure underlining, better with bold at top levels; demands strict Q/A discipline.

**Exhibit 58 (p. 178) — Numbers plus headings.** Opening of Ch. 5 of Antony Jay's *Effective Presentation*:
```
5.  CHAPTER HEADING (caps, centered)          + two intro paragraphs
5.1 SECTION HEADING (caps, at margin)          + one intro paragraph
      5.1.1 Subsection heading (upper/lower, indented) + text
      5.1.2 Subsection heading + text
      5.1.3 Subsection heading + text
5.2 SECTION HEADING + text
(the book's sample: a chapter on delivery; section 5.1 on unscripted talks, with short
 subsections on visuals, time, and the best approach; section 5.2 warning against reading a paper aloud)
```
Lesson: numbers support worded headings, and introductory text precedes each set of children.

**Indented display memo (pp. 179–180) — Creative Thinking session slides.** Before: four prose paragraphs mixing scheduling details (sessions with one group in the second week of September, another in the third), several separate slide needs, a purchased film, and a vague request for slides on an innovation-environment section. After:
```
[one sentence on the two scheduled sessions], ending in a lead-in that says slides are needed for both, showing:
  1. [bold lead phrase: introduction's main points] + note that concepts are attached
  2. [bold lead phrase: sample positive-reinforcement wording] + used as wrap-up and as handout
  3. [bold lead phrase: past innovation results] + useful for one meeting, essential for the other
  4. [bold lead phrase: steps toward an innovation-friendly environment]
```
Lesson: the first version was acceptable but the second makes the points jump out; forcing the items into one grammatical form exposed that the writer had never specified which slides point 4 needed.

**"Format for Progress Reviews" (p. 181) — Dot-dash outline that describes itself.**
```
1. In progress reviews you sometimes lay ideas out differently
   a. When you'll be present as the client reads
      – and want to provoke discussion
         • to get the client's immediate response to findings
         • so you can continue work in the intended direction
   b. So you arrange the page to aid the client's reading
      – grasp main points quickly / see relationships / distinguish less essential points
2. To get the visual effect, obey rules
   a. Short, direct statements at each level – no grace notes – no liaisons
   b. One statement per level
   c. Parallel construction at a level, where possible
   d. Each level relates directly to the one above – explaining it – or supporting it
3. Don't use the format unless you'll obey the rules
```
Lesson: the format lets a small group digest one point at a time; its rules are looser on sibling counts but strict on brevity and relevance.

**Exhibit 59 (pp. 182–183) — Introducing Key Line points with stories (TQM paper from Ch. 4).**
```
S: TQM was the 1980s' hot tool for cutting the cost of quality → competitive advantage, profit
C: Most major firms adopted some TQM but often without the expected gains; yet leaders still hold/gain share and profit
Q: Why? What are the leaders doing better?
Main point: Leaders go beyond TQM to continuous improvement
  1. Repeatedly benchmark themselves against competition
  2. Apply Activity-Based Management to get at real costs
  3. Adjust TQM to reinforce the resulting strategies
```
Section openers:
- BENCHMARKING — S: you're a bank whose TQM effort cut loan processing from 2 days to 2 hours; C: you'd assume that's enough for advantage, but you can't know without comparing to competitors → formal benchmarking is essential.
- ACTIVITY-BASED MANAGEMENT — S: benchmarking shows you're the industry best; C: that matters only if what you excel at returns more than its real cost, which you can learn only by analyzing cost by activity rather than function → ABM.
- TOTAL QUALITY MANAGEMENT — S: you've benchmarked, adopted ABM, perhaps gained advantage; C: not safe if you're running the same old TQM; Q: can you keep the advantage? Probably not unless TQM is realigned → what must change?

Lesson: each section story repeats the SCQ form at reduced scope, starting from where the reader now stands, and contains only accepted facts.

**Exhibit 60 (pp. 184–185) — Linking sections by referencing backward (Ritz-Ryan hotel group).**
```
[Deductive top] Owning the whole group is a strong lever for each hotel's/restaurant's success
  → But a loose organization keeps the group from exploiting that common ownership
  → So slim down the top-executive structure to give firm control over group-wide operations
      Install a full-time chief executive
         (two purposes: coordinating line and staff; driving constructive change)
      Make authority and responsibility lines clear
         (three moves: regroup hotels by support needs, split London / outside London;
          put overseas operations under one person; take subsidiary boards out of the command chain)
```
Links, described by shape: (1) the second section opens by naming the current top structure as having two shortcomings that hold back use of the group's combined resources, echoing the "advantage" idea of the section before; (2) the second subsection opens with an "In addition to [first subsection's action], …" clause that then states its own point; (3) the second support point opens with a "Just as [first support point's benefit], so [this point's benefit]" comparison, both hinging on the full-time chief executive. Lesson: carry the key word forward; no story needed because the original introduction already flagged these points.

**Ritz-Ryan chapter summary (p. 185).** Shape: (1) what the recommended top structure consists of (board and chairman, a group managing director, three executives each running a major business); (2) what it provides (a strong framework for long-term leadership and Groupwide control); (3) why it matters (only such streamlining lets the group realize the improvements identified elsewhere). Lesson: a section summary restates content and tone already in the pyramid.

**Lame vs significant conclusions (pp. 186–187).** Lame: "this report has outlined our recommendations and the steps each department must take". Significant: a report showing a Europe-wide computerized technical-literature retrieval system is technically feasible closes by saying success would not only improve access for business, professional, and academic users, but would create a common market for information spanning all sources, possibly advancing standardization and new standards, and that the authors are eager to help launch the pilot. Lesson: put the significance in perspective and point toward action. (Lincoln's second inaugural close is cited as combining insight with a call to immediate action.)

**Next Steps (p. 187) — acquisition recommendation.** After 30 pages arguing to buy a company:
```
If you agree the acquisition makes sense, your next moves are:
  1. Invite the owner to lunch
  2. Confirm with the bank that purchase funds will be available
  3. Reconvene the Acquisitions Committee for the administrative details
```
Lesson: no reader will ask "why lunch rather than dinner?"; steps are self-evident. Any step that would raise a question belongs in the body.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **Author's preference is explicit but not absolute.** She leans to hierarchical headings, yet acknowledges strong arguments for underlined points and decimal numbering; feelings run high on the choice (p. 170).
- **Numbered paragraphs are inside the text.** Unlike headings, they are read as prose, so the "headings outside the text" rule doesn't apply to them (p. 175).
- **Parallelism is local.** Required among siblings, not between groups under different parents (p. 174).
- **Dot-dash outlines relax the "at least two at every level" rule** (p. 180), unlike hierarchical headings, where one of anything is forbidden (p. 174).
- **Underlined points are hard on the writer.** They only work if the writer can enforce strict Q/A logic, terse wording, and argument-size limits; most people just list points (p. 177).
- **Decimal numbering's referencing advantage is real but secondary**; it costs concentration and maintenance (pp. 177–178).
- **Indented display:** the first (prose) version of the example memo is called acceptably clear; the indented one is better, not mandatory (p. 180).
- **Story vs backward reference:** a story is used when leading the reader into a Key Line point; backward referencing suffices when the original introduction's preview already equipped the reader (p. 185).
- **Conclusions:** theoretically unnecessary; the author discourages most writers from attempting them; emotional pitch must suit the reader and subject (pp. 185–187).
- **Next Steps is the one required ending**, and only for unquestionable actions (p. 187).
- **The reader is not your peer.** Even about their own company, they grasp your analysis less well than you do; design transitions for that (pp. 187–188).
- **Marker order varies between the book's examples.** Exhibit 56 runs numbered paragraph → dash → dot; Exhibit 57 runs … → dot → dash; the progress-review box runs 1. → a. → dash → dot.
  > **Skill note:** treat marker choice as a house-style decision; what the book insists on is consistency within a document and one visual form per level.
- **The numbering illustration (p. 179) shows only one item per level**, purely to display the numbering style; it is not an exception to PAG-7.
  > **Skill note:** don't cite it as permission for single children.
- **Time savings are small per document but add up** for executives who read scores of documents a day (p. 181).
