# Imposing Logical Order (Part Two intro + Chapter 6)

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Introduction to Part Two "Logic in Thinking" (book pp. 73–74) and Ch. 6 "Imposing Logical Order" (book pp. 75–93). Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `ORD`.

## When a skill needs this

- Reviewing a draft whose sections or bullet lists are "N problems / N steps / N reasons" lists that feel loose, long, or arbitrary in sequence.
- Checking whether a grouping of points is complete and belongs together, before writing or approving the summary point above it.
- Untangling a long, flat list (8–12 items) into a few ordered subgroups that carry a real message.
- Ordering recommendations, process steps, organizational units, or findings so the order itself is defensible.
- Coaching someone through "what is this list actually saying?" when a list has no top point or a vague one.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Hard-Headed Thinking | Minto's name for the two-part discipline of Part Two: impose logical order on each grouping (Ch 6), then draw the inductive leap from it (Ch 7). |
| Logical order rule | Every grouping of ideas must have a reason why each point sits where it does; in Ch 6 called "the second rule" of the pyramid. |
| Source of the grouping | The analytical activity the mind performed to bring the ideas together; it dictates the order (Exhibit 23). |
| Time order | Order of steps taken, one after another, to produce an effect; comes from identifying the causes of an effect (a process or system). Also called chronological order. |
| Structural order | Order that mirrors a visualized whole divided into its parts (a diagram, map, org chart, ROI tree); real or conceptual. |
| Degree order | Ranking of items classified together by a shared characteristic, by how strongly each has it; also "comparative order" or "order of importance." |
| MECE | Mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and collectively exhaustive (nothing left out); the test for dividing a whole into parts. |
| Mini-process | A cluster of lower-level steps that produce one end product inside a longer process; must be pulled out as its own level. |
| Class grouping | A grouping justified only because each item shares a characteristic (e.g., "problems," "reasons"); formed by bifurcating a universe into "these" vs "all others." |
| Improper class grouping | A list presented as a class whose items actually come from a process or structure, or contain hidden subclasses. |
| Principle of division | The basis used to split a whole (by activity, by location, by product/market); it determines how the parts are ordered. |

## Concepts

### Introduction to Part Two: why groupings must be checked (pp. 73–74)

- With practice, the top of the pyramid comes fairly easily: Subject, reader's Question, Situation and Complication, the main point, the Key Line, and (via question/answer) the level just below the Key Line.
- Minto's working advice: build the pyramid only to one level below the Key Line, then simply write. Don't try to work out all lower-level ideas in advance. But once the draft exists, you must go back and scrutinize the structure of what you put into prose.
- At that review stage two errors almost always show up:
  1. **Loose lists.** Points grouped as "ten steps" or "five problems" that are justified only because each fits the plural noun, not because they share an internal logic.
  2. **Blank tops.** The heading over the list merely counts and labels its contents ("our client faces five problems"), offering no insight. Minto's term for this is an intellectually blank assertion (developed in `06-summarizing-grouped-ideas.md`).
- Minto doesn't object to listing as such. Everyone does it, and a list is a useful first draft of your thinking because it puts the ideas where you can examine them. The mistake is treating the list as finished. The follow-through has two parts: verify that every grouping hangs together by some inherent logic, then write down the insight that logic yields.
- Why it matters: this scrutiny is the core of thinking, and is hard, which is why it's skipped. The costs of skipping it: the reader never gets your real meaning, you never get to the heart of your own reasoning, time and resources are wasted, and the big insights latent in the material can go unfound.
- Opening illustration (p. 74): an eight-item list of buyer complaints about sales and inventory reports versus a three-point rewrite. The rewrite makes the needed corrective actions obvious far faster (see Worked examples).
- The two techniques that get you from the first version to the second: (1) identify the logical framework binding the ideas, which also fixes their sequence (Ch 6); (2) draw out the insight the set implies, i.e., make the inductive leap (Ch 7). Together = Hard-Headed Thinking. Minto concedes the discipline is difficult both to acquire and to use, and urges readers to invest the time anyway, because without it you can't really know what you think.

### Chapter 6 opening: the logical order rule and the three sources of order (pp. 75–77)

- The rule: every grouping's ideas need a logical sequence. Its value is diagnostic. Order checks that the ideas truly belong together and that none is missing. A set may fairly be called "steps," yet if you can't number them in sequence you have no assurance they form a single process, or that the process is complete.
- **Deductive groupings** pose no ordering problem: the argument's structure fixes the order. **Inductive groupings** involve a choice, so you need to know how to choose and how to verify the choice.
- Reconciling with Ch 1 (see `01-pyramid-fundamentals.md`): Ch 1 names four possible orders (deductive, chronological, structural, comparative). Ch 6 sets the deductive one aside as fixed by the argument and devotes itself to the three that apply to inductive groupings: time (= chronological), structural, and degree (= comparative).
- Premise: ideas never land in a group by chance. The mind picks them because it sees a logical relationship (the steps of a solution, an industry's success factors, a firm's problems). To see such a relationship, the mind must have done a logical analysis. **The order should reflect that analysis.**
- The mind can perform only three such analytical activities (Exhibit 23):

| Analytical activity (source) | Picture | Order it dictates |
|---|---|---|
| Determine the causes of an effect | Effect box over Cause 1/2/3 | Time order |
| Divide a whole into its parts | Company over Division A/B/C | Structural order |
| Classify like things together | Universe of problems split into "these three" vs "all other problems" | Degree order |

- **Causes of an effect → time order (p. 76).** Whenever you instruct the reader to act (replace the head of sales; devolve profit accountability to regional units), you do so because you expect a certain result. You fixed the effect first, then found the action. When several actions are jointly needed, they form a process or system: the set of causes that together create the effect. Steps happen sequentially, never all at once, so a process grouping is necessarily in time order, **and the result of performing the actions is always what sits above them.**
- **Whole into parts → structural order (p. 77).** Familiar from org charts and industry structure diagrams. To name "key factors for success in an industry," you first picture the industry's structure, then decide what must be done well in each part. The resulting three or four factors follow the order of the parts in the structure you pictured.
- **Classify like things → degree order (p. 77).** Saying a company "has three problems" is not literally true. It has a whole universe of problems, and you singled out three as noteworthy because each has a certain characteristic (e.g., each stems from a refusal to delegate). The three are alike in having that characteristic and differ in how much of it they have. (If they had it equally you couldn't tell them apart on that basis.) So you rank them by degree, greatest first. Names: degree order, comparative order, order of importance.
- Orders can be used alone or combined, but **one of the three must always be present** to justify a grouping. Because these three analyses are the only ways a grouping can arise, every legitimate grouping is built on one of their orders. Make a habit of hunting for it. Failing to find an order is an immediate warning sign, and knowing which framework should have produced the group points you toward the repair.

### Time Order (pp. 77–81)

- Seemingly the simplest and the most widely used. A time-ordered group mirrors the doer's sequence: first this, then this, then this, until the intended effect is reached.
- The ideas can be (a) actual steps or other action ideas (recommendations, objectives), or (b) **conclusions drawn with an underlying process in mind**. Each case has its own failure:
  - (a) Writers fail to **distinguish cause from effect** when they list steps.
  - (b) Writers fail to **recognize that a process was the basis** of their conclusions.

#### Distinguishing Cause from Effect (pp. 78–80)

- Every action exists for the sake of some result, yet a lengthy process stacks many cause-effect layers. Flat lists hide those layers.
- Productivity study example (8 Phase I steps; see Worked examples). Two problems:
  1. **Too many points.** Recall the Magic Number Seven. Minto's own recommendation: **cap each grouping at four or five points**. With more than five, it is very likely some items are more closely related to each other than to the rest, and not showing that relationship hides part of your thinking. Illustration: noting that some of the Ten Commandments are sins against God and some against man conveys an insight a plain list of ten misses.
  2. **Mixed levels of abstraction.** The sequence may be correct, yet several steps serve only to create the output that another step names. Those clusters are self-contained mini-processes inside the larger one. Leaving them flattened hides what the writer is really proposing to do.
- After restructuring, the writer can check appropriateness and completeness: are these three steps all that's needed to find productivity opportunities? Are interviews plus tracing work flow enough to determine the critical functions?
- **The trick:** picture yourself actually performing each action, and name what you will have in hand when it's done. Then judge whether this action must be done *before* the next one (same level), or done *in order to achieve* the next one (lower level, a cause of it).
- This visualization is a fast way to judge whether a grouping says what you meant. Strategic-planning-cycle example (pp. 79–80): narrate the list as the person doing it (perceive need → develop strategy → implement → …?) and the narration stalls at item 4. It mixes three actions the company takes with four results (stages of the product life cycle curve: market acceptance, high growth, slower growth/maturity, decline). Fix: item 4 should be an action along the lines of evaluating how the market responds; the life-cycle stages are simply the shape that response can take. One leftover point, high cash generation, is a trait of the onset of maturity, not a step, so it doesn't belong in the list at all.

#### Revealing the Underlying Process (pp. 80–81)

- Seeing that a set of conclusions was generated by thinking about some process is a strong aid to finding the message. Lists of conclusions often gesture at a point without ever making it.
- A list can seem to have a message because each item is understandable and sensible on its own, even with no top point. **Making yourself defend the sequence** exposes what the message must be (business-definition example: once you see the order as "segment, then respond to change, then assess position," the message is about *how* you define a business).
- Once the process is visible you can judge whether steps are missing. More important, tracing thinking back to its source tells you **what questions to ask when checking someone else's thinking**.
- Investment-evaluation example (p. 81): a three-point critique where the third point (it yields misleading prescriptions) is really the effect of the other two, so it moves to the top. To see that, you must picture the source process: establish a concept → build a technique on it → apply the technique. The critique comments on steps 1 and 2 but not 3. Either nothing is wrong with how the technique is applied, or (more likely) the author forgot. The reviewer, having traced the process, knows to ask about step 3.
- Bridge to structural order: sometimes time order is laid over an existing structure, so the structure fixes how many steps there are and in what sequence.

### Structural Order (pp. 82–89)

- Structural order follows the layout of a picture of the thing: a diagram, map, drawing, or photo. The pictured thing may be physical or abstract, a static object or a process. The precondition is that the whole has been correctly split into its parts.

#### Creating a Structure (pp. 82–84)

- When dividing any whole, physical or conceptual, the parts must not overlap one another (**mutually exclusive**) and together must cover the whole (**collectively exhaustive**): MECE. People apply this automatically when drawing org charts (Exhibit 24: Akron Tire and Rubber Company → Tire, Housewares, Sports Equipment divisions, each with Research/Production/Marketing beneath).
  - Mutually exclusive: what happens in one division isn't duplicated in another (no overlaps).
  - Collectively exhaustive: the three divisions together cover everything the company does (nothing left out).
- A MECE division guarantees the diagram contains everything an explanation must cover. Simplest form of structural order: describe the pieces in the order they appear on the diagram.
- **Which order on the diagram?** It reflects the principle of division used. An organization's activities can be divided three ways:
  - **By activity** (research, marketing, production): the units reflect a process, so use **time order**.
  - **By location** (Eastern, Midwest, West regions): use **structural order** that follows geography.
  - **By product/market/customer** (Tires, Housewares, Sports Equipment): you have classified, so use **degree order** by whatever measure is relevant (sales volume, investment size).
- City-government reorganization (p. 83): six departments (housing, transportation, education, recreation, personal health, environmental health) ordered by the sequence in which a city built from scratch would need to look after its people. **Forcing an order like this, especially when creating something new such as an organization, lets you check you've been collectively exhaustive for your purpose.**
- For things other than organizations, the purpose of dividing is usually to analyze how the thing works. Divide by **functioning part** and present the parts **in the order they perform the function** (radar set: modulator → RF oscillator → antenna with scanner → receiver → indicator, following how power and signal flow).

#### Describing a Structure (pp. 84–85)

- One way: walk it top-down and left-to-right, describing parts in the order they appear (standard for technical descriptions of machinery).
- Another: impose a **process order** on the description. Sinai example (a New Yorker passage): the peninsula's several political "contexts" are listed in the order the eye would take in a map, starting top left and moving clockwise (link to Egypt, then extension of Israel, then adjunct of Saudi Arabia, then back across as an east–west land bridge). The writer pictured how a reader would scan the map and followed that path.

#### Recommending Changes to a Structure (pp. 85–86)

- Picturing a process against a structure is especially useful when recommending changes to an existing structure.
- City government example: from 25 departments under 23 committees to about 6 departments under 6 committees plus an administrative arm under a Chief Executive. Four changes are needed. They are **equally important** (so degree order is out) and in principle **simultaneous** (so time order is out).
- Solution: order them the way you'd **draw the new structure on a blank sheet**, element by element, for the reader: (1) consolidate committees into six under a Policy and Finance Committee; (2) regroup departments to match; (3) create the two support units for P&F; (4) create the administrative team under a Chief Executive.

#### Using the Concept to Clarify Thinking (pp. 86–89)

- As with time order, structural order can expose faulty logic.
- Transportation-department example (pp. 86–87): questioning the sequence and the origin of the items first shows one item (identify strengths and weaknesses in each study area) refers to *all* the others, so it is set aside. Listing the subjects of the remaining items and laying them against the road-building process (design, construct, operate, maintain) reveals the real objective: determine whether the department is organized and managed properly to carry out its four activities.
- Plastic-bottle example (pp. 87–89), almost a free association of eleven "risks and constraints." It shows the writer had a structure in mind but, not being consciously aware of it, couldn't use it. Sorting method: ask of each item *why is this bad?* That reveals patterns (high cost, lower price, low sales, low ROI, lower EPS, must borrow, legal ban). Business people discussing costs, sales, prices, investment, and ROI are implicitly using the standard ROI tree. Mapping the points onto that tree, and onto a share-price tree, reveals the message.
- **Core lesson:** muddled reasoning is invisible while the points sit in a list; only after a structure has been laid over them do the errors and gaps show. Once the message is visible you can test whether each point is properly supported. In this case the analysis had omitted the positive effect of plastic packaging on sales; the company in fact entered the business and succeeded hugely.

### Degree Order (pp. 89–93)

- Degree order (usually called order of importance) applies when a grouping gathers things classified as alike because they share a characteristic: three problems, four reasons, five variables. **This is where the urge to list instead of think is strongest.**

#### Creating Proper Class Groupings (pp. 89–91)

- Saying "the company has three problems" automatically splits off these three from every other problem the company has or could have: a two-way split (Exhibit 25: a universe of, say, 3,000 organization problems → "3 that…" vs "2,997 others"; the three share a characteristic you can rank). The two classes are by definition collectively exhaustive and are meant to be mutually exclusive.
- Prove the grouping by (a) defining **very specifically** the characteristic the items share, then (b) searching what you know to make sure **every known item with that characteristic is included**. Then (c) order them by how strongly each has that characteristic, **strongest first**.
- **Strongest-first vs climax order:** people ask whether building up from weakest to strongest would be more dramatic. It would, but drama serves feeling rather than logic, which makes it a stylistic choice. You may legitimately reverse the order for emotional impact in some cases, but normally the strongest point goes first.
- Order carries meaning. Telecom billing example (p. 90): listing customer needs, then internal management needs, then regulatory conformance tells the reader the customer matters most and the regulator least. Underneath sat an automatic classification: all design criteria → functional criteria vs other criteria.
- Class-based order is used much less in business writing than time or structural order. Classifying is constant (naming anything classifies it), but people don't restrict classes to items sharing a characteristic. They also treat ideas as alike, and so "classifiable," when the ideas **come from the same process or the same structure**. That is legitimate **if you are clear about the true source of the grouping and use the order that source imposes.** Vendor Capture example (pp. 90–91): three "reasons" against the strategy are in fact ordered by an existing structure (the warehouse, the handling process inside it, the savings calculated from that), each one conceding the previous ("even if…").

#### Identifying Improper Class Groupings (pp. 91–93)

- Tracing a so-called class back to where it actually came from often unlocks what the writer really means.
- Investment-evaluation "misleading prescriptions" (p. 91): four items labeled as rules of thumb. Reword each as a *result* of the financial focus and three of the four turn out to be stages of a decision-making process. That dictates time order and a clearer top point; the fourth item drops out (see Worked examples).
- That case was easy because the kind of idea was obvious from reading. More often a long list labeled "reasons" or "problems" **hides subclasses of different kinds** of reasons or problems.
- Method (buyer-complaints example, p. 92): sort items into rough categories by naming the **type** of problem each describes (e.g., "report frequency is inappropriate" → type: bad timing). Result: timing, data, format.
- **What order do the subgroups go in? It depends on the process, and the process depends on the question being answered:**
  - Question is why the report is useless → follow how it is *produced* → data, format, timing.
  - Question is why buyers dislike it → follow how it is *read* → timing, format, data.
  - Question is how to repair it → follow the *fix* sequence → format, data, timing.
- Minto says she knows no other way to reach the real thinking beneath a class-style list: (1) label what kind of point each item makes; (2) cluster items with the same label; (3) find the order the set of groups implies.
- New York decline example (p. 93): eight causes → three types (high costs; unsuitability of the area; an alternative choice) → probably order of importance.

#### Chapter wrap-up (p. 93)

- Testing for order is the main test of a grouping's validity. For any inductive grouping you're reviewing:
  1. Run your eye down the list and look for one of the three orders.
  2. If not, can you identify the grouping's source (process, structure, class) and impose the matching order?
  3. If the list is long, can you find similarities that let you subgroup it, and then order the subgroups?
- Only once a grouping is known to be valid and complete can you draw a logical inference from it (Ch 7).

## Rules

- **ORD-1**: Draft to one level below the Key Line, write, then go back and scrutinize every grouping in the prose. *Why:* lower levels are worked out more efficiently in writing, but drafts reliably contain loose lists and blank tops. *Check:* has every grouping in the draft been examined for intrinsic logic, not just the Key Line? (p. 73)
- **ORD-2**: Never let a list stand just because its items fit a plural noun ("steps," "problems"). Confirm the items share an internal logic. *Why:* fitting the label doesn't make items belong together or make the set complete. *Check:* can you say why these items, and only these, are in the group? (p. 73)
- **ORD-3**: Every inductive grouping must show one of three orders: time, structural, or degree. *Why:* a grouping can only come from one of three analyses (causes of an effect, division of a whole, classification), so one of their orders must be its backbone. *Check:* name the order; if you can't, treat the grouping as defective. (pp. 75–77)
- **ORD-4**: Choose the order that matches how the grouping was produced: causes of an effect → time; parts of a whole → structure; class of like things → degree. *Why:* the order should mirror the analysis your mind did. *Check:* for each grouping, state its source and confirm the order matches. (pp. 75–77)
- **ORD-5**: Don't agonize over deductive groupings' order; the argument fixes it. *Why:* only inductive groupings involve a choice. *Check:* is the grouping a "therefore" chain? If so, argument order applies. (p. 75)
- **ORD-6**: Use order to test completeness. If a set of "steps" can't be put in one-two-three sequence, assume something is missing or doesn't belong. *Why:* sequence proves that steps form one process and that the process is whole. *Check:* walk the sequence and look for gaps and intruders. (p. 75)
- **ORD-7**: For a set of actions that jointly produce a result, use time order and make the summary the effect of doing them. *Why:* actions are causes; they happen one at a time; their only common ground is the effect. *Check:* does the point above name the effect, not "N steps"? (p. 76)
- **ORD-8**: Cap each grouping at four or five points. With more, look for items more closely related to each other and subgroup them. *Why:* working memory is limited, and with more than five items some are almost certainly more closely related, so leaving that unshown hides thinking. *Check:* count items per group; for any >5, propose subgroups. (p. 78)
- **ORD-9**: Separate cause from effect in step lists. Pull out mini-processes so each level holds steps of the same level of abstraction. *Why:* mixing sub-steps with the results they produce hides what will actually be done and blocks the completeness check. *Check:* for each pair of adjacent steps, ask "is A done *before* B, or *in order to* produce B?" If the latter, A goes beneath B. (pp. 78–79)
- **ORD-10**: Picture yourself doing each action and name the tangible thing you'll hold when it's finished. *Why:* it's the fastest way to see level errors, hidden results, and missing steps. *Check:* write the end product next to each step; steps with no end product, or whose end product is another step's input, get regrouped. (p. 79)
- **ORD-11**: Don't mix actions and outcomes in one list of steps. Convert outcomes into an action (e.g., "assess the reaction") or remove them. *Why:* results aren't steps a doer can take; mixing them breaks the process. *Check:* role-play the list as "first I…, then I…"; any item you can't do is an intruder. (pp. 79–80)
- **ORD-12**: Remove items that are attributes of another item (or of a stage) rather than members of the sequence. *Why:* they inflate the list and blur the process. *Check:* for each item, ask "is this a step/part in its own right, or a property of one?" (p. 80)
- **ORD-13**: When conclusions rest on an implicit process, surface the process and order the points by it. *Why:* it reveals the real message and shows which stages lack comment. *Check:* sketch the process (box → box → box) and map each point to a stage; empty stages are questions to raise. (pp. 80–81)
- **ORD-14**: If one point in a group is the result of the others, move it up to be their summary. *Why:* an effect isn't a peer of its causes. *Check:* ask of each point "is this caused by the others?" (p. 81)
- **ORD-15**: When dividing a whole into parts, make the parts MECE. *Why:* only then does the structure show every piece that must be explained, with no double-counting. *Check:* look for overlap between any two parts and for anything in the whole that no part covers. (p. 82)
- **ORD-16**: Order structural parts by the principle of division: by activity → time order; by location → geographic/structural order; by product, market, or customer → degree order on a relevant measure (sales, investment). *Why:* the basis of division is the analysis behind the grouping. *Check:* name the basis of division and confirm the order fits it. (p. 83)
- **ORD-17**: When creating a new structure, force an order on its parts (e.g., the order needs arise) to check it's exhaustive for your purpose. *Why:* imposing order surfaces missing parts. *Check:* walk the order and ask what need or stage has no unit. (p. 83)
- **ORD-18**: For a thing (not an organization) analyzed by how it works, divide by functioning part and present parts in the order they function. *Why:* the reader is trying to understand function. *Check:* does each part's output feed the next? (pp. 83–84)
- **ORD-19**: Describe a structure either top-down and left-to-right, or along a process the reader would naturally follow (such as the path the eye takes over a map). *Why:* both give a principled sequence. *Check:* can you name the path the description follows? (pp. 84–85)
- **ORD-20**: When recommending changes to a structure that are equally important and simultaneous, order them as you'd draw the new structure on a blank page, one element at a time. *Why:* neither degree nor time order applies, but a build-up drawing order does. *Check:* would a reader sketching along end up with the target structure? (p. 86)
- **ORD-21**: Set aside any item that refers to all the other items (e.g., "identify strengths and weaknesses in each area"). It's not a peer. *Why:* it sits at a different level. *Check:* does the item's scope span the others? (p. 86)
- **ORD-22**: Faced with a messy list, ask why each item is (good/bad/included), then lay the answers on a known structure (a process, ROI tree, EPS tree) to find the message. *Why:* writers often have an unconscious structure; imposing it exposes the point, flaws, and omissions. *Check:* can every item be placed on one structure? Which branches are empty? (pp. 87–89)
- **ORD-23**: Only after the message is visible, scrutinize each supporting point and look for what the structure says is missing (e.g., an upside never assessed). *Why:* flawed reasoning stays hidden until a structure is laid over it. *Check:* list the structure's branches with no analysis. (p. 89)
- **ORD-24**: For a class grouping, define the shared characteristic precisely and confirm every known item with it is included. *Why:* that is how you prove the class is properly separated from "all others." *Check:* state the characteristic in one specific phrase; brainstorm other items that fit; add or justify their exclusion. (pp. 89–90)
- **ORD-25**: Rank degree-ordered items strongest first. Reverse to weakest-first only as a deliberate stylistic choice for emotional effect. *Why:* order of importance is the logical order; climax order is a matter of style. *Check:* is the first item the one that most strongly has the defining characteristic? (p. 90)
- **ORD-26**: Treat the sequence of a degree-ordered list as a claim about relative importance, and make sure it's the claim you intend. *Why:* readers infer priority from order (customer before regulator). *Check:* would you defend the implied ranking? (p. 90)
- **ORD-27**: If items labeled as a class were really drawn from one process or one structure, say so and use that source's order. *Why:* grouping by common origin is legitimate only if the order reflects the true source. *Check:* do the items chain ("even if…"), or map to stages or parts? If so, order by stage or part. (pp. 90–91)
- **ORD-28**: To expose an improper class grouping, reword the items in a common form (e.g., as results of the subject) and see whether a process or structure emerges. *Why:* uniform rewording reveals the true source and the right top point. *Check:* rewrite each item as "X [verb]s…" and look for a sequence; drop items that don't fit it. (p. 91)
- **ORD-29**: For a long class list, (1) name the type of each point, (2) group same-type points, (3) find the order the set of groups implies. *Why:* long lists hide subclasses; this is the only known way to get at the real thinking. *Check:* every item carries a type label; there are ≤5 types; the types are ordered. (p. 92)
- **ORD-30**: Choose the order of subgroups by the process relevant to the question the document answers (producing vs reading vs fixing). *Why:* the same subgroups legitimately take different orders for different questions. *Check:* state the reader's question and confirm the order follows the process that question implies. (p. 92)
- **ORD-31**: Don't attempt the summary until the grouping is shown valid and complete via its order. *Why:* an inference drawn from a defective grouping is unreliable (feeds Ch 7). *Check:* has ORD-3 been passed for this group? (p. 93)

## Procedures

### P1. Review any inductive grouping (Minto's end-of-chapter routine, p. 93)

1. Read down the list quickly. Is there an evident order: time (steps, sequence), structural (parts of a whole as drawn or mapped), or degree (ranked members of a class)?
   - **Yes** → confirm the order matches the grouping's source (P2); check completeness against it; go to Ch 7 to summarize.
   - **No** → step 2.
2. Identify the source: did the writer (a) work out actions or causes for an effect, (b) divide a whole into parts, or (c) classify items sharing a characteristic? Impose the matching order.
3. If the list is long (>5), look for similarities that let you form subgroups (P5), then order the subgroups.
4. If no source and no order can be found, the grouping is faulty: rethink what the ideas are and why they're together. Don't summarize yet.

### P2. Decide which order a grouping needs

1. Is it a "therefore" chain? → **Deductive**: argument order. Stop.
2. Are the items actions (or conclusions about stages) that together produce an effect? → **Time order**; the summary will be the effect.
3. Are the items parts of a visualizable whole (org, geography, machine, tree, map)? → **Structural order**:
   - By activity → time sequence of the activities.
   - By location → geographic order.
   - By product/market/customer → degree order on a relevant measure.
   - Physical or functional thing → order of functioning.
   - Describing a picture or map → top-down/left-right, or the path the eye takes.
   - Recommending structural changes that are equal and simultaneous → order of drawing the new structure.
4. Are the items members of a class that share a characteristic? → **Degree order**, strongest first.
5. Labeled a class but items chain or map to stages or parts? → It's really process- or structure-derived (ORD-27). Use that order.

### P3. Untangle a step list (cause vs effect)

1. Number the original steps.
2. For each step, picture yourself doing it; write the end product you'd hold.
3. For each pair, ask: is step A done *before* B (same level) or *so that* B can be produced (A is beneath B)?
4. Cluster steps under the step whose end product they create (mini-processes). Keep each level to ≤5.
5. Remove or convert any item that is a result, not an action (e.g., outcome stages → "assess the reaction"); drop items that are attributes of a stage.
6. Rewrite the top as the overall effect (e.g., "In Phase I we will identify X").
7. Completeness check: are the substeps sufficient to produce each parent's end product? Are the parents sufficient for the top effect? Add missing steps.

### P4. Reveal the underlying process behind a list of conclusions

1. Attempt to defend the existing sequence of the points. Ask what sequence of activity they reflect.
2. Draw the process as boxes (e.g., concept → technique → application).
3. Map each point to a stage. A point that is the outcome of the others moves to the top.
4. Note stages with no point; these are either deliberate (nothing wrong there) or omissions. Raise them as questions ("is anything wrong with how they apply it?").
5. Restate the top point to name what the process is about.

### P5. Sort a long class list into ordered subgroups (p. 92)

1. For each item, name the *type* of point it makes (a short category label: "bad timing," "poor data," "high costs").
2. Group items of the same type. Aim for ≤5 types.
3. Determine the reader's question (why does it happen? why do people object? how do we fix it?).
4. Identify the process that question implies, and order the types by that process; if they're truly a class, order by degree (strongest first).
5. Rename each type as a substantive point; then summarize (Ch 7).

### P6. Find the message in a "free association" list by imposing a structure (pp. 87–89)

1. For each item ask *why is this considered good/bad/relevant?* Write the one- or two-word reason.
2. Look at the vocabulary of the reasons (cost, price, sales, investment, ROI, EPS…) and identify the standard structure it implies (ROI tree, EPS tree, a process, an org chart).
3. Place each item on the structure. Each tree or branch that collects items suggests a message.
4. Handle leftovers: fit them by adding a layer (e.g., interest/taxes beneath profits), or treat them as separate conditions.
5. Assemble the message pyramid.
6. Now scrutinize the support, and look for branches the author never analyzed (e.g., the upside).

### P7. Prove and order a class grouping (pp. 89–90)

1. State precisely the characteristic the items share.
2. Search your knowledge for every other item with that characteristic; add any that belong.
3. Confirm no item lacks the characteristic (misfit) and no two overlap.
4. Rank by the degree each has the characteristic; strongest first (unless a deliberate stylistic reversal).
5. Check that the implied priority is one you'd defend.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| "Ten steps" / "five problems" list justified only by the label | Loose list; no internal logic | ORD-2, ORD-3, P1 |
| List has no visible order; items could be shuffled with no loss | Source of grouping unidentified | ORD-3, ORD-4, P2 |
| More than five items in a group | Hidden subgroups | ORD-8, ORD-29, P5 |
| Step list where some steps produce the outputs of others | Cause and effect at one level | ORD-9, ORD-10, P3 |
| Step list that turns into outcomes partway through ("implementation, market acceptance, high growth…") | Actions mixed with results | ORD-11, ORD-12 |
| One point in a list is clearly the consequence of the others | Effect sitting among its causes | ORD-14 |
| Item that covers all the other items ("identify strengths and weaknesses in each area") | Different level of abstraction | ORD-21 |
| List of conclusions with no top point that "seems to make sense" | Unrecognized underlying process | ORD-13, P4 |
| Critique comments on some stages of a process but not others | Possible omission | ORD-13 |
| Org units or parts overlap, or a known activity has no home | Not MECE | ORD-15, ORD-17 |
| Parts listed in an order that matches neither activity flow, geography, nor size | Order ignores principle of division | ORD-16, ORD-18 |
| Equal, simultaneous recommendations in arbitrary order | No ordering principle chosen | ORD-20 |
| Eleven "risks" mixing costs, prices, laws, financing | Unconscious structure not imposed | ORD-22, ORD-23, P6 |
| Class list's first item isn't the strongest; implied priority is unintended | Degree order wrong | ORD-25, ORD-26 |
| "Reasons" that each concede the previous ("even if…") | Structure-derived grouping posing as class | ORD-27 |
| "Prescriptions" or "rules" that, reworded, are stages of a process | Improper class grouping | ORD-28 |
| Same subgroups ordered differently in different sections, or ordered at random | Order not tied to reader's question | ORD-30 |
| Summary written before grouping was validated | Inference on a defective base | ORD-31 |

## Templates and patterns

**Source → order lookup**

```
Grouping came from…                     → Order            → Summary will be…
Working out causes of an effect          → Time             → the effect of the actions
Dividing a whole into parts              → Structural       → (what the structure implies)
Classifying things sharing a trait       → Degree (strong→weak) → (what the similarity implies)
A deductive chain                        → Argument order   → conclusion-led summary
```

**Structural-division ordering**

```
Divide by ACTIVITY  (research, production, marketing)  → time order of the process
Divide by LOCATION  (East, Midwest, West)              → geographic order
Divide by PRODUCT/MARKET/CUSTOMER                      → degree order on sales, investment, …
Divide a DEVICE/SYSTEM by functioning part             → order of functioning
Describe a MAP/PICTURE                                 → top-down/left-right, or the eye's path
RECOMMEND changes to a structure (equal, simultaneous) → order you would draw the new structure
```

**Level test for steps**

```
A before B         → A and B are siblings (same level, time order)
A so that B        → A is a child of B (B is A's effect)
```

**Cause-effect restructure skeleton**

```
<Effect of the whole phase>
1. <End product 1>
   - <step that produces it>
   - <step that produces it>
2. <End product 2>
   - …
3. <End product 3>
```

**Class-list sorting worksheet**

```
| # | Item (as written)        | Type of point |
|---|--------------------------|---------------|
| 1 | …                        | <type A>      |
| 2 | …                        | <type B>      |
Types → order by the process behind the reader's question (or by degree)
```

**Process-mapping probe**

```
[Stage 1] → [Stage 2] → [Stage 3]
 point a     point b     (nothing)  ← ask: deliberate, or forgotten?
```

## Worked examples

**Intro, p. 74 — Buyers' complaints about reports.** Before: a heading saying buyers dislike the system's reports, with 8 flat items (frequency wrong; data unreliable; data late; data can't be matched to sales; want better formats; want meaningless data removed; want exception highlighting; want fewer manual calculations). After:
```
The system's monthly report is useless
  1. Its data can't be trusted
  2. Its layout is hard to use
  3. It comes out too late to act on
```
Lesson: a few ordered, typed points make the needed actions obvious; eight raw complaints don't. (Sorting method shown on p. 92.)

**Exhibit 23 (p. 76) — Where a grouping comes from determines its order.** Three diagrams: Effect over Causes 1–3 (time order); XYZ Company over Divisions A–C (structural order); universe of problems split into "these three" vs "all others" (degree order). Lesson: three analytical activities → three orders.

**pp. 78–79 — Consultant's 8-step Phase I productivity plan.** Before: interview personnel; trace work flow; identify critical functions; analyze org structure; understand services and measures; assess performance; identify problems and causes; identify and justify opportunity areas. After:
```
In Phase I we will identify potential opportunities to improve productivity
  1. Determine the business's critical functions            (orig 3)
     - Interview key personnel                               (1)
     - Map transactions and work flow                        (2)
  2. Identify weaknesses in performing those functions      (7)
     - Lay out the organization structure                    (4)
     - Pin down services and how performance is measured     (5)
     - Gauge performance levels                              (6)
  3. Recommend practical changes                            (8)
```
Lesson: too many items and mixed levels; pulling out mini-processes makes completeness checkable.

**pp. 79–80 — Strategic planning "timing cycle."** Before: 7 items: perceive need; develop strategy; implement; market acceptance and high growth; slower growth/maturity; high cash generation; decline. Diagnosis by role-play: 3 company actions + 4 product-life-cycle results. After:
```
Strategic planning involves recognizing a cycle
  1. Perceive the need
  2. Develop a strategy for a responsive product/service
  3. Implement the strategy
  4. Assess the market's reaction
  5. Change the strategy to match the reaction
```
Lesson: results aren't steps; convert them into the action of assessing them, and drop a stage attribute (cash generation).

**p. 80 — "Business definition" list with no top point.** Before: 4 points (relies on creative segmentation of demand and supply; changes over time with life cycle and competitive dynamics; not necessarily unique within an industry; influenced by marketer's strength vs competition). Justifying the order (segment → respond to change → assess position) reveals the topic. After:
```
Defining what business you are in requires careful analysis
  1. To identify market segments
  2. To assess your competitive position in each segment
  3. To track changes in position over time
```
Lesson: forcing an order surfaces the message and lets you judge completeness.

**p. 81 — Investment evaluation critique (presentation check).** Before: topic "traditional investment evaluation" with three peers: technically flawed; conceptually simplistic; leads to misleading prescriptions. After:
```
Traditional investment evaluation leads to misleading prescriptions
  1. Its underlying concepts are simplistic   (stage: establish concept)
  2. Its technique is often unsound           (stage: develop technique)
  [stage: apply technique — no comment → ask about it]
```
Lesson: the effect moves to the top; mapping to a process (concept → technique → application) exposes an unexamined stage.

**Exhibit 24 (p. 82) — Akron Tire and Rubber org chart.** Company → Tire / Housewares / Sports Equipment divisions, each → Research / Production / Marketing. Lesson: division should yield MECE units (no overlaps, nothing left out).

**p. 83 — City departments and radar set.** City: housing, transportation, education, recreation, personal health, environmental health, ordered by when a new city would need to attend to each. Radar: modulator → RF oscillator → scanning antenna → receiver → indicator, ordered by signal and power flow. Lesson: impose an order to check exhaustiveness; order functional parts by how they work.

**pp. 84–85 — Sinai map passage.** The peninsula's "contexts" (Egyptian, Israeli, Saudi, land bridge) are listed clockwise from top left as the eye scans a map. Lesson: structure can be described along the reader's visual path.

**pp. 85–86 — City government restructuring.** Before: 25 departments under 23 committees. After: 6 committees under Policy and Finance, 6 matching departments, two support committees, an administrative arm under a Chief Executive. Recommendations ordered by drawing sequence:
```
To improve the City's management system, the Council should:
  1. Consolidate the committees into six service committees reporting to a Policy and Finance Committee
  2. Group departments into six program administrations matching the committees
  3. Structure internal services
     - Create a General Purposes Committee
     - Redirect the Personnel Committee to a more positive role (worker motivation)
  4. Appoint a Chief Executive to head the permanent staff
```
Lesson: equal, simultaneous changes take "drawing order."

**pp. 86–87 — Transportation department assignment objectives.** Before: 5 objectives (review maintenance and construction field ops; check field engineers' flexibility for daily operations; review preliminary engineering, design, environmental process, right-of-way, traffic management; review organization structure; identify strengths and weaknesses in each area). Item 5 spans all others and is set aside; the subjects map to Design → Construct → Operate → Maintain. After:
```
Determine whether the Department is properly organized and managed
to carry out its four activities
  1. Design
  2. Construct
  3. Operate
  4. Maintain
```
Lesson: identify the structure the subjects belong to; organization becomes the question asked of each part.

**pp. 87–89 — Plastic bottle venture (11 "risks and constraints").** Step 1, why is each bad? → high cost (1,7); legally barred (2); lower sales or price (3); low sales (4); high investment / low ROI (5); lower EPS (6); must borrow (8); lower price (9,10); low margins, lower P/E (11). Step 2, place on trees:
```
ROI (5) = Profits ÷ Investment (5)
  Profits = Sales (3,4) × Margins (11)
    Margins = Price (3,9,10) − Cost (1,7)
Share price (6) = EPS (6) × P/E ratio (11)
(Borrowing (8) fits under profits if an interest/tax layer is added)
```
After:
```
We should think carefully before entering the plastic bottle business
  - If nonreturnables are banned, we may be barred from it
  - Even without a ban, it would erode our profitability
      - Near term: EPS falls
      - Over time: ROI falls
```
Lesson: imposing a known structure turns a mess into a message and exposes the omission (no analysis of plastic's positive effect on sales; the company actually entered and thrived).

**Exhibit 25 (p. 89) — Classification narrows the universe.** 3,000 organization problems → "3 that…" vs "2,997 others"; the three share a rankable characteristic. Lesson: a class grouping is a bifurcation; prove it by defining the characteristic and checking membership.

**p. 90 — Telecom billing system.** "Should be broadly useful": meet customer needs; satisfy internal management; conform to regulations. The order asserts customer > management > regulator; beneath it, design criteria → functional vs other. Lesson: degree order communicates priority.

**pp. 90–91 — Vendor Capture "reasons."** Don't adopt Vendor Capture: (1) warehouses too small and badly located; (2) even if not, it means double handling; (3) even granting that, the savings are negligible. Order follows an existing structure: warehouse → process within it → savings computed from it. Lesson: a "class" may legitimately come from a structure, if ordered by it.

**p. 91 — Investment evaluation "misleading prescriptions."** Before: 4 rules of thumb (invest wherever returns beat cost of capital; better quantification of uncertainty is key; planning and capital budgeting are separate, budgeting being financial; top management should challenge numbers not thinking). Reworded as results: the financial focus encourages investment; emphasizes quantifying uncertainty; separates planning from budgeting; leads management to focus on numbers. Three are stages of a decision process. After:
```
A purely financial approach to investment evaluation can produce poor
resource-allocation decisions, because it:
  1. Makes quantified risk and uncertainty the basis for choosing projects
  2. Steers top management toward the numbers instead of the reasoning
  3. Promotes any investment whose expected return beats the cost of
     capital, regardless of other factors
```
(The planning/budgeting item drops out.) Lesson: reword to reveal the true source; time order yields a sharper top point.

**p. 92 — Buyers' complaints sorted.** Types: bad timing (1,3); poor data (2,4,6); unhelpful format (5,7,8). Three orderings by question:
```
Q: why is the report useless?   data → format → timing   (order of production)
Q: why do buyers dislike it?    timing → format → data   (order of reading: arrives late; hard to navigate; contents wrong)
Q: how do we repair it?         format → data → timing   (order of fixing: choose data and layout; make data reliable; ship on time)
```
Lesson: subgroups' order follows the process the question implies.

**p. 93 — New York's decline.** Before: "many and complex" causes, 8 items (wages; energy/rent/land; congestion costs; no modern factory space; taxes; technological change; new economic centers in the Southwest and West; shift of life to the suburbs). Types: high costs (1,2,3,5); unsuitable area (4,6,8); alternative choice (7). After (order of importance):
```
New York's decline has clear, traceable causes
  1. Costs are high
  2. Conditions for working there are difficult
  3. Other locations offer attractive alternatives
```
Lesson: identify type → group → order; the top point changes from a hedge ("many and complex") to a claim.

## Nuances and exceptions

- **Rule numbering differs across chapters.** Ch 1 lists logical order as the third pyramid rule (after summaries and same-kind groupings); Ch 6 opens by calling it the second rule, and Ch 7 calls summarizing the first. The substance is the same; only the count shifts (Ch 6/7 appear to fold "same kind of idea" into the ordering and summarizing tests).
- **Listing is fine as a first step.** Minto doesn't ban lists in drafting; she bans leaving them unexamined (p. 73).
- **Don't over-plan before drafting.** Structure only to one level below the Key Line, then write; scrutiny comes after (p. 73).
- **Orders can combine.** A grouping may use more than one order (e.g., time order laid over a structure), but at least one must be present (pp. 77, 81).
- **Four or five, not seven.** Minto cites the Magic Number Seven but personally recommends a ceiling of four or five per grouping (p. 78).
- **Strongest-first can bend.** Reversing to build to a climax is legitimate when you deliberately want emotional impact; it's a style choice, not logic (p. 90).
- **Class groupings by common origin are allowed.** Calling process- or structure-derived items "reasons" is acceptable if you know the true source and order accordingly (p. 90).
- **Order is question-dependent.** The same subgroups can correctly take different orders depending on whether the document explains causes, describes the reader's experience, or prescribes a fix (p. 92).
- **When neither degree nor time applies** (equal, simultaneous structural changes), "drawing order" is the fallback (p. 86).
- **Imposing structure can reveal the conclusion is wrong**, not just badly expressed (plastic bottles, p. 89). Finding the message is the precondition for testing it.
- **A process-derived critique may legitimately skip a stage** (nothing wrong there), but the reviewer should still ask (p. 81).
- **Points can drop out.** Restructuring often shows an item doesn't belong (cash generation, p. 80; planning/budgeting separation, p. 91; "strengths and weaknesses," p. 86). Don't force every original item into the new structure.

> **Skill note:** For a review skill, the cheapest high-yield check is "name the order of every grouping." Groups that fail it should trigger P2, then P3, P5, or P6 depending on whether the items are actions, a long class list, or a free-association mess.
