# Summarizing Grouped Ideas (Chapter 7)

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 7 "Summarizing Grouped Ideas", book pp. 94–118. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `SUM`.

## When a skill needs this

- Reviewing a document whose section heads or slide titles read like "Three key issues" / "Our recommendations" / "Next steps": blank labels instead of claims.
- Rewriting a list of actions (steps, recommendations, objectives, workplan tasks) so each is a concrete end product and the heading states the effect they jointly achieve.
- Turning a list of findings or conclusions ("our conclusions," "assessment results") into a grouped set with a real insight at the top.
- Checking a workplan or proposal that sorts actions into Tasks / Objectives / Benefits columns, or states work as a list of questions.
- Deciding whether a summary sentence actually follows from (and is sufficient for) the points beneath it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Intellectually blank assertion (IBA) | A summary that names only the kind and number of ideas below ("there are three problems"), not their essence. |
| Summary point | A statement that expresses what the ideas below jointly imply (inductive) or conclude (deductive); it completes the thinking. |
| Action idea | A statement telling the reader to do something (step, recommendation, objective, change, task). |
| Situation idea | A statement telling the reader about something (reason, problem, conclusion, finding, characteristic). |
| Effect | What a MECE set of actions jointly produces; the only valid summary of action ideas. |
| Unique closed system | A MECE action set together with its resulting effect: performing exactly those actions yields exactly that effect. |
| Hierarchy of closed systems | A long process seen as nested effect/cause groups, where each effect is also a cause one level up (Exhibit 29). |
| End-product wording | Phrasing an action so it names the tangible result in hand when it's done, letting completion and sufficiency be judged. |
| Levels of action | Distinguishing actions done *before* the next (same level) from actions done *so that* the next can happen (lower level). |
| Structural similarity | The shared kind of subject, predicate (action or object), or implied judgment that links a set of situation statements. |
| "Same kind of" | Not identical; belonging to one category, describable by one plural noun. |
| Closer links | Sub-similarities within a list that let you form subgroups before stating the overall inference. |
| Inductive leap | Putting into words the implication of a similarity that isn't obvious; often launched by visualizing the source of the relationship. |
| Action ideas in disguise | A list presented as a class of situations (e.g., "variables") that really comprises steps toward an effect. |

## Concepts

### Chapter opening: the summary rule and why writers dodge it (p. 94)

- Minto now addresses the pyramid's first rule: each idea at a level must summarize the ideas grouped beneath it, because it was in fact derived from them.
- **Deductive grouping:** the idea above is easy. It is a simple summary that rests mainly on the final conclusion.
- **Inductive grouping:** the parent idea has to spell out what follows from the way the grouped statements relate. **Writing the summary of an inductive group is where the thinking actually gets finished.**
- Most writers stop at grouping. Often the ideas share only a general, not a specific, relationship, so they don't truly belong together and can't be summarized. Even when they do belong, finding the summary is hard work. So people fall back on intellectually blank assertions, headings of the form "the plan has four goals," "the department faces two problems," "six changes are proposed."
- Why "blank": such sentences label the category of what follows instead of distilling it. Both reader and writer pay for that.

### Avoid Intellectually Blank Assertions (pp. 95–96)

**Harm to the reader (p. 95):**
- An IBA gives the reader nothing to hold on to and nothing to engage with, and it makes it quite likely your actual point never lands.
- Radio-exchange illustration: a speaker reports that a biographer (John Wain) thinks he is well suited to write about Samuel Johnson "for three reasons" (same poor Staffordshire background, same Oxford education, same literary tastes). The second speaker retorts with a joke about Staffordshire, and the conversation moves on. Mechanism: the listener waits for an idea, gets only "three reasons," then hears the first reason and treats it as the main point, half-ignoring the rest. Replies target whatever point was heard, so people talk past each other.
- Suppose the speaker had instead claimed Wain is suited because he and Johnson are fundamentally alike as people. Listeners would still hear the support, but their replies would address *that* claim.
- A genuine summary leaves the reader somewhat better prepared to take in the support. "For three reasons" is lifeless in sound and in substance, and a document dotted with such lines is tedious to read.

**Harm to the writer, the more important reason (pp. 95–96):**
- IBAs **mask unfinished thinking**, so the writer forfeits a chance to push the reasoning forward systematically and inventively.
- A formal summary almost always stimulates further thought. Once you have an insight you can carry it forward two ways (Exhibit 26):
  - **Deductively:** comment on it ("…therefore?").
  - **Inductively:** look for other points of the same kind, building a new grouping under a still-higher idea.
- That only works if you have a true summary drawn from a proper grouping.
- Exhibit 27 (two "organization problems"): the writer knew the statement was blank. Rewriting should have been easy if the two items were (a) both organization problems and (b) logically ordered. No order could be found. Asked about the origin of the items and what made them alike, the writer realized the real category was "areas needing more delegation," not organization problems in general. There were four such areas, and only one had been properly identified. Insight: the company's main organizational problem is its inability to delegate. With the problem clearly named, attention could shift to solving it.

**What to do (p. 97):**
1. Trace where the grouping came from and confirm it is MECE, meaning its order rests on a sound process, structure, or classification (Ch 6).
2. Look at the **kind of statement**. Whatever its origin, an idea is expressed either as an **action** (tells the reader to *do* something) or a **situation** (tells the reader *about* something).
   - For **action** ideas, the summary is the **effect** the actions produce once done.
   - For **situation** ideas, the summary is **what their likeness implies**.
- Exhibit 28: Deductive reasoning → argument chain → summary. Inductive reasoning splits in two: action ideas (causes) → **Effect**; situation ideas (a class) → **Inference**.

### State the Effect of Actions (pp. 98–110)

- Most ideas in business writing are actions: things called steps, recommendations, objectives, changes. They appear in manuals, action plans, system descriptions, problem-solving approaches. Minto rates the work of expressing, relating, and summarizing action ideas as the most demanding thinking there is, citing the abundance of unreadable manuals and the failure of Management by Objectives.
- **Why it's hard:** every action serves some purpose, so a set of actions is always summarized by the result of performing them. A MECE set of actions plus its effect forms a **unique closed system**: take exactly those actions and you can be sure of that effect. A big process is a **hierarchy of closed systems** (Exhibit 29: groups of causes under an effect, which is itself one of the causes under a higher effect, and so on up to the top EFFECT).
- The trouble: within a group, actions connect to one another **only through their shared result**. Every action has the same surface form ("you should…" / "we will…" + verb), whether it functions as cause or effect. So **examining actions one by one tells you nothing about whether they belong together**; only the intended effect can settle that.
- The circularity: you can't judge whether actions are missing until the effect is stated, yet the effect depends on which actions you assemble. With long, many-step processes, this interdependence makes sorting out your thinking a nightmare.
- Three techniques ease it:
  1. Make each action's wording concrete first, and only then try to relate the actions.
  2. Spot the evident cause-effect clusters, which keeps every group to five steps or under.
  3. Read the effect straight off the action statements.

#### Make the Wording Specific (pp. 99–104)

- In a cause-effect hierarchy you should be able to say of every group, bottom up: these particular actions produce the effect one level up; that effect and its siblings produce the next one; and so on to the top. Points in a group must be mutually exclusive (no overlaps), and each group must be collectively exhaustive relative to its summary.
- **You can only tell whether a group of actions is complete if its effect is concrete enough to picture as a physical deliverable.** Saying three steps are meant "to improve profits" fails: a 10% and a 2% gain are both improvements, yet need different steps. State instead something like a 10% profit improvement by a given date, so you can judge whether the steps would produce it.
- Numbers aren't required; some observable sign of completion always is. Technique: **picture a real person performing the action, see what they hold at the end, and word the action to reflect that end product.**
- **Gibberish test (p. 100):** a sentence urging the development of a "world consciousness" in which every individual recognizes their place in the global community. What are we to do? How would we know it's done? Could you tell someone who has it from someone who doesn't? If not, you don't know what the author means, and you can't answer "How?" to fill in the steps beneath it. It may stir feeling, but it carries no thinking.
- Vague steps compound the problem. Task Force example: to keep conflict from turning into confrontation, a task force must "be able to" handle varied attitudes, build rapport, develop interviewing skills, plan and run interviews, gain agreement while staying objective. No visualizable end products, no point at which a step is clearly done, and since the objective itself is unclear, no way to judge sufficiency.
- The remedy is always the same: **insist on identifying the deliverable or stopping point that shows the step is finished, and phrase the step as that** (Exhibit 30, below).
- Benefits of end-product wording:
  - Each rewrite conjures a picture, and a picture makes the text livelier.
  - More important for the writer, it **stimulates further thinking**. E.g., once regions have planning responsibility, you can picture them producing annual plans and ask: how will I know they're the right kind of plan? What happens when the plans come back? Which suggests more steps (a system for setting annual planning objectives; a planning review group). Picturing "strengthen regional effectiveness" shows nothing concrete enough to suggest the next step.
- Since any action can be both cause and effect (Exhibit 29), **write every step, at every level, to imply an end product.** Without the effect stated specifically you can't tell whether all steps are present.
- **Accounts-receivable example (pp. 102–103):** six steps (find and chase overdue accounts; age the larger accounts routinely; send reminders scaled to sum and delay; chase overdue accounts (again); have directors settle the oldest ones; hand some to an outside collector). The objective is unclear (what concretely does chasing an account involve?), as are most steps. The author's chart (amount × months overdue) and accompanying explanation (escalate from normal billing → accounting note → salesman visit → director call → collection agency as age increases) lead to a clearer two-step version: sort accounts by age and amount; assign collection responsibility by seriousness (Accounting, Salesmen, Directors, Collection Agency). The top, "reduce accounts receivable," is still wrong: collecting a single bill reduces receivables, and neither step directly collects a bill. What the company will actually hold at the end is **a system for following up overdue accounts.** Seeing that, you can ask whether the two steps suffice to make a system. Probably not: some follow-up step is needed, e.g., telling the sales force to stop calling on chronic nonpayers.
- Minto gives this point maximum emphasis: unless the wording names end products, there is no objective way to decide whether the step list is complete.
- **Questions are not a shortcut (pp. 103–104).** Some writers phrase actions as questions, reasoning that each answer is an end product. In practice it just piles on complexity: the end products still have to be imagined and checked for desirability. Stakeholder-alliance example: five questions (owners' view; reputation and market response; top managers' willingness; how to win over those threatened; reactions of customers, suppliers, partners, financiers, competitors). Test: imagine sending five assistants out, one per question. You get back five unrelated things. Instead imagine one not-very-bright assistant, little time, no budget, and the goal of ending with a plan to get stakeholders to see the alliance's benefits. You'd direct: (1) identify which groups the alliance touches; (2) predict each group's reaction; (3) work out how to win each group's active support. Now anyone can follow it; step 1 is already done (the list of groups), step 2 is the assistant's job, step 3 must wait for step 2.
- Restated principle: the easiest way to clarify action ideas is to picture yourself doing the action and word it as the end product you hold when finished.

#### Distinguish the Levels of Action (pp. 104–107)

- The usual habit is to list all steps in the order they'll be done. That generally puts causes and effects on the same level.
- Deliberately separate levels so no level has more than five steps. The shape of the whole process then becomes visible at a glance, and each summary has fewer items to cover.
- **The test:** sibling = performed ahead of the next listed step (*before*). Child = performed to make the next step possible (*so that*).
- Telecommunications program example (ten steps → three groups; see Worked examples). Afterward you grasp the program quickly *and* can judge omissions: how do you identify business tasks needing support? How do you build an organizational control framework? Perhaps a central manager is needed. (In the rewrite that step carries a "(?)" because the original list had no corresponding item.)
- **Don't overdo it by classifying actions.** Consulting firms in particular love to split a project into Tasks vs Objectives vs Benefits. This assumes a clean fence between the three, with Tasks achieved first, then Objectives, then Benefits. That sequence is real, but the implied hierarchy slices the pyramid **horizontally** (a row of benefits, above a row of objectives, above a row of tasks) instead of vertically. It doesn't clarify anything. It implies you could tell, just by looking, whether a given action is a Task, Objective, or Benefit, which is nonsense. **Actions don't form classes; the only legitimate tie among them is the specific effect they jointly bring about.** Sorting them into categories guarantees duplication, since no inherent feature marks one action as a Task and another as an Objective or a Benefit. **Organize around end-product actions and nothing else.**
- Strategic-planning-training example (pp. 106–107): six Tasks, five Objectives, three Benefits. Technique: **strip each item down to its core and look for duplicates.** Repetitions and levels of abstraction become visible, and the material rebuilds into a pyramid around end-product actions (see Worked examples).

#### Summarize Directly (pp. 107–110)

- With the steps sorted, the hardest part remains: stating the overall effect. Minto offers no foolproof technique, only two conditions:
  1. The grouping must be MECE.
  2. The summary names the **immediate** result of doing the actions, phrased as an end product.
- Then verify by weighing the points against one another. In the training example: trained people + an adapted planning system + a handbook should put the company *in a position* to devise the right strategies. That is not the same as saying it *will* devise them. Neither rule guarantees you'll find the right summary; Minto offers before/after examples and her reasoning instead.
- **London equity sales (pp. 107–108):** "To improve equity sales in London we should rank customers' revenue potential by area, decide desired penetration in each area, reassign salesmen accordingly." Minto's reasoning: these won't "improve sales," since a single extra sale would count as improving sales. Ask: *I rank, decide, reassign in order to make what happen? Or, if I don't, what won't happen?* Answer: put resources where customer potential is greatest. The new statement presents an idea instead of an IBA; it prompts the reader to ask "How?" (so they're primed for the steps), and the writer can check the steps produce the result.
- **UK blue-collar training (p. 108):** three complex sentences (show top management the government considers training top priority; set a framework within which suppliers develop suitable courses; create upward pressure from the workforce). Because the sentences are complex, first extract their essence: (1) isolate the real subjects (top management, suppliers, workforce) and ask why these three and no others → they're all **participants in the UK training system**; (2) isolate how each sentence acts on its participant (demonstrate importance *to*, establish a framework *for*, create pressure *from*) and ask what's alike → **they are all incentives**. Summary: improving the training environment means giving every party in the system a reason to back training. It raises the question "what would we do?" and both pulls the reader through and allows a completeness check.
- **Product development issues (pp. 108–109):** six "how to…" issues (incorporate desired features from corporate and market views; prioritize resources across projects; shorten development while meeting marketing's needs; organize R&D to hit lead times; keep people informed for coherent deliverables; motivate scientists and managers into partnership). Process:
  1. State each at its barest: develop the right products; allocate the right resources; do it quickly; do it on time; market it effectively; get scientists and managers to cooperate.
  2. Find subgroups (note issue 3 splits in two: 3a speed, 3b marketing requirements): identify products that meet market needs (1, 3b); develop them in the shortest time (3a; with resources 2, R&D organization 4, cooperation 6); offer them to the market compellingly (5).
  3. Find the summary: if we do these three, what do we get? A product the market wants, ahead of everyone, with the highest sales. Bring in what business people generally know (being first to market earns a premium; product life cycles keep shrinking, so cutting development time is a priority). Inferred message: the central question is whether the company can organize to out-respond competitors in the market, broken into three yes/no sub-questions (right products? avoidable delays removed? sales-maximizing marketing?).
- Wrap-up (p. 110): Minto admits clear action writing is hard mental work, but argues the reader's experience of the unclear alternative justifies the effort. The three steps: **phrase points as end products; separate the levels of abstraction; derive the effect straight from the actions.**
- Bridge: inferring from conclusions is a comparable but lighter task. The target shifts from the result a set of actions will produce to the insight a set of like statements points to.

### Look for the Similarity in Conclusions (pp. 110–118)

- Situation ideas are describable by plural nouns such as reasons, problems, conclusions. You grouped them because you believed each shares a characteristic.
- Recap of Ch 6 classification (Exhibit 31, same picture as Exhibit 25): "the company has three organization problems" bifurcates a universe (3,000 problems → 3 that share a rankable characteristic vs 2,997 others).
- So calling them organization problems reveals **nothing significant**. Three-step thinking process:
  1. **List**: a simple list of points that may be worth considering (classification).
  2. **Prove they belong together**: name the shared link that sets them apart from everything else in the universe.
  3. **State the wider significance** of that common link, i.e., create a new idea.
  Only then is the thinking complete.
- Most business writers halt after step 1. Sometimes they don't know the later steps exist; more often the later steps are simply hard. The three moves required:
  - identify the structural similarity binding the ideas;
  - search for tighter links among those similarities;
  - take the inductive leap to the top point.

#### Find the Structural Similarity (pp. 111–112)

- A shared property is what makes ideas a legitimate group. Since ideas are written as sentences with a subject/predicate structure (Ch 5), the shared property usually shows up because the sentences all:
  - share a **kind of subject**;
  - share a **kind of predicate** (action or object); or
  - share a **kind of implied judgment**.
- "Same kind of" means same category or describable by the same plural noun, not identical.
- **Decision rule:** identical subjects → seek the link among the predicates. Identical predicates (actions or objects) → seek it among the subjects. Neither identical → seek it in the judgment each statement carries.
- Harder than it sounds, especially when the points are nicely phrased, because **polished wording dulls critical reading**. (We've all been lulled by the Five Forces, the Seven S's, the Four P's, the Seven Habits.) Look past the phrasing to the skeleton of each claim.
- **Planning and control example (pp. 111–112):** a heading announcing four features of a new planning and control system: an annual cycle with matching controls; plans assembled through an integrated system; plans prepared under firm direction from division leadership; a system that separates current practice from intended change. It sounds plausible and elegant, but the top is blank. The subjects are all the same (plans/the planning system), so the link must be in the predicates: annual, integrated, top-down, separates present from future. Stripped of style, the grouping supports no message. Ask: what's significant about a system with these four traits? **Being true isn't the same as being relevant**, and the blank top shuts down further thought. After much rewriting the author's real point emerged: the system exists to make every unit concentrate on raising profits, which it does by demanding a yearly profit plan from each unit, reconciling those plans at each reporting level, and holding managers to them.
- Rule of thumb: **failure to find a clear link among items labeled problems, reasons, conclusions, etc. always signals a defective grouping that needs more thought.**
- This lack of upward push matters because the impetus to think further is the main reason for drawing inferences at all. A grouping that doesn't force a summary insight can't guide new thinking forward.

#### Look for Closer Links (pp. 113–114)

- Longer lists: after locating where the similarity lies, look for closer links between the similarities, which yields subgroups.
- **Information-system complaints (p. 113):** five complaints; the similarity is in the predicates about the information (needs updating; now required; ought to be collected; not adequate; needed). Two distinct subgroups: information that **doesn't exist** (2, 3, 5) and information that **exists but is inadequate** (1, 4). Those two subgroups are themselves a class, so ask: what makes exactly these two kinds of defect belong together? What made the author see them as one group? Possibly that both make the information **useless for planning**. Top point: in its current form the system gives planners information they can't use (Why?) → some needed information is missing altogether; the rest is available but not good enough.
- With the top point visible, **apply order to the points below to find overlooked defects**. A logical next category for the author to check: information that exists and is adequate but is poorly presented.
- **Value of a proper summary (p. 113):** (a) for the writer, it is how you learn your own view; (b) for the reader, it signals up front how to interpret the points, so they are absorbed more readily and trusted more; (c) with a collectively exhaustive grouping, there is little left for the reader to argue with; (d) most of all, the document stops being dull.
- **IS Assessment example (p. 114):** eight boring findings (need committed due dates from IS project managers; inexperience in the PM role; a culture that lets dates slip instead of finding creative alternatives; inconsistent use of methodology and tools; PMs have never installed mission-critical systems this large or complex; PMs have little or no PM training or practice; schedules for mission-critical projects are high-level and risky; the methodology doesn't support client/server techniques like RAD, JAD, prototyping). Reduce to bare structural elements (need due dates; inexperienced PMs; danger of slipping dates; inconsistent tool use; never done anything this big; limited experience; afraid of slipping dates; lack tools for the work), then regroup (see Worked examples). Result reads clearly even to someone who doesn't know the subject.
- When what the similarities imply is not obvious, expressing it takes an **inductive leap**. What usually launches the leap is **picturing where the relationship comes from**.

#### Make the Inductive Leap (pp. 115–118)

- **Automotive aftermarket (pp. 115–116):** a consultant's five conclusions (market large and growing attractively; aftermarket profitable; key characteristics indicate high entry barriers; overall trends favorable but uncertainties cloud some segments' outlooks; overall attractive but highly fragmented). Two groups: positives (large, growing, profitable, favorable trends, attractive: 1, 2, 4, 5) and negatives (barriers, uncertainties, fragmentation: 3, 4, 5). Positives summarize at once: the market is attractive (picture a circle). Negatives don't group easily, so visualize them: fragmented = the circle has segments; uncertainties = some segments look different from the rest; barriers = a line blocking entry. The picture yields two conclusions: **just certain segments are attractive**, and **those segments are hard to break into**. Do they relate inductively? Is anything the same about being attractive and being hard to enter? No. So they can only relate **deductively**: "only some parts are attractive → they're hard to enter → therefore…?" The reasoning was never finished. Should the client walk away? Acquire its way in? Engage the consultant to craft an entry plan? Lesson: the danger of settling for a blank assertion instead of pushing the thinking to completion.
- **Action ideas in disguise (p. 116):** a set dressed up as situation statements can turn out to be steps. Handle it first as a class; if a joint effect becomes visible as you work, recast the set as actions. Resource-allocation example: a heading announcing four "variables" to manage (timing and order of activities; what individuals are to do; information requirements; how decisions get made). Ask why these four and no others, and what they share. Restate each more concretely in search of an order, and they reveal themselves as four steps. Rewrite: the central management job in resource allocation is getting the right people involved early and substantially (How?) → set out the order and timing of project-planning work; pinpoint where decisions are required; name who will help make them; specify what information they'll need.
- **Situation ideas can still be in time order (pp. 116–117).** Sales-proposal example: seven statements about the company's proposals (better opportunity analysis to maximize resource use; coordinate all proposals with one quality process, content and packaging standards, continuous improvement; maximize reuse of proposal information; share knowledge across the company and industry; more cost-effective preparation; faster response; focus the process on customer needs as a sales tool rather than technical transfer). Applying the usual routine (find the likenesses, then infer) gives three ideas, ordered by when each activity happens in the proposal process: proposals fail as sales tools → weak message (1, 4, 7); unremarkable appearance (2); too slow (3, 5, 6).
- **Permissible relaxation (p. 117):** Minto expects you to apply the discipline with some flexibility. The issue isn't that it lacks value; full precision simply isn't always required, since readers fill in an overall pattern on their own. **Where you're confident the logic holds, a looser summary is acceptable.** E.g., proposals can project a new image to customers on condition that the message is stronger, the presentation is striking, and delivery is fast.
- **Closing message (pp. 117–118):** grouping alone doesn't convey meaning; readers won't work out the significance for you. Any group carries an implicit overall point, which expresses how its ideas relate. Work out that relationship privately, then spell it out on the page. Put one question to every grouping: **why these ideas, and only these?** Only two answers are legitimate:
  - They have a feature in common that nothing else shares → the summary is the **insight** gained from thinking about what that common feature means.
  - They make up the complete set of actions jointly required for a desired result → the summary names that **direct result**.
- Minto's promise: hold every grouping to this justification and your writing will be clear, and will often surface insights you hadn't recognized before drafting.

## Rules

- **SUM-1**: Never top a grouping with an intellectually blank assertion, i.e., one that only names the kind and number of ideas below ("four risks," "six initiatives," "key findings"). *Why:* it doesn't anchor or engage the reader, invites them to latch onto the first point, and hides incomplete thinking. *Check:* delete the list beneath; if the heading alone tells the reader nothing about the substance, it's blank. (pp. 94–95)
- **SUM-2**: Summarize a deductive grouping by restating the chain with the weight on its final conclusion. *Why:* the deduction's point is its "therefore." *Check:* does the summary carry the conclusion, not the premises? (p. 94)
- **SUM-3**: Summarize an inductive grouping by stating what the relationship among its ideas implies. The summary must complete the thinking. *Why:* the summary is where the new idea is created. *Check:* could the summary be written without having read the points? If so, it's too generic. (p. 94)
- **SUM-4**: Before summarizing, confirm the grouping is MECE through a valid order (process, structure, or classification). *Why:* ideas with only a general relationship can't be summarized. *Check:* apply `05-logical-order.md` ORD-3 to the group first. (pp. 94, 97)
- **SUM-5**: Classify each grouping as action ideas (do) or situation ideas (about). Summarize actions by their effect; summarize situations by what their similarity implies. *Why:* the two kinds relate differently and need different summaries. *Check:* label the group; confirm the summary type matches (effect vs inference). (p. 97)
- **SUM-6**: Judge whether actions belong together only against the effect they're meant to produce. Never by how the actions look. *Why:* all actions look alike ("you should" + verb); their only significant relationship is their joint effect. *Check:* write the effect first; strike any action that doesn't contribute to it; add actions it still needs. (pp. 98–99)
- **SUM-7**: Word every action, at every level of the hierarchy, as an end product: what a real person would hold when the step is done. *Why:* only end-product wording lets you judge completion and whether steps are sufficient; it also sparks further thinking. *Check:* for each step, name the tangible deliverable or cutoff point. If you can't, rewrite. (pp. 99–103)
- **SUM-8**: State effects specifically enough to judge whether the steps beneath would achieve them (amount, deadline, or a concrete completion test). *Why:* "improve profits" is met by 2% or 10%, which need different steps. *Check:* could two different step-sets both "satisfy" the effect? If yes, sharpen it. (p. 99)
- **SUM-9**: Reject action statements you can't visualize: if you can't tell someone who has done it from someone who hasn't, or can't answer "How?" beneath it, it has no intellectual content. *Why:* neither reader nor writer can derive or check the steps. *Check:* apply the "who has done it?" and "How?" tests. (p. 100)
- **SUM-10**: Don't phrase action steps as a list of questions to avoid specific wording. Convert them into a sequence of end-product steps toward one deliverable. *Why:* questions return unrelated answers and add a layer of complexity; you still must picture the end products. *Check:* "five assistants" test: would separate answers combine into one result? If not, restructure as "one assistant, little time, no budget" steps. (pp. 103–104)
- **SUM-11**: Separate levels of action: steps done *before* the next are siblings; steps done *so that* the next can be produced go beneath it. Keep each level to five or fewer. *Why:* flat lists put causes beside their effects, obscuring structure and gaps. *Check:* for each step ask "before, or so that?"; count items per level. (p. 104)
- **SUM-12**: Don't organize actions into horizontal category bins such as Tasks / Objectives / Benefits. Organize vertically around end-product actions. *Why:* actions don't form classes; no inherent feature separates the three bins, so the bins breed repetition. *Check:* look for the same idea appearing in two or more columns. (pp. 105–107)
- **SUM-13**: To untangle repetitive or category-labeled action lists, pare each item to its barest essence, mark repetitions and levels, then rebuild one pyramid. *Why:* bare wording exposes duplicates and level differences that polished phrasing hides. *Check:* map every original item number into the rebuilt pyramid. (pp. 106–107)
- **SUM-14**: Summarize actions directly: with a MECE group beneath, name the *immediate* result of performing the actions, phrased as an end product. *Why:* indirect or distant effects can't be verified from the steps. *Check:* would doing exactly these steps produce exactly this result, no more and no less? (p. 107)
- **SUM-15**: Test the action summary against the points: reject any summary that a trivial result would satisfy (one extra sale "improves sales"; one paid bill "reduces receivables"). Don't overclaim: steps may put you "in a position to" achieve something without guaranteeing it. *Why:* the summary must be sufficient and accurate, not aspirational. *Check:* find the smallest outcome that technically meets the summary; if it's trivial, restate. (pp. 102, 107–108)
- **SUM-16**: Find an action summary by asking "I do these in order to make what happen?" and "If I don't, what won't happen?" *Why:* it forces the effect out of the actions. *Check:* the answer names a specific result, not a domain ("sales," "receivables"). (p. 108)
- **SUM-17**: When action sentences are complex, extract their essence first: isolate the subjects and ask what they share; isolate how each acts on its subject and ask what's alike; summarize from those commonalities. *Why:* complex wording hides the common pattern. *Check:* write the subject list and verb list separately; name each set's plural noun. (p. 108)
- **SUM-18**: Phrase the summary so it raises the question the grouping answers (How? Why? What would we do?). *Why:* it primes the reader for the support and lets the writer check the steps deliver the result. *Check:* does the natural reader question after the summary match what the group below answers? (pp. 108, 113, 116)
- **SUM-19**: For situation ideas, go past listing: prove the common link that separates these items from all others, then state its wider significance as a new idea. *Why:* classification alone reveals nothing; the thinking is only complete at step 3. *Check:* can you name (a) the specific shared link and (b) what it means? (p. 111)
- **SUM-20**: Find the structural similarity by comparing subjects, predicates, and implied judgments: same subjects → compare predicates; same predicates → compare subjects; neither → compare implied judgments. *Why:* sentence structure is where the shared property shows up. *Check:* tabulate subject / predicate / judgment for each item and look for the column with a shared "kind." (p. 111)
- **SUM-21**: Strip polished, framework-style language down to the bare structure before judging a grouping. *Why:* elegant phrasing lulls critical thinking and disguises the absence of a message. *Check:* rewrite each item in three to five plain words and reread the set. (pp. 111–112, 114)
- **SUM-22**: Don't accept a grouping just because each point is true. If no clear relationship emerges, the grouping is defective; rethink it. *Why:* truth isn't relevance, and an unrelated set can't push thinking upward. *Check:* ask "what is significant about something having all these properties?" If there's no answer, regroup. (p. 112)
- **SUM-23**: For longer situation lists, find closer links (subgroups) among the similarities, then ask why *these* subgroups and no others to reach the top inference. *Why:* subgroups are themselves a class whose shared property is the insight. *Check:* ≤5 subgroups, each with a named common link, plus a top point explaining why they belong together. (p. 113)
- **SUM-24**: After stating the summary, use it plus the order of the points to look for missing members. *Why:* a proper summary defines the class, making gaps visible (e.g., "exists and adequate but badly presented"). *Check:* enumerate the logical categories the summary implies; flag any not covered. (p. 113)
- **SUM-25**: When the implication of a similarity isn't obvious, visualize the source of the relationship (draw it) and read conclusions off the picture. *Why:* the visualization is the springboard for the inductive leap. *Check:* can you sketch the situation and state what the sketch shows in one or two sentences? (pp. 114–115)
- **SUM-26**: If the conclusions you reach share nothing in common, they relate deductively. Carry the chain to its explicit "therefore." *Why:* stopping before the conclusion is another form of blank assertion. *Check:* does the final point answer "therefore what?" (p. 116)
- **SUM-27**: Detect action ideas disguised as situations ("variables," "areas," "issues," "characteristics"). Restate them specifically; if you can picture the effect they jointly produce, switch to action form with the effect on top. *Why:* summarizing them as a class yields a blank or false insight. *Check:* try rewriting each as an imperative; if they then form a sequence toward one outcome, they're actions. (p. 116)
- **SUM-28**: Don't assume situation ideas must be in degree order. They can follow time order when they describe stages of an activity. *Why:* order follows the source, not the grammatical form. *Check:* do the subgroups map to stages of a process? (pp. 116–117)
- **SUM-29**: Ask of every grouping "why these ideas and no others?" If the answer is a shared characteristic, the summary is an insight about its significance. If the answer is joint necessity for a goal, the summary is the direct effect. *Why:* these are the only two legitimate justifications. *Check:* write the answer explicitly for each group. (p. 118)
- **SUM-30**: Use each real summary as a springboard: ask what follows from it (deductive comment) and what else is like it (inductive extension). *Why:* a key value of summarizing is that it pushes thinking forward. *Check:* for key summaries, record one "therefore" and one "what else of this kind?" (pp. 95–96)

## Procedures

### P1. Fix an intellectually blank assertion (pp. 94–97, 118)

1. Spot the IBA: a heading that states only the number and kind of items.
2. Validate the group (ORD-3): does it have an order? If not, ask what produced the ideas and what makes them similar. Expect to discover the true category (e.g., "areas needing more delegation," not "organization problems"), which may change the membership (two items became four).
3. Classify the group as action ideas or situation ideas (SUM-5); watch for actions in disguise (SUM-27).
4. Actions → P2 then P3. Situations → P4.
5. Replace the heading with the effect or insight. Check that it raises the question the group answers (SUM-18).

### P2. Clean up action wording (pp. 99–104)

1. For each action, picture a real person performing it. Write what they hold at the end.
2. Rewrite each action as that end product (see Exhibit 30 patterns).
3. If an action can't be visualized (can't tell who has done it; can't answer "How?"), push the author for what they mean or flag it as content-free.
4. If actions are phrased as questions, apply the five-assistants test. If answers wouldn't combine, rewrite as a sequence toward one deliverable (one-assistant test).
5. Ensure the effect at the top is specific (amount, date, or tangible completion test).

### P3. Structure and summarize a set of actions (pp. 104–110)

1. Number the original actions.
2. If they're bucketed into categories (Tasks/Objectives/Benefits), pare each to its barest essence and list repetitions.
3. For each pair: *before* (sibling) or *so that* (child)? Build effect/cause clusters; ≤5 per level.
4. Name the end product of each cluster.
5. Derive the overall effect directly: "I do these in order to make what happen? If I don't, what won't happen?"
6. If the sentences are complex: list subjects → common category; list verbs/relations → common category; combine into the effect.
7. Bring in relevant general knowledge of the domain to frame the effect (e.g., first-to-market premium).
8. Test: is the group MECE? Would doing exactly these produce exactly this? Reject summaries a trivial outcome satisfies; don't claim "will" when the steps only put you "in a position to."
9. Use the end products to look for missing steps (follow-ups, review mechanisms, owners).

### P4. Draw an inference from situation ideas (pp. 110–118)

1. Reduce each statement to its bare structure (three to five plain words).
2. Tabulate subject / predicate / implied judgment. Apply the decision rule (SUM-20) to locate the similarity.
3. If the list is long, find closer links: subgroups with a shared sub-property (e.g., "doesn't exist" vs "exists but inadequate"). Split items that bundle two ideas.
4. Ask why these subgroups and no others; name the common property; state its significance as the top point.
5. If no relationship appears, the grouping is wrong. Rethink membership (SUM-22).
6. If the implication won't come, visualize the situation (circles, segments, barriers) and read conclusions off the picture.
7. If the resulting conclusions share nothing, they're deductive. Finish the "therefore."
8. If the items turn out to be steps toward an effect, switch to P3.
9. Order the support (degree, or time if the items are stages), then use the summary to check for missing categories.

### P5. Final "why these and no others?" check (p. 118)

For each grouping, write one line:
- "These are all the X that share property P" → summary = significance of P; or
- "These are all the actions needed to achieve E" → summary = E, stated as an end product.
If neither line can be written, the grouping isn't finished.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| "There are three issues / five recommendations / our conclusions:" | Intellectually blank assertion | SUM-1, SUM-3, P1 |
| Reader responds only to the first bullet and ignores the rest | No summary to anchor the reader | SUM-1, SUM-18 |
| Heading can't be made specific because the items don't hang together | Grouping not valid/MECE | SUM-4, SUM-22, ORD-3 |
| Action list under a goal like "improve profits" or "reduce receivables" | Effect too vague to test sufficiency | SUM-8, SUM-15 |
| Steps like "strengthen effectiveness," "review processes," "tackle issues" | No end product; can't tell when done | SUM-7, SUM-9, P2 |
| Statement of aspiration with no observable completion ("develop a world consciousness") | Unvisualizable; no intellectual content | SUM-9 |
| Workplan written as a list of questions | Questions substituted for end-product steps | SUM-10 |
| Ten-step flat program list | Levels of action not distinguished | SUM-11 |
| Proposal with Tasks / Objectives / Benefits columns that repeat each other | Classifying actions | SUM-12, SUM-13 |
| Summary of actions claims more than the steps deliver ("will" vs "in a position to") | Indirect or overclaimed effect | SUM-14, SUM-15 |

## Templates and patterns

**Blank → substantive heading**

```
BLANK:    There are <N> <plural noun>.
          We recommend <N> <changes>.
          <Topic>: <N> reasons / our conclusions
ACTION:   To <goal>, we need to <direct effect stated as end product>.   (How?)
SITUATION:<Subject> <claim expressing what the shared property implies>. (Why?)
```

**Action summary frames**

```
<Specific effect: what will exist / be in hand> by <date> (to <measurable degree>)
  1. <End product 1>
  2. <End product 2>
  3. <End product 3>
Self-check: "I do 1–3 in order to make <effect> happen; if I don't, <effect> won't happen."
```

**Level-of-action test**

```
A before B   → siblings
A so that B  → A beneath B
Max 5 per level
```

**One-assistant sequencing frame** (replacing a question list)

```
Goal: end with <deliverable>
  1. List <the things / parties / items>        ← often already done
  2. Estimate / assess <their state or response>
  3. Determine <what to do about each>
```

**Situation-similarity worksheet**

```
| # | Bare statement | Subject | Predicate (action/object) | Implied judgment |
Rule: same subjects → compare predicates; same predicates → compare subjects;
      neither → compare judgments.
Subgroups (closer links): A = {…}, B = {…}
Why A and B and no others? → <shared property> → TOP POINT
Gap check: what other category does the top point imply? → <missing item?>
```

**Final justification line**

```
"These are the only <plural noun> that <share P>"  → summary = significance of P
"These are all the actions required to <E>"        → summary = E (direct, end-product)
```

**Exhibit 30: vague action → end-product action** (the "what was said / what was meant" pairs, p. 101, paraphrased)

| Vague wording | End-product wording |
|---|---|
| Strengthen regional effectiveness | Give the regions responsibility for planning |
| Reduce accounts receivable | Set up a system to follow up overdue accounts |
| Review management processes | Decide whether management processes need revising |
| Improve financial reporting | Install a system that warns early of change |
| Tackle strategic issues | Define a clear long-term strategy |
| Redeploy manpower resources | Place people in jobs of comparable responsibility |

## Worked examples

**pp. 94–95 — Radio exchange about a biography.** Before: "well placed to write this biography for three reasons" + background / education / tastes. After:
```
He is suited to write this biography because he and his subject are
fundamentally alike as people
  - Same modest regional background
  - Same university education
  - Same literary preferences
```
Lesson: "for three reasons" leaves listeners to latch onto the first reason; a real summary is what they respond to.

**Exhibit 26 (p. 96) — A summary point inspires further thinking.** Left: summary → "comment on the point?" → "therefore…?" rising to a new idea (deductive). Right: summary beside "points of the same kind?" under a new higher idea (inductive). Lesson: a true summary is the launch point for further deduction or induction.

**Exhibit 27 (p. 96) — Blank assertions hide incomplete thinking.** Before:
```
The company has two organization problems
  1. …
  2. …
```
After (true category found: areas needing more delegation, four of them):
```
Your central organizational problem: authority isn't being delegated
  1. … 2. … 3. … 4.
```
Lesson: forcing a real summary changed the category and the membership, and revealed the problem to solve.

**Exhibit 28 (p. 97) — Form of argument dictates the summarizing process.** Deductive: argument → summary. Inductive: action ideas (causes) → Effect; situation ideas (class) → Inference.

**Exhibit 29 (p. 98) — Actions are grouped by the result they jointly create.** Nested pyramid: three causes → Effect; that Effect + two causes → Effect; that Effect + two causes → top EFFECT. Lesson: every action is both a cause (of the level above) and an effect (of the level below), so every level needs end-product wording.

**p. 100 — Task Force abilities.** Five vague capabilities (handle attitudes, build rapport, interviewing skills, run interviews, win agreement objectively) serving an unclear objective (keep conflict from becoming confrontation). No after given. Lesson: without end products and a clear objective, sufficiency can't be judged.

**Exhibit 30 (p. 101) — Six vague → end-product rewrites.** See table above. Extension for item 1: once regions own planning, picture their annual plans → ask how you'll know they're right and what happens when they come back → add "system for setting annual planning objectives" and "planning review group." Lesson: end-product wording generates the missing steps; vague wording doesn't.

**pp. 102–103 — Accounts receivable.** Before: 6 steps under an implied "reduce receivables" (identify/pursue; age accounts; reminders by amount and time; pursue; director-level settlement; collection agency). Middle (after the author's age × amount grid):
```
Reduce accounts receivable
  1. Classify overdue accounts by age and size
  2. Escalate collection responsibility as seriousness rises
     (Accounting → Salesmen → Directors → Collection agency)
```
After:
```
Set up a follow-up system for overdue accounts
  1. Classify overdue accounts by age and size
  2. Escalate collection responsibility as seriousness rises
  3. [missing] e.g., tell salespeople to stop visiting chronic nonpayers
```
Lesson: "reduce receivables" is met by one paid bill; the real end product (a follow-up system) exposes a missing step.

**pp. 103–104 — Strategic alliance stakeholder questions.** Before: 5 questions (owners convinced? reputation/market effect? top managers willing? how to win over the threatened? reactions of customers, suppliers, partners, financiers, competitors?) → five assistants bring back five unrelated things. After:
```
A plan for winning stakeholder support for the alliance
  1. Identify which groups the alliance will affect
     (stockholders, top management, customers, suppliers, partners, financiers, competitors)
  2. Predict how each will react
  3. Work out how to persuade each to help the alliance succeed
```
Lesson: questions don't sequence; end-product steps do, and one is already done.

**pp. 104–105 — Telecommunications program.** Before: 10 flat steps (analyze facilities and usage; identify business tasks needing support; set objectives; provide researchers for review; examine supplier relationships; identify technology options; control internal costs; scrutinize equipment policies; examine existing links; determine organizational approach). After:
```
1. Define telecom objectives                              (3)
   - Review current facilities and how they're used        (1)
   - Find the business tasks that need (more/less) support (2)
   - Review existing communication links                   (9)
2. Form a project team to select equipment                (4)
   - Map the main technology options                       (6)
   - Review equipment policies                             (8)
   - Review supplier relationships                         (5)
3. Build a framework for organizational control           (10)
   - Appoint a central manager                             (? — not in original)
   - Install cost controls                                 (7)
```
Lesson: "before" vs "so that" yields three levels and reveals gaps.

**pp. 105–107 — Strategic-planning training proposal (Tasks/Objectives/Benefits).** Before: three columns. Tasks: train in methods; transfer know-how; moderate seminars; suggest refinements to the planning system; identify information gaps; prepare staff to use results next cycle. Objectives: transfer know-how; adapt the method to existing systems; put know-how in a handbook; foster a climate for strategic thinking; position the client to formulate strategies. Benefits: two trained core groups; cheaper and faster know-how transfer than hiring a planner; use of expertise in the next cycle. Pared to essence and numbered 1–14, repetitions show (e.g., "transfer know-how" appears in Tasks, Objectives, and Benefits). After:
```
We will quickly equip your company with strategic planning know-how     (2, 7, 13)
  1. Train two advisory groups in the methods and concepts            (1, 12)
  2. Fit the concepts to your current planning system                  (4, 5, 8, 9)
  3. Help your staff use them in the coming planning cycle             (3, 6, 10, 11, 14)
```
Lesson: horizontal category slicing creates repetition; vertical end-product actions remove it. Check: these give the client a *position* to devise good strategies, not a guarantee.

**pp. 107–108 — London equity sales.** Before: "To improve equity sales: rank customers' revenue potential by area; decide penetration per area; reassign salesmen." After:
```
To lift London equity sales, put our resources where customer potential
is highest   (How?)
  - Rank customers' revenue potential by area
  - Set target penetration per area
  - Redeploy salesmen to match
```
Lesson: "improve sales" is met by one sale; ask "in order to make what happen?"

**p. 108 — UK blue-collar training.** Before: three complex sentences (show top management that government prioritizes training; set a framework for suppliers' courses; create upward pressure from the workforce). Subjects → participants in the training system; relations (importance *to*, framework *for*, pressure *from*) → incentives. After:
```
Better UK blue-collar training requires incentives that make every party in
the training system back it   (What would we do?)
  - Top management: show them it matters
  - Suppliers: give them a framework
  - Workforce: generate demand from below
```
Lesson: isolate subjects and verbs separately, name each set's commonality, combine.

**pp. 108–109 — Product development issues.** Before: six "how to" issues. Bare: develop right products; allocate right resources; do it quickly; do it on time; market it effectively; get scientists and managers to cooperate. Subgroups (issue 3 split into 3a/3b):
```
1. Pick products that fit what the market wants          (1, 3b)
2. Develop them as fast as possible                       (3a)
   - Put the right resources on them                      (2)
   - Structure R&D around deadlines                       (4)
   - Get scientists and managers cooperating              (6)
3. Launch them as persuasively as possible                (5)
```
After (using general knowledge: first-to-market premium; shrinking life cycles):
```
The key issue: can we organize ourselves to respond to the market better
than competitors do?   (What does responding fast and well require?)
  1. Can we pick the right products for our market?
  2. Can we remove avoidable delays in reaching that market?
  3. Can we run marketing that maximizes sales?
```
Lesson: pare → subgroup → ask "if we do these, what do we get?" → frame with domain knowledge.

**pp. 111–112 — Planning and control "four characteristics."** Before: blank "four characteristics" (annual; integrated; strong top-down direction; separates current practice from planned change). Subjects identical → predicates compared → no message. After (from extensive rewriting):
```
The new system exists to make every unit concentrate on raising profits, by
  - Demanding a yearly profit plan from each unit
  - Reconciling the plans at each reporting level
  - Holding managers accountable to their plans
```
Lesson: true and elegant ≠ relevant; a missing relationship means the grouping is wrong.

**p. 113 — Information-system complaints.** Before: five complaints (productivity figures need updating; turnover figures needed; competitor data from tenders should be gathered; salary market-rate data inadequate; capital lockup figures needed). Predicates → two subgroups. After:
```
As currently set up, the system gives planners information they can't use   (Why?)
  - Some of what they need is missing entirely         (2, 3, 5)
  - The rest is available but not good enough          (1, 4)
  [order suggests a third check: exists and adequate but badly presented?]
```
Lesson: closer links → subgroups → "why these and no others?" → top point → gap check.

**p. 114 — IS Assessment findings.** Before: "some of the results indicated:" + 8 findings. Bare: need due dates; inexperienced PMs; danger of slipping dates; inconsistent tool use; never done anything this big; limited experience; afraid of slipping dates; lack tools for the work. After:
```
We see a risk that your project managers will miss their target dates   (3, 7)
  - Their experience with this type of work is thin                  (2, 6)
  - They've never delivered a system this large or complex           (5)
  - They lack skill with the needed methods, tools, and techniques   (4, 8)
```
Lesson: reduce to bare elements, regroup by closer links, and lead with the risk. (Finding 1, the need for committed due dates, isn't cited by number in the rewrite; it appears to be absorbed into the top point's concern with target dates. This is my reading.)

**pp. 115–116 — Automotive aftermarket "Our Conclusions."** Before: five mixed conclusions with "overall" hedges. Positives (1, 2, 4, 5) → attractive. Negatives (3, 4, 5) visualized: circle (market) cut into segments, some shaded differently (uncertain outlooks), a barrier line blocking entry. Derived:
```
Only certain segments are attractive
   → Those segments are hard to enter
      → Therefore … ?   (walk away? acquire your way in? commission an entry strategy?)
```
Lesson: visualize to make the leap; non-similar conclusions are a deductive chain that must be finished.

**p. 116 — Resource allocation "four variables."** Before: blank "four variables to be managed" (sequence and timing; people's tasks; information needs; decision process). After (actions in disguise):
```
Resource allocation's central management job is to involve the right people
early and substantially   (How?)
  - Set out the order and timing of project-planning work   (1)
  - Pinpoint where decisions are required                   (2)
  - Name who will help make them                            (4)
  - Specify what information they will need                 (3)
```
Lesson: if "variables" can be stated as steps toward one effect, switch to action form; note the reordering into process sequence.

**pp. 116–117 — Sales proposals.** Before: "can show a new image through improvements in these areas" + seven mixed items. After (time order of the proposal activity):
```
Our proposals don't work as sales tools
  1. Their message isn't compelling           (1, 4, 7)
  2. They don't look outstanding              (2)
  3. They take too long to produce            (3, 5, 6)
```
Acceptable looser variant when reasoning is sound: proposals can project a new image if the message is stronger, the look is striking, and delivery is quick. Lesson: situation ideas can take time order; precision can relax when the logic is valid.

## Nuances and exceptions

- **Deductive groupings are easy to summarize**; the hard work is with inductive ones (p. 94).
- **Relaxed precision is allowed.** Minto concedes you won't apply the discipline rigidly everywhere; readers supply a gestalt, so a less precise summary is acceptable when you *know* the reasoning is valid (p. 117). The relaxation is about wording precision, not about permitting blank assertions or invalid groupings.
- **Not every effect is numeric**, but every step needs some tangible completion test (p. 99).
- **"In a position to" vs "will."** A valid action summary may only claim the capability the steps produce, not the ultimate outcome (p. 107).
- **No foolproof technique** exists for finding the action summary. MECE plus direct-effect wording are necessary, not sufficient; Minto teaches by example (p. 107).
- **Don't overdo level-distinguishing.** Separating levels is good; turning levels into category labels (Tasks/Objectives/Benefits) is not (p. 105).
- **Situation vs action can be misread.** "Variables," "issues," "characteristics" may be actions in disguise; treat them as a class first, then switch if an effect appears (p. 116). Conversely, situation ideas can legitimately be in time order (p. 116).
- **Question-form summaries.** Minto warns against stating action *steps* as questions (p. 103), yet her product-development rewrite (p. 109) frames an *issue* as "whether we can…" with three yes/no sub-questions.
  > **Skill note:** The book doesn't reconcile these explicitly. A reasonable reading: questions are fine when the document's point *is* an issue to be resolved and each sub-question is a crisp, end-product-testable condition; they're not a substitute for steps in a plan.
- **Interdependence of actions and effect.** You can't judge completeness without the effect, and the effect depends on the actions chosen. Expect to iterate (p. 99).
- **Restructuring changes membership.** Expect items to split (3a/3b), merge (repetitions), disappear, or new ones to appear (a central manager; a follow-up step; two more delegation areas).
- **True but irrelevant.** A set of true statements can still fail to support any message (p. 112).
- **Polished frameworks soothe.** Elegant, branded-sounding lists are where critical reading most often switches off (p. 111).

> **Skill note:** For a review skill, the Exhibit 30 table and the "smallest outcome that technically satisfies the summary" test (SUM-15) are the most mechanical, high-yield checks on action summaries. For situation summaries, the subject/predicate/judgment table (SUM-20) is the most mechanical.
