# Abduction: Problem Solving in Structureless Situations

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Appendix A "Problem Solving in Structureless Situations", book pp. 210–215. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `ABD`.

## When a skill needs this
- Deciding whether a problem is *analytical* (the structure is known and its output is unwelcome) or *scientific/creative* (the output can't be explained because the structure is missing, hidden or contradicted).
- Generating hypotheses when no ready-made diagnostic framework applies: invention, root-causing a mysterious failure, a theory that doesn't fit the data.
- Designing tests or experiments that return a clean keep-or-drop verdict on a hypothesis.
- Explaining or checking which reasoning mode (deduction, induction, abduction) is in play at a given moment of an analysis.
- Coaching a hypothesis-driven investigation so that every step yields a concrete, picturable output that later anchors the write-up.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Structureless situation | A problem whose outcome can't be explained, because the producing structure is absent, hidden or contradicted. |
| Abduction | Peirce's 1890 label for problem-solving reasoning, picked to show it is a sibling of deduction and induction. |
| Rule | What you believe about how things are structured (if A then B). |
| Case | A fact actually observed (A). |
| Result | What you'd expect to see when the Rule operates on the Case (B). |
| Deduction | Rule → Case → Result; the conclusion is necessary. |
| Induction | Case → Result → Rule; the conclusion is a probable rule. |
| Abduction (form) | Result → Rule → Case; the conclusion is a possible case that must be checked. |
| Analytical abduction | Structure (Rule) known: from an unwelcome Result, propose and verify the Case. This is the Ch 8–9 method. |
| Scientific abduction | Structure unknown: a Rule (hypothesis) must be invented before any Case can be checked. |
| Hypothesis | A candidate structure that would account for the observed outcome. |
| Experiment | A test built on what a hypothesis necessarily implies, designed to give a yes/no. |
| Sub-hypotheses / sequential hypotheses | Narrower follow-up hypotheses used when the method is rerun on what remains open. |
| Visual thinking | Picturing structures and their consequences; Minto says hypothesis generation and experiment design both rely on it. |

## Concepts

### Introduction: a second kind of problem (p. 210)
- **Recap of Ch 8.** There, problem solving meant a rigorously logical hunt for the hidden structures that generate unwanted events, followed by laying those structures out for view. The working assumption is that the fix always involves adjusting that structure. The assumption is sound whenever the complaint is that the structure's *output is unwelcome*.
- **The other kind of problem.** Sometimes the outcome isn't unwelcome so much as *inexplicable*. Minto gives three reasons:
  1. **No structure exists yet.** You are inventing (her examples: the telephone; tunnelling under water).
  2. **The structure can't be seen.** You have only its outputs to study (e.g., the brain, DNA).
  3. **The structure doesn't account for the outcome.** The accepted model is contradicted by events. Examples: Aristotle's notion of force couldn't explain why a fired cannonball keeps going; tools that rust no matter how they are protected.
- **Relevance.** Such cases can crop up inside an everyday analytical assignment. They call for more visual thinking than Ch 8–9 work, but Minto stresses that **the reasoning involved is much the same**.
- **Naming.** The tool is a second variety of **Abduction**, the name Charles Sanders Peirce gave in 1890 to problem-solving reasoning. Peirce wanted the name to signal that such thinking belongs in the same family as deduction and induction.
- Minto sets out to separate the two forms of abduction (analytical and scientific) and to show how the second is used.

### Analytical Abduction (p. 211)
- **Peirce's observation:** all reasoning juggles three separate things:
  1. a **Rule**, your belief about how some part of the world is put together;
  2. a **Case**, a fact you have actually observed;
  3. a **Result**, the outcome you'd predict when that Rule operates on that Case.
- **The type of reasoning you are doing is set by your starting element and by which extra fact you hold.** Minto illustrates with pricing and sales (example sentences paraphrased):

| Mode | Order | Example | Logical form | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deduction | Rule → Case → Result | Overpricing cuts sales. We've overpriced. Sales will drop. | If A then B; A; therefore B | **Necessarily** B |
| Induction | Case → Result → Rule | Price went up. Sales went down. Overpricing is the likely reason. | A; B; so if A then probably B | **Probably** |
| Abduction | Result → Rule → Case | Sales are down. Overpricing commonly causes that. Check whether we're overpriced. | B; if A then B; so possibly A, to be checked | **Possibly** A |

- **Analytical problem solving is abduction.** The book's method all along has been:
  1. spot the unwelcome outcome (R1, the Result);
  2. use what you know of the situation's structure to name a likely cause (the Rule);
  3. verify whether that cause is really present (the Case).
- That is the abductive sequence, step for step.

### Relationship among the three forms (p. 212)
- Abduction is not the same as induction or deduction, and the distinction is worth keeping, but the three are tightly connected.
- In a complex problem you will typically **cycle through all three**. The one you are using at any moment, and the kind of conclusion it can deliver, is fixed by where you began.
- **Exhibit A-1** (its caption: starting point decides the type of thinking): three loops, each running 1 → 2 → 3 and back.
  - Deduction: 1 Rule → 2 Case → 3 Result.
  - Induction: 1 Case → 2 Result → 3 Rule.
  - Abduction: 1 Result → 2 Rule → 3 Case.

### Scientific Abduction (p. 212)
- **What separates it from Ch 8's analytical problem solving.** The analyst already has the structure that produces the outcome; the scientist lacks it.
  - The analyst holds two of the three elements (Result and Rule) and reasons to the missing Case.
  - The scientist holds only the Result, and has to **invent the Rule (the structure)** before any reasoning toward a Case is possible.
- **The classical scientific method gets the scientist from there to the Case:**
  1. Propose a structure that might account for the outcome.
  2. Design a test whose result will either support or eliminate that proposal.
  3. Run the test so it returns an unambiguous yes or no.
  4. Repeat, using narrower or follow-on hypotheses to pin down whatever possibilities are still open.
- **Its two defining activities** are hypothesis generation and experiment design, and each leans heavily on visual thinking.

### Generating Hypotheses (pp. 212–213)
- **Hypotheses don't come from nowhere.** Inspecting the structural components of the problem-generating situation is what prompts them.
  - Example: if the goal is long-distance conversation without raised voices, your thinking naturally turns to changing the speaker's voice or boosting the listener's hearing. The hypotheses mirror whatever possibilities you can picture.
- **No recipe.** Minto admits there is no step-by-step method for picturing fruitful possibilities. Often it takes something like genius: noticing a parallel between the problem at hand and something else you know about the world.
- **Bell and the telephone.** The book quotes Alexander Graham Bell's account. Bell was struck that a thin, fragile membrane in the ear manages to move comparatively heavy bones. That led him to think a tougher membrane could move a piece of steel.
- **The limits of what is known.** Why one person hits on a fitting analogy and another doesn't remains a mystery. Two things help:
  - knowing the problem situation completely;
  - writing down every assumption you hold about it and questioning each one.
- **The insight is visual.** Accounts by people who have described their own breakthroughs agree that the insight appears as a picture.

### Devising Experiments (pp. 213–214)
- With a hypothesis in hand, let it suggest tests that would confirm or refute it. This is visual work too: picture what would have to happen automatically if the structure were real, then build a test that shows decisively whether it does happen.
- **In abductive terms:**

| Step | Content |
|---|---|
| Result | An unexpected fact, A, is observed. |
| Rule | Perhaps A holds because B holds. |
| Case | If B holds, consequence C must follow automatically. |
| (Test) | Look for C. |

- **Galileo and the cannonball (pp. 213–214):**
  - **Result.**
    - On Aristotle's account, force creates velocity, so motion should cease once force does.
    - A cannonball, though, keeps flying after the explosion's push is over.
    - So Aristotle's model of force and motion can't be right.
  - **Rule.**
    - A simple way to study force and motion is to let a ball drop.
    - Three structural elements are present: how heavy the ball is, how far it drops, and how long the drop takes.
    - One hypothesis per element follows: force is proportional to (1) weight, (2) distance travelled under the force, or (3) duration of the force.
  - **Case.**
    - Hypothesis 3 implies that distance grows with the square of elapsed time (1, 4, 9 … distance units after 1, 2, 3 … time units).
    - A ball rolled down a slope falls slowly enough for distance at each time interval to be measured against that prediction.
  - **New Rule.**
    - The measurements match.
    - Hence force is what *changes* velocity.
- **The trick in designing an experiment: the outcome must be an unambiguous yes or no.**
  - Merely tweaking conditions to "see what happens" doesn't qualify.
  - The outcome has to settle, beyond doubt, whether the hypothesis survives or is dropped.
- **Evidence for the rule.** Minto notes that the fields enforcing this demand most strictly made the biggest gains in knowledge over the preceding eight decades (relative to her writing).
- **Darwin.** She invokes Darwin's view that observations are of use only if they count for or against some hypothesis.

### The common pattern (pp. 214–215)
- Minto lines the two forms up side by side in **Exhibit A-2** (its caption: analytic and scientific problem solving share one pattern). Cells paraphrased:

| Basic process (Sequential Analysis step) | Analytical problem solving | Scientific problem solving |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem? | Picture the gap between the current outcome and the wanted one. | Pin down how the observed outcome departs from what accepted theory predicts. |
| 2. Where? | Picture which parts of the current structure might be producing the outcome. | List the theory's standing assumptions that could explain the mismatch. |
| 3. Why? | Test each part: is it responsible, and if so how? | Propose rival structures that would remove the mismatch and account for the outcome. |
| 4. Could do? | Lay out the logically possible ways to alter the structure so it yields the wanted outcome. | Design tests that knock out one or more of the rival structures. |
| 5. Should do? | Build a revised structure containing the changes that best deliver the outcome. | Rewrite the theory in light of the test results. |

- **Why the pattern is valuable:**
  - It can produce fast breakthroughs in understanding and solving problems.
  - It drives the reasoning ahead with rigor and economy, avoiding detours and irrelevant side-issues.
  - Every step must yield a concrete, visible output, and that picture shows where the following analysis should head.
  - After the solution is found, the same pictures steer how you present it and the words you choose.

### Closing (p. 215)
- Minto borrows Herbert Simon's idea that to solve a problem is to find a representation of it in which the answer is obvious.
- She describes her aim as showing how such representations can be built and used as efficiently as possible.
- Her parting thought: most of us could think much more creatively and efficiently than we do, and understanding the process better might prompt us to make the attempt.

## Rules
- **ABD-1**: First classify the problem. Is the outcome unwelcome (structure known → analytical abduction, Ch 8–9) or unexplained (structure non-existent, hidden or contradicted → scientific abduction)? *Why:* the two need different second steps: apply a known structure, or invent one. *Check:* can you name the structure producing the outcome? If not, which of the three reasons applies? (pp. 210, 212)
- **ABD-2**: Identify your reasoning mode from your starting point. Rule-first is deduction (necessary conclusion); Case-first is induction (probable rule); Result-first is abduction (possible case to check). *Why:* the mode fixes how much confidence the conclusion deserves. *Check:* label each inference D/I/A and confirm the claimed certainty matches ("necessarily / probably / possibly"). (pp. 211–212)
- **ABD-3**: In analytical problem solving, go from the unwelcome outcome (R1), through your knowledge of the structure (Rule), to a specific candidate cause (Case), and then verify it. *Why:* this abductive sequence is what the book's method rests on. *Check:* each proposed cause cites the structural rule linking it to R1 and has a planned check. (p. 211)
- **ABD-4**: Treat any abductive conclusion as only *possibly* true until verified. *Why:* abduction yields a candidate, not a proof. *Check:* no cause is presented as established without a test result. (p. 211)
- **ABD-5**: When the structure is unknown, propose candidate structures *before* reasoning toward cases. *Why:* the missing Rule must be invented before anything can be tested. *Check:* the plan contains explicit hypotheses stated as structures that would explain the outcome. (p. 212)
- **ABD-6**: Generate hypotheses from the components of the problem situation: list them, then form one hypothesis per component or relation (as with weight / distance / time), and use analogy where you can. *Why:* structure-prompted hypotheses are productive; hypotheses from nowhere are not. *Check:* each hypothesis traces to a named component of the situation. (pp. 212–214)
- **ABD-7**: Support hypothesis generation by getting complete knowledge of the situation and by writing down and challenging every assumption about it. *Why:* these are the only aids Minto identifies for finding apt analogies. *Check:* a written assumptions list exists, and each item has been questioned. (p. 213)
- **ABD-8**: For each hypothesis, derive a consequence that would have to follow automatically, and test that consequence. *Why:* a hypothesis is tested through what it implies. *Check:* each experiment states "if H, then C" and measures C. (p. 213)
- **ABD-9**: Design every experiment or test to deliver a clear yes/no that lets you keep or drop the hypothesis; never just vary conditions to "see what happens". *Why:* observations help only if they bear for or against a view, and the strictest sciences advanced fastest. *Check:* before running it, write down which outcome keeps and which drops the hypothesis. (p. 214)
- **ABD-10**: After each test, iterate: form narrower or follow-on hypotheses for the remaining possibilities, and repeat. *Why:* one experiment rarely settles everything. *Check:* the investigation log shows hypothesis → test → verdict → next hypothesis. (p. 212)
- **ABD-11**: Make each step of the five-step pattern produce a concrete, picturable output (a picture of the gap, of the structure, of the alternatives). Use those pictures to steer the next step and later to anchor the write-up. *Why:* visible outputs keep the reasoning moving in the fewest steps and later guide the presentation and wording. *Check:* every step has a tangible artifact (diagram, table, structure). (p. 214)
- **ABD-12**: Expect to cycle among deduction, induction and abduction in complex problems, switching deliberately according to what you know. *Why:* your starting element sets the reasoning mode. *Check:* changes of mode in the reasoning chain are explicit, not accidental. (p. 212)
- **ABD-13**: Aim for a representation of the problem in which the answer is obvious. *Why:* Minto adopts Simon's view of what solving a problem amounts to. *Check:* does the final diagram or structure make the answer evident to a reader? (p. 215)

## Procedures

### P1. Classify the problem and pick the abduction form
1. State the outcome you observe.
2. Ask: is the outcome unwelcome, and do we know the structure producing it?
   - **Yes** → analytical abduction. Use the problem-definition and diagnostic-framework methods (`07-defining-the-problem.md`, `08-structuring-the-analysis.md`), following P2.
   - **No** → scientific abduction (P3). Note which reason applies:
     - (a) no structure exists yet (invention);
     - (b) the structure is hidden (only outputs observable);
     - (c) the accepted structure is contradicted by the outcome.

### P2. Analytical abduction (structure known)
1. **Result:** state the unwelcome outcome R1.
2. **Rule:** from your knowledge of the structure, write down the rules that could produce R1 (e.g., overpricing cuts sales). In practice, draw the diagnostic framework.
3. **Case:** for each rule, frame the check as a yes/no question (e.g., are we overpriced?).
4. Run the checks. Confirmed cases are causes; excluded ones are dropped.
5. Move to steps 4–5 of the basic process: alternative changes to the structure, then choose the revised structure that best yields R2.

### P3. Scientific abduction (structure unknown)
1. **State the mismatch** between the observed outcome and what the prevailing theory or assumption predicts.
2. **List the standing assumptions** that could be responsible for that mismatch.
3. **Generate hypotheses.**
   - Enumerate the situation's structural components (e.g., weight, distance, time).
   - Form a hypothesis about the missing structure for each component or relation.
   - Look for analogies with structures known elsewhere (e.g., membrane moving bones → membrane moving steel).
   - Get to know the situation completely, and question every assumption.
   - Sketch each hypothesis as a picture.
4. **Derive consequences.** For each hypothesis, state what would automatically follow if it were true (e.g., distance ∝ time²).
5. **Design the experiment.** Find a feasible setup that makes the consequence measurable (e.g., a slope that slows the fall). Decide in advance which outcome keeps and which drops the hypothesis. *Decision:* if the design can only "show what happens", redesign it.
6. **Run it** and record the yes/no verdict.
7. **Iterate.**
   - *Decision:* if the hypothesis survives, state the new Rule and form narrower hypotheses to refine it.
   - *Decision:* if it is dropped, move to the next hypothesis in sequence.
   - Continue until the open possibilities are resolved.
8. **Rewrite the theory or structure** in light of the results. Then proceed as in the analytical case to change the structure.

### P4. Audit the reasoning in an analysis or draft
1. For each inferential step, mark which elements were given (Rule, Case, Result) and which was concluded.
2. Label it deduction, induction or abduction, and check that the language matches the certainty: "necessarily", "probably" or "possibly / to be checked".
3. Flag abductive conclusions presented as facts without a check (ABD-4).
4. Flag tests that can't produce a keep/drop verdict (ABD-9).

## Diagnostics
| Symptom | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| A cause is reported as fact because it's the usual reason ("sales fell, so we must be overpriced") | Abductive "possibly" treated as proven | ABD-4, ABD-3 |
| Team keeps applying a standard diagnostic framework that never explains the anomaly | Structureless situation not recognized; the structure is contradicted by the outcome | ABD-1, ABD-5 |
| Hypotheses are brainstormed with no link to the situation | Not generated from structural components | ABD-6, ABD-7 |
| Experiment plan reads "try changing X and see what happens" | No predefined keep/drop outcome | ABD-9, ABD-8 |
| One test is run, then the investigation stalls or overreaches | No iteration with narrower or follow-on hypotheses | ABD-10 |
| Induction ("we raised price, sales fell, so price is the cause") presented with deductive certainty | Mode and certainty mismatched | ABD-2, ABD-12 |
| Findings are prose-only; nobody can picture the gap, structure or alternatives | Steps lack visual outputs | ABD-11, ABD-13 |
| Analysis wanders through irrelevant detail | Five-step pattern not followed in minimum sequence | ABD-11 |

## Templates and patterns

**Three reasoning forms (Exhibit A-1 and p. 211)**
```
DEDUCTION   1 Rule:   If A then B          2 Case:   A                 3 Result: necessarily B
INDUCTION   1 Case:   A                    2 Result: B                 3 Rule:   if A then probably B
ABDUCTION   1 Result: B                    2 Rule:   if A then B       3 Case:   possibly A → verify
```

**Abductive experiment frame (p. 213)**
```
Result: Unexpected observation <A>.
Rule:   Candidate explanation: <A> because <B>.             (hypothesis about structure)
Case:   If <B>, then <C> must follow automatically.
Test:   Look for <C>. Keep <B> if found; drop it if not.
```

**Hypothesis-from-structure frame**
```
Situation's structural components: <e1>, <e2>, <e3>
H1: <effect> depends on/is proportional to <e1>
H2: <effect> depends on/is proportional to <e2>
H3: <effect> depends on/is proportional to <e3>
For each Hn → necessary consequence Cn → feasible experiment Xn → keep/drop criterion
```

**Five-step pattern (Exhibit A-2, paraphrased)**
```
Step            Analytical                                     Scientific
1 Problem?      Picture gap: current vs wanted outcome          Mismatch: observed vs theory's prediction
2 Where?        Picture structural parts that might cause it    Standing assumptions that might cause it
3 Why?          Test each part: responsible? how?               Propose rival structures
4 Could do?     Logical alternative changes to the structure    Tests that knock out rivals
5 Should do?    Revised structure that best yields the outcome  Rewrite theory from results
```

## Worked examples
**Price and sales (p. 211): one domain, three reasoning modes.**
- Deduction: rule plus case gives a certain result.
- Induction: case plus result gives a probable rule.
- Abduction: result plus known rule gives a case to verify.
- Lesson: the same facts support different inferences depending on your starting point; analytical problem solving is the abductive one.

**Long-distance communication (pp. 212–213): inventing the telephone.**
- Before: the goal is conversation over distance without shouting, and no structure exists.
- After: the structural components (speaker's voice, listener's ear) suggest two hypothesis families: alter the voice, or strengthen the hearing.
- Bell's analogy: a fragile ear membrane moves heavy bones, so a sturdier membrane might move steel.
- Lesson: hypotheses come from the situation's structure plus analogy, and the insight arrives as a picture.

**Galileo's cannonball (pp. 213–214): structure contradicted by the outcome.**
- Skeleton:
  ```
  Result:   Aristotle's force→velocity model says the ball should stop when the push ends; it doesn't.
  Rule:     Falling ball has 3 structural components → H1 force ∝ weight; H2 force ∝ distance; H3 force ∝ time
  Case:     If H3, distance ∝ time² (1, 4, 9 …) → roll ball down a slope and measure
  New Rule: Measurements match → force changes velocity
  ```
- Lesson: one hypothesis per structural component, a necessary consequence, an experiment built for a clean yes/no, then a revised rule.

**Rusting tools (p. 210).**
- Mentioned only as an everyday instance of a structure failing to explain an outcome. No worked solution is given.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **Similarity of the two forms.** Minto stresses it: scientific abduction needs more visual thinking, but the reasoning is essentially that of the analytical method (p. 210).
- **Use all three forms.** Abduction is distinct from deduction and induction, yet in practice all three alternate (p. 212). Don't treat them as rival methods.
- **No recipe for hypotheses.** Producing fruitful hypotheses can't be reduced to a procedure; it often takes something like a gift for analogy. Minto can only point to aids: complete knowledge of the situation and challenged assumptions (p. 213).
- **Yes/no is mandatory.** The yes/no requirement is the same discipline as Ch 9's yes/no diagnostic questions, which Minto calls the analyst's equivalent of crucial experiments. See `08-structuring-the-analysis.md`.
- **Attributions.**
  - The Abduction term and the Rule/Case/Result triad are credited to C. S. Peirce (1890).
  - The closing representation idea is credited to Herbert Simon.
  - The observation-must-serve-a-view idea is credited to Charles Darwin.
  - The Bell passage is quoted in the book; it is paraphrased here.
- **Where the scientific procedures sit.** The four-step scientific method is listed twice: in Ch 9 (p. 142), as what better consulting firms emulate, and here (p. 212), as the procedure for scientific abduction. The Ch 9 version ends with "plan remedial action"; this version ends with "recycle with sub-hypotheses".

> **Skill note:** For root-cause or debugging assistants, ABD-1 is the practical switch. If a trusted model of the system exists, use the diagnostic-framework workflow. If the model itself is in doubt (the outcome contradicts it), move to hypothesis → consequence → yes/no experiment loops. This mapping to software or ops debugging is an interpretation, not Minto's.
