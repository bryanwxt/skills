# Deduction and Induction: The Difference

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 5 "Deduction and Induction: The Difference", book pp. 60–72. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `DED`.

## When a skill needs this

- Deciding how to lay out the Key Line of a recommendation document — reasons/actions in parallel (inductive) or a premise-to-"therefore" chain (deductive).
- Reviewing a report that walks the reader through "what's wrong → why → what to do" before saying what to do.
- Testing whether a grouping of points is logically related at all, and whether its summary claims more than the points support.
- Spotting "news" — true but unrelated facts listed together — and cutting it.
- Restructuring a findings / conclusions / recommendations analysis into a reader-first pyramid.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Deductive reasoning | A line of reasoning in which each point grows out of the one before, ending in a "therefore". |
| Inductive reasoning | Treating several things as the same kind and drawing an inference from that sameness. |
| Statement / comment / therefore | Minto's plain-language replacement for major premise / minor premise / conclusion. |
| Comment on the statement | A second point that says something more about the first point's subject or predicate. |
| Chained deductive argument | Two or more deductive arguments linked, with obvious steps left out. |
| Resting on the final point | The parent of a deductive grouping summarizes the chain with its weight on the conclusion ("Because..., ..."). |
| Plural noun | The single "kind-of" word naming every idea in an inductive grouping (reasons, steps, schemes...). |
| Misfit | An idea in an inductive grouping that the plural noun doesn't cover. |
| Recommendation Worksheet | Three-column layout of an analysis: Findings (what's wrong) / Conclusions (what's causing it) / Recommendations (what to do). |
| Abduction | Forming a hypothesis, then seeking evidence for it; becomes induction once the evidence is in. |
| Deduction masquerading as induction | A causal chain (A led to B led to C) presented as if its links were parallel items. |
| News (vs. thinking) | True statements that share no subject or predicate and so support no inference. |
| Overstructured argument | (From Ch. 3) a deductive Key Line with a step that raises no question. |

## Concepts

### Chapter opening: two forms, the only two (pp. 60–61)

- Recap of the premise: clarity comes from spelling out precisely how a set of ideas on one subject relate. Organized properly, they always form a pyramid with distinct levels of abstraction under one governing thought.
- Ideas in that pyramid connect in three directions: upward (an idea sums up the grouping beneath it), downward (the grouping explains or supports the idea above), and sideways (members of a grouping proceed in a logical sequence). What counts as a logical sequence depends on whether the grouping was built deductively or inductively.
- Those two forms of reasoning are the only ways ideas can be logically linked, so knowing how they differ and what rules govern each is a prerequisite for sorting out your thinking and writing it clearly.
- The contrast in a nutshell (Exhibit 16):
  - **Deduction** advances step by step toward a "therefore". The idea above it condenses that whole chain, with most of its weight on the conclusion.
  - **Induction** declares a set of facts or ideas to be instances of one kind of thing and then states an inference about what they share.
  - In deduction each point grows out of the previous one; in induction the points stand side by side, independent of each other.
- Minto calls the gap between the two enormous, but says that once it is understood, recognizing either form, untangling it, and picking the right one becomes easy.

### Deductive Reasoning (p. 61)

- Most everyday thinking seems to run deductively — perhaps because deduction is easier to build than induction. It is the usual shape of problem solving, and so it is the shape people reach for when they explain their thinking.
- Minto's verdict: a fine way to think, a heavy-handed way to write.

### Deductive Reasoning — How It Works (pp. 62–63)

- Textbooks frame deduction as a syllogism, with a conclusion drawn from a major and a minor premise. Minto considers that vocabulary unhelpful for explaining deduction in writing and stops using it.
- Her substitute: a deductive argument performs three moves.
  1. Assert something about a state of affairs that exists.
  2. Assert something about a related state of affairs existing at the same moment. This second assertion is connected to the first if it **says something about the first one's subject or about its predicate**.
  3. Spell out what follows from both being true together.
- **Exhibit 17** sets out five arguments that each make exactly these three moves (see Worked examples). In every case the parent above should loosely condense the group with its weight on the conclusion — framed as "Because X, Y", "Since X, Y", or "If you want X, you must Y".
- **Chaining rule.** You may omit steps and link several deductive arguments into one chain — useful because writing out every link is slow and sounds fussy — on one condition: **readers must be able to supply the missing links and agree with them.** (Exhibit 17's five arguments, by contrast, show every step.)
- **Exhibit 18** is such a chain (see Worked examples): three boxes stand in for what, fully written out, would be six steps — two complete syllogisms. Writing all six would be wearisome, and that is Minto's chief objection to deduction in prose: it drags, mainly because it turns a simple point into a puzzle with the answer held back until the end.

### Deductive Reasoning — When to Use It (pp. 64–68)

- Because deduction is slow, Minto's advice is to **steer away from it at the Key Line and aim always for an inductive presentation there** — it asks less of the reader.
- **What a deductive report demands of its reader** (her "You must change" illustration):
  - Deductive layout: You must change → (Why?) what is going wrong (A1, B1, C1) → what is causing it (A2, B2, C2) → so here is what to do (A3, B3, C3).
  - The reader's workload: hold the three problems; match each to its cause while keeping the other matches in mind; then carry each problem-and-cause pair over to its remedy.
  - The cost: the action they actually need (what to do on Monday) comes last, and getting there means re-running the writer's entire analysis — as though the writer wanted credit for the effort.
- **The inductive alternative:** You must change → (How?) A3, B3, C3 → beneath each, its own Why? (A1/A2, B1/B2, C1/C2). The order of the two questions is simply flipped — "How?" first, "Why?" second. Deductive arguments may still sit at lower levels, but the reader's main question has been answered at once, each subject area is fenced off from the others, and everything about a subject is in one place.
- **"Surely deduction is more rigorous?"** — the standard objection. Minto's reply: no. The reasoning is identical; the only thing at issue is how it is arranged on the page.
- **Recommendation Worksheet (Exhibit 19, captioned as showing that problem analysis is always deductive).** By the end of problem solving, the ideas can be sorted into three columns — Findings (what is going wrong), Conclusions (what is causing it), Recommendations (what to do about it) — with one row per issue (A, B, C).
  - "Findings" and "conclusions", though common labels, are somewhat misleading: they differ only by an arbitrary tag for level of abstraction. Summarize a cluster of findings and you have, by definition, a conclusion. So one set of findings-plus-conclusions underpins what is going wrong, and another underpins what is causing it.
  - Reaching those clusters will have involved three modes of reasoning: induction, deduction and abduction (hypothesizing first, then hunting for supporting evidence — see Appendix A). Once the evidence is gathered, abduction turns into induction.
  - The reasoning on the worksheet is finished; what remains is purely a presentation choice. **Deductive presentation:** walk through the worksheet a column at a time. **Inductive presentation:** turn the worksheet 90° to the left — the recommendations become the Key Line, each with its own findings/conclusions beneath it.
- **Action before argument.** The real choice is between explaining *why* change is needed and then *how*, or stating *that* change is needed and then justifying each change. Rule of thumb: lead with the action, because the action is what the reader cares about — except in the uncommon cases where the reasoning is what they really care about.
- **When the reasoning outweighs the action:** when the top point clashes with what the reader expects the writer to say.
  - Situation 1: the reader asks how to cut costs; the writer replies that it's easy; the reader asks how; the writer lists actions A, B, C → an ordinary inductive pyramid.
  - Situation 2: the reader asks how to cut costs; the writer replies that cost-cutting is the wrong goal and selling the business should be considered; the reader responds with alarm and a string of challenges → a deductive Key Line is clearly needed (see Worked examples).
- **The one other case where a deductive Key Line is automatically right:** when readers can't make sense of the actions without first being given the reasoning — Minto's example is the David Hertz article on conducting risk analysis (discussed in Ch. 4, p. 38), where readers needed the logic of the analytical approach before its steps would mean anything.
- Few business readers fit either case, so the Key Line should generally be inductive.
- **Scope: the Key Line, not lower levels.** What makes deduction hard is distance. Adjacent steps (a three-step syllogism read in one breath) are absorbed instantly; steps separated by ten or twelve pages lose that clarity. Hence place deduction as low as possible so that little material intervenes. Inside a paragraph it reads well and flows; at higher levels induction is always the easier read.
- **Permissible chained forms (Exhibit 20).** Deduction at lower levels need not follow only the basic three-step shape. Minto shows four variants (see Templates and Worked examples): statement → comment → therefore; statement → comment → comment → therefore; statement → comment → therefore → therefore; statement → therefore → therefore.
- **Just two limits on chaining:** (a) a deductive argument may have at most four points; (b) at most two "therefore" points may be chained. Both can be broken (Minto notes French philosophers do so routinely), but the resulting groupings become too heavy to summarize well. To keep proper summaries possible, cap deductive groupings at four points.

### Inductive Reasoning (p. 68)

- Induction is far harder to do well than deduction because it is more creative: the mind spots that a number of different items (ideas, events, facts) resemble one another in some respect, collects them, and remarks on what that resemblance signifies.
- The tanks example shows that the definition determines the inference: classify the movements as hostile moves against the border country and you infer an imminent invasion of it; classify the same movements as that country's allies preparing to strike elsewhere in Europe and an entirely different inference follows.
- Two core skills for creative inductive thinking:
  1. defining what the ideas in the grouping are;
  2. spotting which of them don't belong (misfits).
- Ch. 6 treats both in depth; here only enough is needed to tell induction apart from deduction.

### Inductive Reasoning — How It Works (pp. 69–71)

- **The plural-noun technique.** Label the grouping with one word naming the kind of idea it contains. Why a noun: a "kind of" thing is always a noun. Why plural: the grouping always holds more than one instance. ("Hostile moves" and "preparations to attack" both qualify.)
- **Exhibit 21:** three inductive groupings whose members are cleanly covered by a plural noun — schemes, steps, and ways of being harmed — with no misfits in any (see Worked examples).
- **Always verify the reasoning next — by questioning upward from the children.** Given these items, could you legitimately reach the parent? For the first grouping in Exhibit 21 (a list of one scholar's outlandish projects, used to argue the scholar was clever but impractical), yes — at least by the standards of the era when the judgment was written.
- **Exhibit 22, captioned to show that an inference must stay within its grouping:**
  - Five examples of poor management behaviour (avoiding reality, rejecting internal criticism, keeping losing activities alive, neglecting detail, not questioning policy) — can you conclude managers mismanage *on purpose*? Clearly not; that's careless reasoning.
  - Three indicators (low productivity, high overtime, uncompetitive prices on simple jobs) — can you conclude there is a profit-improvement opportunity? Possibly, but three or four other conditions could just as well be called indicators of one. So the parent sits too high in abstraction relative to its children: it does not speak to these three **specifically and only**.
  - In fact that second grouping is a **deductive argument dressed up as an inductive one** (Minto links it back to Ch. 3's printing-company case): poor productivity caused overtime, and overtime caused uncompetitive pricing. Any time a claim rests on a single piece of evidence, the reasoning has to be handled deductively. The parent it actually implies is closer to "Prices are high because productivity is low."

### How It Differs (pp. 71–72)

- **Distinguishing test (DED-15).** Look at point two. If it comments on point one's subject or predicate → deductive. If not → it should share point one's plural noun, which confirms a sound inductive grouping. Minto calls the forms very different and easy to tell apart once this test is known.
- **Two textbook "deductive fallacies"** share one shape: *all A have property P; some B have property P; therefore some B are A* (one of Minto's two instances is political, the other about animals). Invented instance: all cats have whiskers; some dogs have whiskers; therefore some dogs are cats. In each, point two doesn't comment on point one, so the points can't be deductively linked. What point two actually does is add another member to the class that point one set up — and assigning items to a class is naming them with a plural noun, which is induction. That is why the "therefore" is illegitimate.
- **Self-test, in the book's shape.** Start from a statement that one group of business people is stepping up its push into a particular market. One candidate follow-on says a rival group's imminent arrival will push them harder still — that comments on the first statement, so it is deductive. The other says a second group of business people is stepping up its push into the same market — another member of the same class, so it is inductive. Invented equivalent: *Acme is cutting its prices in the UK.* → "Those cuts will squeeze Beta's UK margins" (deductive) vs. "Beta is cutting its prices in the UK" (inductive).
- **How inductive sets are formed:** they typically fix one half of the sentence and vary the other — keep the subject and change the predicate, or keep the predicate and change the subject.
  - Book's shape, varying the subject: three different nations' business people all stepping up their push into the same market → inference: smart money is flowing into that market. Invented: Acme, Beta and Gamma are all cutting UK prices → a UK price war has begun.
  - Book's shape, varying the predicate: one nation's business people stepping up their push into three markets in one region → inference: an aggressive regional push. Invented: Acme is cutting prices in the UK, France and Germany → Acme is fighting for European share.
- **Counter-example — news, not thinking.** The book's shape: one group stepping up its push into three markets that have nothing in common beyond that group's arrival. Nothing links the varied element, so no broader insight can be drawn; listing the facts merely relays news, and news doesn't belong in a document meant to convey thinking. Invented: *Acme opened a Lisbon office; Acme changed its logo; Acme's CFO spoke at a fintech conference* — the predicates share nothing, so no inference follows.
- Why the distinction matters: writers are tempted to include such points because they are true. But (Ch. 1) a point earns its place only by helping, alongside others, to explain or support a higher point — and a higher point can be legitimately drawn only from a properly related grouping, either inductively (shared subjects or predicates) or deductively (point two comments on point one).
- **The test in short:** deduction is present when point two comments on point one and leads to a "therefore". Induction shows up in the grammar of the sentences: find the shared subject or shared predicate and base the inference on it. If nothing is shared, no inference is possible and the points don't belong in the document.
- **Reader projection — why the top point must come first.** Any pair of ideas, whether the start of a set or the start of a chain, makes readers expect a summary or a "therefore", so they guess ahead. A wrong guess leaves them confused and irritated. Stating the top point before its supporting ideas removes the guesswork.

## Rules

- **DED-1** — Decide, for every grouping, whether it is deductive or inductive — the only two ways ideas can be logically related. *Why:* logical sequence and the way you summarize differ by form. *Check:* label each grouping D or I; a grouping that is neither isn't a valid grouping. (p. 60)
- **DED-2** — Build a deductive argument from three moves: an assertion about an existing state of affairs; a second assertion saying something about the first one's subject or predicate; what follows from both being true together. *Why:* this is what makes deductive points grow out of one another. *Check:* point 2 reuses point 1's subject or predicate; the last point begins (or could begin) with "therefore". (p. 62)
- **DED-3** — Summarize a deductive grouping with the weight on its final point (e.g. "Because X, Y" / "Since X, Y" / "If you want X, you must Y"). *Why:* the conclusion carries the argument's meaning; the parent must still condense the whole chain. *Check:* the parent states the conclusion plus the key premise, not just the topic. (pp. 60, 63)
- **DED-4** — Skip steps or chain deductive arguments only when readers will readily supply and accept the missing steps. *Why:* every-step deduction is slow and fussy, but gaps readers can't fill break the argument. *Check:* reconstruct the full chain; restore any omitted step a reader might dispute. (p. 63)
- **DED-5** — On the Key Line, avoid deduction and present the message inductively. *Why:* a deductive Key Line forces readers to hold and cross-match several sets of points, delays the action, and makes them relive the writer's problem solving; deduction isn't "stronger" — it's the same reasoning arranged differently. *Check:* the Key Line points share a plural noun (actions, reasons, steps) rather than forming problem → cause → therefore. (pp. 64–65, 67)
- **DED-6** — Present the action before the argument — state *that* the reader should act and then justify each action, rather than explaining why and only then how. *Why:* the action is what the reader cares about. *Check:* the top point and Key Line state what to do; diagnosis appears beneath each action. (pp. 65–66)
- **DED-7** — Use a deductive Key Line only when (a) the top point clashes with what the reader expects the writer to say, or (b) the reader cannot understand the actions without the reasoning first. *Why:* in those cases the argument matters more to them than the actions. *Check:* if the Key Line is deductive, confirm one of the two conditions explicitly; otherwise convert it (DED-8). (pp. 66–67)
- **DED-8** — To convert a findings → conclusions → recommendations analysis to inductive form, rotate it: put the recommendations on the Key Line and group each one's findings/conclusions beneath it. *Why:* the reasoning is already complete; only its layout changes, and the rotated layout answers the reader's question first and keeps each subject in one place. *Check:* no top-level section is titled "Findings" or "Conclusions"; each recommendation section contains its own supporting evidence. (p. 65)
- **DED-9** — Keep deductive reasoning as far down the pyramid as possible — ideally within a paragraph. *Why:* deduction is easy to follow when its steps are adjacent, but loses clarity when pages separate them. *Check:* no deductive chain spans multiple sections; where deduction appears, its steps sit within a paragraph or short passage. (p. 67)
- **DED-10** — Keep any deductive argument to at most four points and at most two chained "therefore" points. *Why:* heavier chains can't be summarized well. *Check:* count points and "therefores" in each deductive grouping. (p. 68)
- **DED-11** — Name each inductive grouping with one plural noun that covers every member, and remove the misfits. *Why:* defining the grouping and spotting misfits are the two core inductive skills; the plural noun fixes what the inference can be about. *Check:* write the noun; test each item against it. (pp. 68–69)
- **DED-12** — Verify every inductive inference from the bottom up: from these items alone, could you reasonably reach the parent? *Why:* induction is the creative, error-prone form; the upward check catches careless leaps. *Check:* cover the parent, read the children, and see whether the parent is the inference you'd draw. (p. 70)
- **DED-13** — Keep the inference inside the grouping: the parent must speak to its children specifically and only. *Why:* if other, unlisted items could equally support the parent, it sits too high in abstraction; if the parent asserts a motive or cause the children don't show, it is careless. *Check:* could three or four other sets of facts justify the same parent? If yes, narrow it. (p. 70)
- **DED-14** — When a claim rests on a single piece of evidence, or the items form a causal chain, treat the grouping as deductive — don't dress a cause-and-effect sequence up as a list of parallel items. *Why:* that is deduction masquerading as induction, and the true parent is a "because" statement. *Check:* if A causes B causes C, the parent should read "C because A". (p. 71)
- **DED-15** — Tell the forms apart with one test: if the second point says something about the first one's subject or predicate, the grouping is deductive; if not, it must share the first one's plural noun (inductive); if neither, the points aren't logically related. *Why:* this is the defining difference; a "therefore" drawn from two members of a class is a fallacy. *Check:* apply the test to points 1 and 2 of every grouping. (p. 71)
- **DED-16** — Build inductive groupings by fixing the subject and varying the predicate, or fixing the predicate and varying the subject, and base the inference on that shared element. *Why:* an inductive relationship lives in sentence structure — shared subjects or shared predicates. *Check:* underline the repeated subject or predicate across items. (pp. 71–72)
- **DED-17** — Cut points that share neither subject nor predicate with their siblings: they are news, not thinking, however true. *Why:* a point belongs only if, together with others, it explains or supports a higher point, and unrelated items support no inference. *Check:* for any list, ask what (beyond the obvious repeated phrase) the varied elements have in common; if nothing, remove or regroup. (p. 72)
- **DED-18** — State the top point before the ideas grouped under it. *Why:* readers automatically anticipate a summary or a "therefore" and will supply their own; a mismatch confuses and irritates them. *Check:* no grouping makes the reader wait to learn where it is heading. (p. 72)

## Procedures

### Procedure A — Classify a grouping (deductive, inductive, or neither)

1. Take the first two points of the grouping.
2. Does point 2 say something further about the **subject** or the **predicate** of point 1 (rather than adding another case)?
   - Yes → candidate **deductive**. Go to step 3.
   - No → go to step 4.
3. Does the final point draw out what follows from the earlier ones being true together ("therefore")? Count points (≤ 4) and "therefores" (≤ 2).
   - Yes and within limits → valid deductive grouping. Write the parent as a "because/since/if" summary weighted on the final point (DED-3).
   - Too many points/therefores → split or simplify (DED-10).
4. Can point 2 be classed under the same plural noun as point 1 (same subject with a different predicate, or same predicate with a different subject)?
   - Yes → **inductive**. Name the noun; test all remaining points against it; remove misfits (DED-11).
   - No → the points are **not logically related**. Either they are news (DED-17) or they belong in different groupings.
5. Special case: if the "inductive" items are really links in one causal chain (A led to B led to C), reclassify as deductive (DED-14).

### Procedure B — Build and check an inductive grouping

1. Gather the candidate ideas.
2. Find the one plural noun naming the kind of idea they all are. If none fits, regroup.
3. Remove or relocate misfits.
4. Identify the shared element: same subject with varying predicates? Same predicate with varying subjects?
5. Draft the inference from that shared element.
6. Bottom-up check (DED-12): reading only the children, would you draw this inference?
7. Scope check (DED-13): could other, unlisted items equally justify the inference? If yes, narrow the parent so it speaks specifically and only to these items. Does the parent assert a motive or cause the items don't show? If yes, retract it.
8. Order the items (see `05-logical-order.md`).

### Procedure C — Build a deductive grouping

1. Write an assertion about an existing state of affairs.
2. Write a second assertion that says something about the first one's subject or predicate.
3. Write what follows from both being true ("Therefore...").
4. Optionally use one of the Exhibit 20 variants (an extra comment, or a second "therefore"), staying within four points and two "therefores".
5. If steps are obvious, you may chain or skip them — only if readers will supply and accept them (DED-4).
6. Write the parent with the weight on the final point.
7. Place it as low in the pyramid as possible (DED-9).

### Procedure D — Choose the form of the Key Line

1. Default: inductive (DED-5).
2. Ask: does the top point clash with what the reader expects — would their reaction be "Why? How? Are you sure?" →
   - Yes → a deductive Key Line is warranted (DED-7a).
3. Ask: can the reader understand the recommended actions without first understanding the reasoning behind them? →
   - No → a deductive Key Line is warranted (DED-7b).
4. Otherwise → inductive. If the material was developed deductively (problem → cause → solution), convert it with Procedure E.
5. Even with a deductive Key Line, keep it to ≤ 4 points and ≤ 2 "therefores" (DED-10).

### Procedure E — Rotate a Recommendation Worksheet into an inductive pyramid

1. Lay out the analysis in three columns: Findings (what's going wrong) | Conclusions (what's causing it) | Recommendations (what to do), one row per issue (A, B, C).
2. Top point: the overall action message (e.g. "You must change").
3. Key Line: the recommendations A3, B3, C3 — answering "How?".
4. Under each recommendation, place its own finding and conclusion (A1, A2 under A3, etc.) — answering "Why this action?". Deductive sub-arguments are fine at this level.
5. Check: each subject's information sits in one place; the reader learns what to do first.

### Procedure F — Audit for "news"

1. For each list or grouping, write down the element repeated across items.
2. Ask what the *varying* elements (e.g. the places, the people, the actions) have in common. If nothing, the list is news.
3. Either find a genuine shared quality that supports an inference, or remove the points.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Report is organized "Situation / Problems / Causes / Recommendations"; actions appear only at the end | Deductive Key Line; reader relives the analysis | Procedure E; DED-5, DED-6, DED-8 |
| Top-level sections titled "Findings" and "Conclusions" | Worksheet columns presented one at a time | DED-8 |
| Reader must remember point A from section 1 to make sense of section 4 | Deductive chain spread across pages | DED-9 |
| A deductive passage with five or six linked steps and several "therefores" | Over-long chain that can't be summarized | DED-10; split into separate arguments |
| An argument jumps a step the reader may not accept | Chaining beyond what the reader will supply | DED-4 |
| "Therefore" drawn from two parallel facts (all A are P, some B are P, therefore...) | Inductive pair mistaken for deduction (classic fallacy) | DED-15 |
| Parent claims a motive or cause ("because they want to") the examples don't show | Inference goes beyond the grouping | DED-12, DED-13 |
| Parent so general that many other facts would also support it ("represents an opportunity") | Summary pitched too high in abstraction | DED-13 |
| Three "indicators" that are really successive effects of one cause | Deduction masquerading as induction | DED-14 |
| List of true facts whose varying elements share nothing | News, not thinking | DED-17; Procedure F |
| Items in a group are a mix of class members and comments on earlier items | Deductive and inductive mixed in one grouping | DED-1, DED-15 |
| A grouping's purpose becomes clear only at its end; the reader guesses wrong en route | Top point withheld | DED-18 |
| Writer insists the deductive layout is "more rigorous" | Confusing reasoning with layout | DED-5 (same reasoning, different arrangement) |

## Templates and patterns

**Deductive grouping (basic)**

```
[Because <statement 2>, <conclusion>]
  Assertion about the world → Remark on its subject/predicate → Therefore <implication>
```

**Exhibit 20 — permissible deductive variants (max 4 points, max 2 "therefores"), with invented fillers**

```
(a) Statement → Comment → Therefore
    Our main supplier is raising prices 20% → That increase wipes out our margin on product X
    → Therefore we must reprice or drop product X
(b) Statement → Comment → Comment → Therefore
    Users adopt features that fit their routine → They avoid features that force new habits
    → The new dashboard forces new habits → Therefore adoption will be slow
(c) Statement → Comment → Therefore → Therefore
    The startup was seen as well placed → On that view investors raised valuations
    → So its next round was oversubscribed → So competitors rushed to raise too
(d) Statement → Therefore → Therefore
    Only the platform team can approve schema changes → So every feature with a schema change
    waits on them → So platform capacity sets the release pace
```

**Deductive summary sentence frames**

```
Because <minor point>, <conclusion>.
Since <minor point>, <conclusion>.
If you want <goal in statement 1>, you must <conclusion>.
```

**Inductive grouping**

```
[Inference about what the items share — specifically and only about them]
  ├─ <Subject A> <shared predicate>      (vary subject, fix predicate)
  ├─ <Subject B> <shared predicate>
  └─ <Subject C> <shared predicate>
          — or —
  ├─ <Shared subject> <predicate A>      (fix subject, vary predicate)
  ├─ <Shared subject> <predicate B>
  └─ <Shared subject> <predicate C>
  Plural noun: <kind of idea>
```

**Recommendation Worksheet and its rotation**

```
FINDINGS (what's wrong) | CONCLUSIONS (what's causing it) | RECOMMENDATIONS (what to do)
  A1                    |   A2                            |   A3
  B1                    |   B2                            |   B3
  C1                    |   C2                            |   C3

Deductive layout (avoid at the Key Line):
  You must change ← [A1 B1 C1] → [A2 B2 C2] → Therefore [A3 B3 C3]

Inductive layout (rotate 90° left):
  You must change
  ├─ A3   (Why? → A1, A2)
  ├─ B3   (Why? → B1, B2)
  └─ C3   (Why? → C1, C2)
```

**Key Line form decision**

```
Does the top point clash with what the reader expects?        → deductive
Can the reader not grasp the actions without the reasoning?   → deductive
Otherwise                                                     → inductive (action before argument)
```

## Worked examples

**Exhibit 16 (p. 61) — deduction vs. induction side by side.** Book's shape: deductive — a general capability of a class → the speaker belongs to that class → so the speaker has the capability, summarized as "X, because I am one of them"; inductive — three nations' tanks massing at one country's border, summarized as an imminent tank invasion of that country. Invented equivalents:

```
Deductive:  Friday's release needs a rollback plan, because it's a production deploy
              Every production deploy needs a rollback plan → Friday's release is a production deploy
              → Therefore it needs a rollback plan
Inductive:  Our login flow is driving users away
              ├─ Login-related support tickets have doubled
              ├─ App-store reviews cite login failures
              └─ Sales reports prospects abandoning at sign-in
```

Lesson: deductive points grow out of each other; inductive points are parallel members of a class.

**Exhibit 17 (p. 62) — five complete deductive arguments, by structure.**

```
1. Rule about a whole class → an individual belongs to the class → the rule applies to the individual
   (the textbook mortality syllogism)
2. A law exists to promote some activity → a particular group's control over a resource blocks
   that activity → so the law should be applied to that group
   (labour unions and anti-monopoly law)
3. Any company meeting our three criteria is worth buying → Company A meets all three
   → so Company A is worth buying
4. Sales volume comes from doing four things well → your current structure prevents all four
   → so change your structure
5. Most corporations hold both growing and mature businesses → the two types have opposite cash
   needs → so the mature ones can bankroll the growing ones
```

Lesson: each second point refers back to the first one's subject or predicate; each parent would carry the conclusion (e.g. "Since the group acts as a monopoly, the anti-monopoly law should cover it"; "If you want more volume, you must change your structure").

**Exhibit 18 (p. 63) — chained deduction.** Book's shape: top = continuing to export a recycled raw material will worsen an existing regional shortage of the product made from it; boxes = local supply of the raw material would meet local demand → but exports have created a lasting shortage of it → that shortage will deepen the existing product shortage. Fully written out, it is two syllogisms (six steps): supply suffices for us → but we exported it → so we're short; a raw-material shortage causes a product shortage → we have a raw-material shortage → so we'll have a product shortage. Invented equivalent:

```
Continuing to lend our two senior engineers to other teams will delay the migration
  Our team has enough senior capacity for the migration → But two seniors are on long-term loan,
  leaving a lasting gap → That gap will push the already tight migration past its deadline
```

Lesson: chaining is fine when readers can fill in the omitted steps; writing all six would be tedious and would withhold the point.

**"You must change" (p. 64) — deductive vs. inductive Key Line.** Deductive: You must change → what's wrong (A1 B1 C1) → what's causing it (A2 B2 C2) → therefore what to do (A3 B3 C3); readers must hold and cross-match three sets and reach the action last. Inductive: You must change → How? → A3, B3, C3, each with its own Why? (A1, A2...). Lesson: same reasoning; flipping Why/How answers the main question first and keeps each subject in one place.

**Exhibit 19 (p. 65) — Recommendation Worksheet.** Three columns (Findings / Conclusions / Recommendations), rows A–C. Lesson: problem analysis runs deductively, but the presentation needn't — rotate 90° to put the recommendations on the Key Line.

**Situations 1 and 2 (p. 66) — when the argument matters more than the action.**

```
Situation 1 (expected answer) — inductive:
Cutting your costs is easy
├─ Do A
├─ Do B
└─ Do C

Situation 2 (unexpected answer) — deductive:
Stop focusing on cost cuts; consider selling the business
  The business faces a rising threat from overseas competition
  → Its current corporate structure can't respond effectively to that threat
  → Other owners would be able to respond
```

Lesson: when the top point clashes with the reader's expectations (their reaction: Why? How? Are you sure?), lay out the argument first.

**Hertz risk-analysis article (p. 67; cited from Ch. 4, p. 38).** Readers needed the reasoning behind the analytical approach before its steps would make sense. Lesson: the second legitimate case for a deductive Key Line. (Details of the example are in the Ch. 4 files, `03-introductions.md` / `03b-introduction-patterns.md`.)

**Exhibit 20 (pp. 67–68) — the deductive form can vary, by structure.**

```
(a) Statement → Comment → Therefore
    A government softens its foreign stance for deliberate policy reasons → that return to normal
    diplomacy could shift the regional balance of power → so another major power must drop its
    pretence that the government doesn't exist
(b) Statement → Comment → Comment → Therefore
    Scientists welcome ideas that extend existing ones → but resist ideas that overturn how they
    think → atomic physics did overturn it → so atomic physics met slow acceptance
(c) Statement → Comment → Therefore → Therefore
    A group of bottling companies was thought well positioned → analysts therefore forecast earnings
    growth → as a result the share price rose → which triggered a wave of long-term buy ratings
(d) Statement → Therefore → Therefore
    Under central planning the state alone allocates everything → so the only power worth having is
    a share of existing power → so economic and social outcomes hinge entirely on who holds power
```

Lesson: valid deductive groupings can take several shapes, within four points and two "therefores". (Invented fillers for each shape are in Templates.)

**Exhibit 21 (p. 69) — inductive groupings with clean plural nouns.**

```
A historical scholar was clever but impractical, as the scholar's endless schemes show       [Schemes]
├─ A city where only Latin would be spoken
├─ A hole dug toward the earth's centre to find new substances
├─ Psychological research conducted with opium
└─ A gravitational explanation of how embryos form

Cut wasted effort in on-site work                                                   [Steps]
├─ Build smaller, more highly skilled crews
├─ Deploy crews according to available work
└─ Get accurate information on available work to each site

Jointly held property can harm you or your family later                            [Ways of being harmed]
├─ May disrupt your will
├─ May raise estate tax
├─ May trigger gift-tax liability
└─ May complicate a divorce settlement
```

Lesson: one plural noun per grouping; no misfits; the bottom-up check confirms the inference (for the scholar: yes).

**Exhibit 22 (p. 70) — inferences that overreach.**

```
Managers mismanage deliberately                                    ✗ (careless — motive not shown)
├─ Avoid facing reality
├─ Reject internal criticism
├─ Keep losing activities alive
├─ Neglect detail
└─ Never question policy                 [Examples of mismanagement]

Composing-room costs may be a profit-improvement opportunity        ✗ (too abstract)
├─ Low productivity
├─ High overtime
└─ Uncompetitive prices on simple jobs   [Indicators]
   → actually a causal chain: low productivity → high overtime → uncompetitive prices
   → implied parent: prices are high because productivity is low
```

Lesson: the inference must concern these items specifically and only; a causal sequence is deduction, not a list of indicators.

**Logic-textbook fallacies (p. 71).** Shape: all A are P; some B are P; therefore some B are A (Minto's two instances: one political, one about two animal species that are both fast runners). Invented: all cats have whiskers; some dogs have whiskers; therefore some dogs are cats. Lesson: the second point adds a class member instead of commenting on the first; that's an inductive pair, so no "therefore" follows.

**One statement, three continuations (pp. 71–72).** Book's shape: a statement that one nation's business people are stepping up their push into a market, followed by (a) a deductive comment (a rival group's arrival will spur them further), (b) inductive variants varying the subject (other nations doing likewise → smart money moving in) or the predicate (the same group pushing into neighbouring markets → an aggressive regional push), and (c) a "news" set (the same group pushing into three markets with nothing in common → no inference). Invented equivalent:

```
Statement:             Acme is cutting its prices in the UK
Deductive follow-on:   → Those cuts will squeeze Beta's UK margins
Inductive (vary subject):   A UK price war has begun
                       ├─ Acme is cutting its prices in the UK
                       ├─ Beta is cutting its prices in the UK
                       └─ Gamma is cutting its prices in the UK
Inductive (vary predicate): Acme is fighting for European share
                       ├─ Acme is cutting its prices in the UK
                       ├─ Acme is cutting its prices in France
                       └─ Acme is cutting its prices in Germany
News (no inference):   Acme opened a Lisbon office; changed its logo; its CFO spoke at a conference
                       → the varied elements share nothing → cut
```

Lesson: inductive relationships live in shared subjects or predicates; with nothing shared there is only news.

## Nuances and exceptions

- **Thinking vs. writing.** Deduction is how the mind prefers to think and solve problems (it's easier to construct), which is why writers default to presenting it. Minto objects only to deduction as the layout for the upper levels of a document.
- **Deduction isn't more rigorous.** The reasoning is identical; only the page layout differs. Rotating the worksheet changes nothing about validity.
- **Key Line only.** The anti-deduction advice targets the Key Line (and, by extension, high levels). Within a paragraph deduction reads well; the problem is distance between steps.
- **Two named exceptions.** A deductive Key Line is right when the top point clashes with what the reader expects (Situation 2), or when they can't understand the actions without the reasoning first (Hertz). Minto says she can think of no other automatic case and that few business readers fit either.
- **Induction is harder to write, easier to read.** Inductive reasoning is the more creative and harder-to-master form, yet it is the easier form for readers to absorb at high levels.
- **The limits are soft but practical.** You *can* exceed four points or two "therefores" (as French philosophers do), but you lose the ability to summarize properly.
- **The definition drives the inference.** The same facts (tanks at a border) support different inferences depending on how you define them (moves against the border country vs. its allies preparing to attack elsewhere). Defining the grouping is a creative act, refined in Ch. 6.
- **Inferences may date.** Minto notes the scholar inference held at least when it was written — the bottom-up check is relative to the knowledge of the time.
- **One piece of evidence forces deduction.** If a claim rests on a single fact, there is no class to induce from; the reasoning is deductive whether or not it is laid out that way.
- **"Findings" vs. "conclusions" is arbitrary.** Both are summaries at different levels of abstraction; a summary of findings is a conclusion.
- **Abduction folds into induction.** Hypothesis-first reasoning (Appendix A) becomes induction once the supporting information is gathered — so the presentation choice is still only deductive vs. inductive.
- **Truth is not enough.** True "news" still doesn't belong if it supports no higher point.

> **Skill note:** For an agent reviewing a consulting-style deck or report, the highest-yield check from this chapter is "Is the Key Line a set of actions (inductive) or a problem → cause → solution sequence (deductive)? If deductive, does DED-7 justify it?" Most drafts fail here.

> **Skill note:** Procedure A (the second-point test) is mechanical enough to apply sentence by sentence: underline the subject and predicate of point 1, then see whether point 2 talks about one of them (deductive) or repeats one of them with a new partner (inductive).

> **Skill note:** The invented examples in this file (contracts, releases, Acme/Beta/Gamma, hiring) are reference-author constructions that mirror the shapes of Minto's exhibits; they are not from the book.
