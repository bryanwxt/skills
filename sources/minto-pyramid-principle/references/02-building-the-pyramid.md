# Building the Pyramid: Top-Down and Bottom-Up Procedures

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 3 "How to Build a Pyramid Structure", book pp. 21–33. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `BLD`.

## When a skill needs this

- Helping a user plan a memo, email, report or deck from scratch — drawing the right points out of their head in the right order before any drafting.
- Restructuring an existing draft that describes rather than answers (e.g. explains how something would work when the reader asked whether to do it).
- Rescuing a rambling draft whose author can't yet say what the main point is (bottom-up: list → relate → conclude).
- Writing or reviewing an introduction so it contains only what the reader knows, ends in the main point, and previews the Key Line.
- Coaching a beginner through the common traps (drafting first and structuring later, skipping the introduction, history in the body, deductive Key Lines).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Top box | The apex of the pyramid: one sentence whose grammatical subject is the document's Subject and whose predicate is the Answer. |
| Subject | What the document is about; it becomes the subject of the top sentence. |
| Question | What the reader wants settled about the Subject by the time they finish reading. |
| Answer | The top point; the predicate of the top sentence. |
| Situation | The first uncontroversial statement about the Subject that the reader will accept as fact. |
| Complication | The development within the Situation that triggers the Question (the reply to the reader's imagined "So what?"). |
| New Question | The question the Answer itself provokes; the Key Line answers it. |
| Key Line | The row of points directly under the top box. |
| Top-down approach | Building from Subject/Question/Answer and the introduction down to the Key Line and its support. |
| Bottom-up approach | Listing all points, working out how they relate, and deriving conclusions upward. |
| Closed universe | Treating a document under revision as the only source of facts — clarify it without judging its truth. |
| Overstructured argument | A deductive Key Line containing a step that raises no question and could be dropped; a cue to switch to induction. |
| 30-second test | The reader should grasp the writer's entire thinking within roughly the first half-minute of reading. |
| Action ideas vs. situation ideas | Recommendations/solutions vs. descriptions of what is; action ideas are easier to validate (Ch. 7). |

## Concepts

### Ch. 3 opening: what you already know before you start (p. 21)

- The usual starting point: a writer knows the general topic but not the specific message or how to express it — and that uncertainty doesn't lift just because the end result must be a pyramid.
- Yet several things about the finished product are already certain:
  - The apex will be a single sentence containing a subject and a predicate.
  - That sentence's subject will be the document's subject.
  - The sentence will answer a question the reader already has.
  - That question exists because something (the Complication, which the reader knows about) happened within a setting (the Situation, also familiar to the reader) — and that sequence is the reason the writer has to write at all.
  - Some of the points to be made may already be roughly known.
- This knowledge can be used from either end: begin at the apex and descend, or begin at the base and climb. Descending is usually the easier route, so attempt it first.

### The Top-Down Approach (pp. 22–26)

- Why it's easier: it begins with the things the writer is surest of — the subject, and what the reader already knows about it (which the introduction will recall for them).
- Resist the urge to start writing the opening paragraph. Instead, let the shape of the introduction act as a prompt that extracts the right points, one by one.
- **Exhibit 4 (p. 22), captioned to show that the parts of the structure verify one another.** The diagram splits the top box into Subject (labelled 1) and Predicate/Answer (labelled 3); beside it, the introduction elements carry numbers too — Situation 4, Complication 5, Question 2; under the top box sits the New Question (6), then the Key Line boxes (7), then the support boxes (8). Its accompanying checklist, in four stages:
  - *Top box:* name the Subject (1); state the reader's Question about it (2); state the Answer (3).
  - *Test the Answer against the introduction:* identify the Situation (4) and the Complication (5); confirm the Question and Answer still hold (back to 2).
  - *Key Line:* work out the New Question the Answer raises (6); choose deductive or inductive treatment (7); if inductive, name the plural noun (7).
  - *Support:* apply the same question/answer routine one level down (8).
- The text's sequence, each step with a fallback when something is unknown:
  1. **Box.** *Do:* draw the apex box; enter the Subject. *If unknown:* go on to step 2.
  2. **Question.** *Prompt:* who exactly is reading, and what should be settled for them about the Subject by the last page? *If unknown:* skip to step 4 (the Situation will help reveal it).
  3. **Answer.** *Do:* enter it. *If unknown:* simply confirm that you are able to answer.
  4. **Situation.** *Goal:* confirm that Q and A are as sharp as you can make them for now. *Do:* widen out from the Subject to its setting and write one statement about the Subject that the reader will accept without argument. *Acceptance test:* the reader is already familiar with it, or it is a matter of record anyone could verify. The opening Situation sentence must be about the Subject itself.
  5. **Complication.** *Prompt:* picture the reader nodding at the Situation and replying "Fine — and?" — this is where the dialogue starts. *Do:* write the event inside the Situation that produced their Question. *Typical forms:* a setback; a newly surfaced problem; an inconsistency that has come to light.
  6. **Recheck.** *Test:* the Complication should lead straight to the Question already written. *If it leads elsewhere:* adopt the question it actually raises. *If nothing fits:* either the Complication or the Question is wrong — rethink both.
- The whole routine exists to pin down which Question is really being answered. With that settled, the rest falls into place with relatively little effort.
- Once the introduction holds together, the worked example continues: (6) identify the New Question the Answer provokes; (7) select Key Line points that answer it — a "Why?" is always answered with reasons; (8) confirm the points are the right ones and correctly ordered, then descend and work out the support for each. In a very short document further structuring is usually unnecessary; the supporting material tends to surface naturally as each section is written.
- **What the routine achieves:** it extracts from the writer only what bears on the reader's question, and it pushes the writer to address that question completely rather than in part. Presented from the top down, the resulting message is very easy to take in.
- Note: the text labels the introduction recheck as step 6 and then reuses 6 for the New Question in the worked example; Exhibit 4 labels the recheck as a return to step 2. The sequence of operations is the same either way (see Procedure A).

### The Bottom-Up Approach (pp. 26–30)

- **When to use it:** often the thinking isn't mature enough to settle the apex — the Subject is fuzzy, the Question is unclear, or it's hard to separate what the reader knows from what they don't. In that case, drop down to the Key Line.
- If Key Line points come to mind, good; frequently they won't. That's not a dead end — the ideas can be built upward in three moves:
  1. Write down every point you expect to make.
  2. Figure out how those points connect.
  3. Derive conclusions from the connections.
- Demonstration (Exhibits 7–8): a progress memo from a junior consultant to the engagement manager, written two weeks into the consultant's first assignment at a printing company (TTW). Minto has no knowledge beyond the memo itself, so she treats it as a **closed universe** — no verdict on whether its content is correct, only an effort to make its message clear.
- **Move 1 — list.** Extracting and sorting the memo's content yields eight problems and two solutions. Start with the **recommendations**, since action ideas are easier to validate than situation ideas (Ch. 7). Here the two proposed solutions (streamlining the process for low-value jobs; raising productivity through new methods) amount to the same idea, so comparing them adds nothing.
- **Move 2 — relate.** The problems carry implied cause-and-effect links, which should be drawn out as visually as possible. The diagram exposed two distinct chains of reasoning and hinted that some needed points were absent (it includes a box marked "(?) High costs" that the memo never actually states).
- **Move 3 — conclude.** Two rival structures appear: one says costs are high because productivity is poor and overtime heavy; the other says the way to cut costs is to simplify methods and raise pay. **The tie-breaker is the introduction:** what does the memo imply its reader already knows? Apparently that composing costs matter a great deal, that the firm's pricing on simple jobs is uncompetitive, and probably that no one there knows whether the costs are excessive. Running S-C-Q-A from that base shifts the Question from "are the costs too high?" to "can they be reduced?", and yields a How? Key Line (see Worked examples).
- **The introduction carries the Key Line.** Exhibit 8's introduction closes by listing the Key Line points; Minto prints the whole rewritten memo precisely to make this visible. Payoffs: (1) the reader holds the writer's complete thinking after about half a minute; (2) because the remainder only elaborates, no major point can ambush them later; (3) a time-pressed reader can skim. Test: if a reader can't extract the whole argument in roughly 30 seconds, rewrite.
- **Headings.** Purpose: let readers jump to the detail behind any main point — most valuable in long documents. Form: an idea, not a category (Ch. 10). Banned: labels like "Findings" or "Conclusions", which tell a scanning reader nothing. (Exhibit 8's two headings are simply its two actions — eliminating steps; raising wages.)
- **Style isn't what changed.** The original and rewritten memos use almost identical wording. The rewrite is clear because its ideas are arranged as a pyramid, not because its prose was refined.

### Caveats for Beginners (pp. 31–33)

- Because the rules are so specific, a writer can begin from any single idea and work out the rest; in practice, though, the work runs in one of two directions — apex-first or base-first. Since the variations are endless, Minto addresses the questions beginners raise most often:
1. **Default to top-down.** *Trap:* dictating the whole document "just to get it down" and planning to structure it later. *Why it fails:* text acquires an aura of perfection the moment it exists, and writers grow attached to a typed draft whatever its logical gaps — so the restructuring never happens.
2. **Think through the introduction starting from the Situation.** *Distinction:* thinking order vs. writing order. Writing order is free — S, C, Q and A can appear in any sequence, chosen for effect and tone, and will differ by document type (Ch. 4). Thinking order is fixed: S first, because it gives the best chance of landing on the correct Complication and Question.
3. **Never skip the introduction step.** *Temptation:* when the main point is already crisp and its triggering Question feels self-evident, writers leap to the Key Line and start answering the New Question. *Typical damage:* Situation and Complication material gets dragged into the body and structured as argument, yielding a convoluted, unwieldy deductive Key Line. *Remedy:* settle the introductory material first, which frees full attention for the lower levels.
4. **Keep narrative history in the introduction.** *Rule:* the body is not a place to recount events for the record. *Reason:* the body may contain only ideas (statements offering new thinking, which therefore raise questions), and ideas connect only through logic. *Exception:* events may appear in the body as links in a cause-and-effect explanation, because such links are products of analysis. A plain chronology reflects no reasoning, so it doesn't qualify as an idea.
5. **Put in the introduction only what the reader will accept as true.** *Role:* the introduction recalls what readers already know. *Difficulty:* the writer often can't tell whether a reader knows a given fact, and is sometimes sure they don't. *Working test:* if a neutral observer could readily verify the point, treat the reader as "knowing" it — meaning they won't challenge it.
   - Keep anything new to the reader out of the introduction — it will skew their Question.
   - Keep anything the reader already knows out of the pyramid. If familiar information is being used to answer a lower-level question, something important was probably omitted from the introduction — something that, had it been included, would have led the reader to a different Question.
6. **When there's a choice, make the Key Line inductive.** (Expanded in Ch. 5.) *Reason:* an inductive Key Line demands less effort, so it is absorbed more easily. *Common pull:* writers want to present ideas in the order they were reached — usually a deductive order. *Correction:* discovery order doesn't dictate presentation order; most deductively derived conclusions can be laid out inductively. Minto's warehouse example: a recommendation to buy a specific property supported by a three-step deductive Key Line (we judge warehouses by three criteria → this one meets them → so buy it). The final step raises no question; if the apex is stated first and the Key Line next, it contributes nothing. That redundancy marks the argument as **overstructured** and signals that an inductive version would communicate more efficiently (see Worked examples).

## Rules

- **BLD-1** — Try the top-down approach first; never dictate or draft the whole document "to get it down" and structure it afterward. *Why:* top-down starts from what you're surest of, and once written, ideas acquire a false finish that makes writers resist restructuring. *Check:* the top box and S-C-Q-A exist (or were attempted) before any prose was drafted. (pp. 21, 31)
- **BLD-2** — Start from what you already know: the top will be one sentence whose subject is the document's Subject and whose predicate is the Answer to an existing reader Question that arose from a known Situation and Complication. *Why:* those certainties anchor the search for the rest. *Check:* the top point is a full sentence (subject + predicate), not a topic label. (p. 21)
- **BLD-3** — Fill the top box in the order Subject → Question → Answer, skipping ahead whenever one is unknown, and at least confirm that you *can* answer. *Why:* each element constrains the next; unknowns get resolved by the introduction check. *Check:* Subject, Question and Answer are each written down (or explicitly marked unknown). (p. 22; Exh. 4)
- **BLD-4** — Begin the introduction with the first uncontroversial statement about the Subject that the reader will accept — because it is familiar to them or a matter of verifiable record. *Why:* it gives writer and reader a shared starting point and tests whether Q and A are right. *Check:* the Situation's first sentence concerns the Subject and contains nothing the reader would dispute. (pp. 22–23, 25)
- **BLD-5** — Derive the Complication by imagining the reader responding "Fine — and?": state what occurred within the Situation (a setback, a new problem, an inconsistency) that triggers the Question. *Why:* the Complication is what creates the reader's need. *Check:* the Complication describes a change or tension within the Situation, not your answer. (p. 23)
- **BLD-6** — Recheck that the Complication leads straight to the Question you wrote and that your Answer answers it; if not, adopt the question it does raise, or revise the Complication, and rethink. *Why:* pinning down the right Question is the purpose of the exercise; once it's right, the rest follows. *Check:* read S, C aloud — the next thing a reader would naturally ask is your Q, and your top point answers it. (p. 23)
- **BLD-7** — Find the Key Line by writing down the New Question the Answer raises, deciding whether to answer it inductively or deductively, and (if inductively) naming the plural noun. *Why:* the Key Line exists only to answer that question; a "Why?" is always answered with reasons. *Check:* the New Question is written; every Key Line point is an instance of the plural noun. (p. 25; Exh. 4)
- **BLD-8** — Confirm the Key Line points are the right ones and logically ordered, then repeat the question/answer routine at each lower level; for a very short document you may stop structuring and write. *Why:* support follows the same dialogue; in short documents it surfaces readily. *Check:* each Key Line point has a written question and answering children, or the document is short enough not to need them. (p. 26)
- **BLD-9** — If you can't settle the top (Subject, Question or reader knowledge unclear), drop to the Key Line; if no Key Line points come, work bottom-up: list all points, work out how they connect, derive conclusions. *Why:* the bottom-up route still converges on a pyramid when the top is fuzzy. *Check:* a point list, a relationship diagram, and at least one candidate conclusion exist. (p. 26)
- **BLD-10** — When restructuring someone else's document, treat it as a closed universe: clarify what it says without judging whether it is true or right. *Why:* the editor's job is clarity; adding outside facts or opinions muddies the task. *Check:* every point in the rewrite traces to the original; nothing new is asserted. (p. 26)
- **BLD-11** — In bottom-up work, sort the listed points by type (e.g. problems vs. solutions) and examine the action ideas first, merging any that say the same thing. *Why:* action ideas are easier to validate than situation ideas. *Check:* recommendations were reviewed for duplication before problems were analysed. (p. 28)
- **BLD-12** — Lay out cause-and-effect relationships among situation points visually, looking for separate chains of reasoning and for missing links. *Why:* the diagram shows how many lines of argument exist and which points were left out. *Check:* a causal chain/diagram exists; implied but unstated points are marked (e.g. "(?)"). (p. 28)
- **BLD-13** — When the points could support more than one conclusion, choose by thinking through the introduction — what the reader already knows determines their Question, and so which structure answers it. *Why:* the right pyramid is the one that answers the reader's actual question. *Check:* the chosen top point is reached via an explicit S-C-Q-A built from what the reader knows. (p. 29)
- **BLD-14** — End the introduction by stating the Key Line points, so the reader has your whole thinking within about the first 30 seconds; if not, rewrite. *Why:* the rest of the document only explains or supports, so readers can trust there are no surprises and can skim. *Check:* read only the first paragraph(s) — can you state the top point and every Key Line point? (p. 29)
- **BLD-15** — Word headings as ideas (the point of the section), not as categories; never use headings like "Findings" or "Conclusions". *Why:* category headings tell a scanning reader nothing; idea headings let them find the discussion of any point. *Check:* each heading would work on its own as a Key Line point. (p. 30; see Ch. 10)
- **BLD-16** — Begin thinking about the introduction with the Situation, even though S, C, Q, A may later be written in any order for effect. *Why:* thinking S-first makes finding the correct Complication and Question more likely; written order only sets tone. *Check:* planning notes start with S, whatever the final written order. (p. 31)
- **BLD-17** — Never skip thinking through the introduction, even when the main point and its Question feel obvious. *Why:* jumping straight to the Key Line drags introductory material into the body and forces a convoluted deductive argument. *Check:* if the Key Line is deductive and its first step is background the reader knows, move that step to S/C. (p. 31)
- **BLD-18** — Put historical chronology ("what happened") in the introduction; in the body, mention events only when spelling out cause-and-effect relationships. *Why:* the body may contain only ideas related logically; a bare sequence of events is not the product of thought. *Check:* no body section is a narrative of events; any events in the body serve a causal argument. (pp. 31–32)
- **BLD-19** — Limit the introduction to what the reader will accept as true (known, or readily verifiable by a neutral observer); keep anything new to them out of it, and keep anything they already know out of the pyramid. *Why:* new information in the introduction skews their Question; known information in the body implies the introduction omitted something that would have changed their Question. *Check:* nothing in S/C would be news to the reader; nothing in the body is something they already know. (p. 32)
- **BLD-20** — Given a choice, make the Key Line inductive rather than deductive, even if the ideas were developed deductively. *Why:* an inductive Key Line takes less effort to absorb; discovery order need not be presentation order. *Check:* the Key Line points share a plural noun rather than forming premise → premise → "therefore". (p. 32; full treatment in DED-5)
- **BLD-21** — Treat a deductive Key Line step that raises no question (typically the "therefore" restating the top point) as a sign of overstructuring, and recast the Key Line inductively. *Why:* once the top point is stated first, that step adds nothing. *Check:* for each Key Line point, ask what question it raises; a point that raises none and repeats the top should go. (p. 33)

## Procedures

### Procedure A — Top-down build (Exhibit 4), with decision points

Inputs: the subject area, the intended reader, anything you know about what the reader knows.

1. **Draw the top box.** Write the Subject (what the document is about).
   - If you can't name the Subject → skip to step 2.
2. **Decide the Question.** Identify the reader. Write the question about the Subject you want settled for them when they finish.
   - If you can't state it → skip to step 4 and let the introduction reveal it.
3. **Write the Answer** (the predicate of the top sentence: Subject + Answer = top point).
   - If you don't know it yet, note "answerable" and continue — firm it up after step 6.
4. **Situation.** Write the first uncontroversial statement about the Subject that the reader will accept (familiar to them, or verifiable record). For planning, capture only the gist of the paragraph, not its full wording.
5. **Complication.** Imagine the reader saying "Fine — and?". Write what occurred within the Situation to trigger the Question (a setback; a new problem; an inconsistency).
6. **Recheck Q and A.** Does C lead straight to the Q from step 2?
   - Yes, and A answers it → continue.
   - C raises a different question → replace Q with that one; revise A.
   - Neither fits → the C or the Q is wrong; return to step 4 or 2 and rethink.
   - Don't proceed until Q is right; the rest depends on it.
7. **New Question.** Write the question the Answer provokes (usually Why? or How?).
8. **Key Line form.** Decide: answer inductively or deductively?
   - Default to inductive (BLD-20, DED-5) unless the Answer is something the reader won't expect, or they can't understand the actions without prior explanation (see `04-deduction-and-induction.md`).
   - If inductive → name the plural noun (Why? → reasons; How? → steps/ways/actions).
9. **Key Line points.** List the points that answer the New Question, each an instance of the plural noun. Check that they fully answer it and are logically ordered.
10. **Support.** For each Key Line point, repeat steps 7–9 one level down (its question → its answering grouping). End a leg when the reader would have no further logical question.
    - Very short document → you may stop after step 9 and write; the support will come as you write each section.
11. **Assemble the introduction** for writing: S, C, (Q), A, then a statement of the Key Line points (BLD-14). Choose the written order of S/C/Q/A for tone (see `03-introductions.md`).
12. **Write headings** as the Key Line ideas themselves (BLD-15).

### Procedure B — Bottom-up build (3 moves) and hand-off to top-down

Use when: the Subject is fuzzy, the Question unclear, or what the reader knows is unclear — and no Key Line points come to mind.

1. **Move 1 — List.** Write every point you expect to make, one line each.
   - If restructuring someone else's document: extract its points only; treat it as a closed universe (BLD-10).
   - Sort the list by type (e.g. Problems / Solutions; situation ideas / action ideas).
2. **Move 2 — Relate.**
   1. Examine the action ideas (recommendations/solutions) first (BLD-11). Merge duplicates; note whether they are steps toward one goal or alternatives.
   2. Then examine situation ideas (problems/findings). Look for cause and effect: draw arrows from cause to effect.
   3. Note how many separate chains appear and where links seem missing; add placeholder boxes for implied-but-unstated points.
3. **Move 3 — Conclude.** For each chain or cluster, write the conclusion it supports (a "because" summary for a causal chain; an inference for a like-kind cluster; a goal for a set of actions).
   - One conclusion emerges → it is your candidate Answer.
   - Several candidate structures emerge → go to step 4 to choose.
4. **Choose via the introduction (BLD-13).** Determine what the reader already knows (from the brief or the original document). Run Procedure A steps 1–7 with that knowledge: Subject, Question, Answer, Situation, Complication, recheck (the Question may shift here), New Question.
5. **Key Line.** The candidate structure whose Key Line answers the New Question wins. Order its points and attach the supporting chains beneath each.
6. **Write** the introduction ending with the Key Line points (BLD-14), idea headings (BLD-15), and the body leg by leg.

### Procedure C — Choosing the entry point

1. Can you state the Subject and the reader's Question? → Procedure A.
2. Can't state them, but can name a few main points? → Start at the Key Line; derive the top point by summarizing them (see `06-summarizing-grouped-ideas.md`) and validate with S-C-Q-A.
3. Can't do either? → Procedure B.
4. In every case, finish by running the S-C-Q-A check (BLD-17).

### Procedure D — Pre-write caveat check (run before drafting prose)

1. Was the structure worked out before drafting? (BLD-1)
2. Did the introduction thinking start from the Situation? (BLD-16)
3. Is any Key Line point really background the reader knows? → move it to S/C. (BLD-17, BLD-19)
4. Is any body section a narrative of events? → move the history to the introduction or recast it as cause and effect. (BLD-18)
5. Does the introduction contain anything the reader doesn't know or would dispute? → remove it or move it to the body. (BLD-19)
6. Is the Key Line deductive? → can it be recast inductively (Procedure E)? (BLD-20)
7. Does the introduction end with the Key Line points? (BLD-14)
8. Are headings ideas, not categories? (BLD-15)

### Procedure E — Recast an overstructured deductive Key Line as inductive (warehouse pattern)

1. Write the deductive Key Line as it stands (e.g. our criteria → this option meets them → therefore choose it).
2. For each Key Line point, ask what question it raises once the top point has already been stated.
3. If the final "therefore" raises no question and merely restates the top point → it is redundant (overstructured).
4. Fold the general premise into the top point (e.g. "...because it meets our criteria").
5. Promote the specific facts that satisfy each criterion to the Key Line as a like-kind set (one plural noun — here, criteria met).
6. Recheck: each Key Line point raises a question you can answer below it.

## Diagnostics

| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Memo describes how something would work but never says whether to do it (Exhibit 5 pattern) | Writer never identified the reader's Question | Procedure A; BLD-3, BLD-6 |
| Opening sentence is a topic label ("Re: customer request") rather than a claim | Top box has a Subject but no predicate/Answer | BLD-2, BLD-3 |
| Draft lists findings in the order they were discovered, conclusions at the end (Exhibit 7 pattern) | No structure worked out; bottom-up thinking presented as-is | Procedure B; BLD-1, BLD-9 |
| Two "solutions" that are really the same idea in different words | Action ideas not compared | BLD-11 |
| Problems listed without their causal links; reader can't see why costs are high | Relationships not worked out | BLD-12 |
| Several plausible conclusions; writer hedges among them | Reader's knowledge/Question not used to choose | BLD-13 |
| Introduction doesn't preview the main points; key thinking emerges only pages later | Key Line missing from the introduction | BLD-14 |
| Headings like "Findings", "Analysis", "Conclusions", "Background" | Category headings with no scanning value | BLD-15 |
| A long deductive Key Line whose first step is context the reader already knows | Introduction skipped; introductory material pushed into the body | BLD-17, BLD-19 |
| Body section titled "History" or "What happened" narrating events | Chronology in the body | BLD-18 |
| Introduction asserts things the reader would question or hasn't heard | Introduction informing instead of reminding; Question skewed | BLD-19 |
| Body spends space restating facts the reader already knows | Known information in the pyramid; the introduction probably omitted something | BLD-19 |
| Key Line reads premise → premise → "therefore" restating the top point | Overstructured deduction | BLD-20, BLD-21; Procedure E |
| Writer has polished prose for hours but reviewers still find it unclear | Ordering problem, not style | PYR-1; Procedure A or B |

```
5. Complication:       ______________________   ("Fine — and?")
   Recheck → does C raise Q? does A answer it?  [ ] yes  [ ] change Q  [ ] change C
6. New Question:       ______________________   (raised by A: Why? / How?)
7. Key Line form:      inductive / deductive    plural noun: ________
   Key Line points:    1. ______  2. ______  3. ______
8. Support:            repeat 6–7 under each Key Line point
```

**Bottom-up worksheet**

```
MOVE 1  List
  Situation ideas (problems/findings)      Action ideas (solutions/recommendations)
  1. ...                                   1. ...
  2. ...                                   2. ...

MOVE 2  Relate
  Actions: duplicates? alternatives? steps toward one goal?
  Situation: cause → effect chains
     [a] → [b] → [c] → [(?) implied] → [d]
     [e] → [f] ─────────────┘

MOVE 3  Conclude
  Candidate pyramid 1: <conclusion> ← <chain/cluster>
  Candidate pyramid 2: <conclusion> ← <chain/cluster>
  Choose via S-C-Q-A from what the reader knows.
```

**Introduction that carries the Key Line (Exhibit 8 shape)**

```
[S] What I did / what we both know about the subject.
[C] What is uncertain or wrong.
[A] Our conclusion is that <top point>, by / because:
      • <Key Line point 1>
      • <Key Line point 2>
<KEY LINE POINT 1 AS A HEADING>
  ...support...
<KEY LINE POINT 2 AS A HEADING>
  ...support...
```

**Overstructured-to-inductive conversion**

```
Before (deductive):   [Top: Do X]
                       Criteria are A,B,C → X meets A,B,C → Therefore do X
After (inductive):    [Top: Do X because it meets our criteria]
                       ├─ It meets A
                       ├─ It meets B
                       └─ It meets C
```

## Worked examples

**Exhibits 5–6 (pp. 23–25) — a customer's request to change the billing process (top-down).** Context: a soft-drinks company bills from delivery tickets that drivers send in, on a cycle of about five weeks (tickets processed → bill sent → payment received → payment processed). A large fast-food customer (called Big Chief) proposes keeping its own tickets, recording and totalling them on disk, and mailing the disk with a check once a month — shrinking the company's side to roughly a day (disk and check received → payment processed). The accounting head was asked whether this would work. Before (Exhibit 5, captioned as failing to answer the question): a memo that confirms the review was done, then sets out the data format required, the extraction program the customer would write, the reconciliation procedure, and the downstream processing — in effect a description of how the new arrangement would operate, with no verdict on whether to accept it. Top-down rebuild:

```
1 Subject      = the customer's request to change the billing process
2 Question     = Is it a good idea?
3 Answer       = Yes
4 Situation    = They have asked for a procedural change
5 Complication = You asked me whether it makes sense
  Recheck: the Question this raises ("does it make sense?") ≈ step 2 → Q and A match
6 New Question = Why?
7 Key Line (a Why? is answered with reasons):

The customer's proposed billing change is a good idea
├─ We'd get all the information we need
├─ Our cash flow would improve
└─ Our workload would drop
8 Short document → write without further structuring
```

Lesson: the routine extracts only what bears on the reader's question — and forces the writer to answer it fully instead of describing around it.

**Exhibits 7–8 (pp. 26–30) — printing-company composing-room memo (TTW; bottom-up).** Before (Exhibit 7, captioned as rambling): a two-week progress memo that opens with already-known context (composing is the dominant cost element — around 40% of hardback costs and roughly half of paperback costs), tabulates the components of composing cost, then drifts through low productivity against industry standards (PAR), every job going through identical quality steps, local interest in investigating, departmental overload and late jobs, below-market pay and hiring difficulty, a pending union claim, recent departures, under-budget staffing and overtime far above budget — and closes with a "Conclusions" list (two ways to cut costs, an experiment, a possible comparison with competitors, mixed views among local managers).

Move 1, list:

```
PROBLEMS                                    SOLUTIONS
1 Poor composing productivity               1 Streamline the process for low-value jobs
2 Every job goes through identical steps    2 Raise productivity via new methods
3 Uncompetitive pricing on simple jobs         (→ same idea; nothing to analyse)
4 Work running late
5 Below-market pay
6 Too few staff
7 Heavy overtime
8 Below industry standard in typesetting and hand composition
```

Move 2, relate (cause → effect):

```
Low pay(5) → Too few staff(6) → Late work(4) → Heavy overtime(7) → (?) High costs → Uncompetitive price(3)
Identical steps(2) → Below standard(8) → Poor productivity(1) ──────────────↗
```

Two separate chains; "high costs" is implied but never stated.

Move 3, candidate conclusions:

```
Costs are high                    To cut costs
├─ Poor productivity              ├─ Remove steps
└─ Heavy overtime                 │   ├─ On simple jobs
                                  │   └─ Across the board
                                  └─ Raise pay
```

Choosing via the introduction (the reader knows costs matter and that pricing on simple jobs is uncompetitive; no one knows whether costs are excessive):

```
Subject      = composing-room costs
Question     = are they excessive?   Answer = yes
Situation    = composing is the largest element in total cost
Complication = no one knows if the share is too high, but uncompetitive pricing hints it may be
Question (revised) = can they be reduced?   Answer = yes
New Question = how?
Key Line     = remove unnecessary composing steps; bring pay up to market levels
```

After (Exhibit 8, captioned as making the conclusions clear):

```
[Intro] Two weeks spent on composing-room costs; recap of the known cost share; the firm
        can't tell whether these costs are too high, but is seen as uncompetitive on simple
        jobs. Preliminary view: costs could likely be cut substantially by the two actions below.
Composing costs could probably be cut substantially
├─ ELIMINATING STEPS (unnecessary ones in the composing process)
│   ├─ Productivity 20–50% below the industry standard in typesetting and hand composition
│   ├─ Every job, simple or complex, passes through the same quality steps → likely cause
│   ├─ A local manager will run a trial: which steps can go (esp. on simple jobs); why productivity lags
│   └─ Next week: track simple jobs, test fewer checks against quality and customer reaction
│       (potential saving up to a tenth of composing cost); plus a methods study on the gap
└─ RAISING WAGES (to market levels)
    ├─ Pay trails local competitors → hard to recruit and keep compositors
    ├─ Recent departures → understaffed → late jobs and overtime far over budget
    └─ A pending union claim may force higher pay → would allow hiring and an end to overtime
```

Lessons: list → relate → conclude finds structure in a rambling memo; the introduction decides between candidate pyramids; the Question can shift during the recheck (excessive? → reducible?); the introduction should state the Key Line; headings are the Key Line ideas; the wording barely changed — clarity came from the ordering.

**Warehouse (pp. 32–33) — overstructured deductive Key Line.** Structure of Minto's example: top = buy a specific named warehouse; Key Line = (a) we judge warehouses by three criteria → (b) this building satisfies all three → (c) so buy it, with the three criteria listed beneath (a) and again beneath (b). The (c) step raises no question and, with the top stated first, is redundant. Her inductive rewrite: top = buy it because it meets our criteria; Key Line = one point per criterion met (a location criterion, a minimum-size criterion, a maximum price-per-square-foot criterion). Invented example of the same shape:

```
Before:  Hire candidate Lee
           We hire engineers on three criteria → Lee meets all three → Therefore hire Lee
After:   Hire Lee — she meets all three of our hiring criteria
         ├─ She has shipped production systems at our scale
         ├─ She passed the design interview with top marks
         └─ She is available within our start window
```

Lesson: a deductive step that raises no question signals that the inductive form will communicate more efficiently.

## Nuances and exceptions

- **You can start anywhere.** The pyramid rules let you begin from any idea and discover the others; top-down and bottom-up are the two practical routes, and top-down is generally easier, so try it first.
- **Unknowns are allowed in the top box.** Steps 1–3 explicitly allow skipping the Subject, Question or Answer; the introduction check (steps 4–6) fills or corrects them. You may record only that you are able to answer before you know the answer.
- **The Question may change during the check.** In the TTW case the first Question (are the costs excessive?) gave way to the one the Complication actually raises (can they be reduced?). Treat step 6 as a genuine revision point, not a formality.
- **Short documents need less structure.** Once the Key Line is set, a short memo can be written directly; the support emerges while writing each section.
- **Thinking order vs. writing order of the introduction.** Think S → C → Q → A; write them in whatever order suits the effect and tone (Ch. 4 covers the patterns). Don't confuse the two.
- **"Knows" means "won't question".** For the introduction, a reader "knows" a fact if a neutral observer could readily confirm it — even if the reader hasn't personally seen it.
- **Events in the body are allowed only as causal argument.** Chronology isn't banned from the body; bare narration is. Cause-and-effect sequences are ideas because they were found through analysis.
- **An inductive Key Line is a preference "given a choice", not an absolute.** Ch. 5 names the exceptions where a deductive Key Line is needed.
- **Closed-universe editing.** When restructuring another person's document, Minto deliberately withholds judgment on its truth; the reader of the rewrite may still disagree, but can now see exactly what to challenge.
- **Exhibit numbering quirk.** Exhibit 4 labels the recheck "2" and the New Question "6", while the text calls the recheck step 6. The order of operations is identical.

> **Skill note:** For an agent restructuring a user's draft, Procedure B's first move (extract and type each point) plus the closed-universe stance is a safe default: it keeps the agent from silently adding claims while reorganizing.

> **Skill note:** BLD-14's 30-second test translates for agents into: "From the opening paragraph alone, can I state the top point and every Key Line point?" This is a cheap, high-signal review check.
