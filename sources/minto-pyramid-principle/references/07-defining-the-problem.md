# Defining the Problem

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Introduction to Part Three "Logic in Problem Solving" (book pp. 119–120) and Ch. 8 "Defining the Problem" (book pp. 121–139). Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `DEF`.

## When a skill needs this
- Helping someone define a business problem before any analysis starts (consulting proposal, project plan, strategy study, final report).
- Drafting the Situation–Complication–Question introduction of a problem-solving document from a long or tangled history ("we tried X, it failed, then we tried Y...").
- Working out which question the reader actually has: what to do, whether a proposed fix is right, how to implement, which alternative, what the objective should be, or whether there is a problem at all.
- Reviewing a report or proposal whose introduction poses the wrong question, whose desired result is vague, or whose pyramid does not answer the question it raises.
- Explaining a problem orally or in writing in a few sentences.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Problem-oriented document | A long report, presentation, project plan or consulting proposal that gives, or says how you will find, the solution to a problem. |
| Four-stage process | Define the problem → structure the analysis → conduct the analysis / find the solution → form the pyramid to communicate. |
| Pre-structuring the pyramid | Running stages 1–2 so their output passes through stage 3 and drops almost directly into pyramid form. |
| Problem | The gap between the result a situation now yields (R1) and the result you want (R2). |
| Solution | What tells you how to get from R1 to R2. |
| Sequential Analysis | B. Robert Holland's (McKinsey, 1972) five-question problem-solving sequence: is there a problem, where, why, what could we do, what should we do. |
| Problem Definition Framework | Minto's layout of a problem as Situation (Starting Point/Opening Scene + Disturbing Event), R1 and R2, with a "?" or solution between R1 and R2. |
| Starting Point / Opening Scene | The structure or process, at a specific place and time, in which the problem originated; the "stage set" before anything happens. |
| Disturbing Event | What happened, or could or probably will happen, that threatens the stable Opening Scene and brings R1 to the surface. |
| External / Internal / Recently Recognized | The three kinds of Disturbing Event: change in the environment, change made by the company, or newly seen evidence that change is needed. |
| R1 (Undesired Result) | The problem the reader is trying to solve or likely to face, or an opportunity they could take. |
| R2 (Desired Result) | What the reader wants the structure/process to produce instead of R1, stated as a measurable end product. |
| Layered problem | A history with more than one round of problem → solution → new problem; labelled R1-a, R2-a, Solution-a, R1-b, and so on. |
| Read left to right and down | The conversion rule: turn the laid-out framework into S-C-Q by reading it in that order. |
| Last thing known by the reader | The most recent development the reader already knows; it always becomes the Complication. |
| Seven problem situations | The seven positions a reader can occupy relative to the solution, each with its own S-C-Q pattern (Exhibit 34). |
| Benchmarking study | Case 7: the reader knows R2 but is unsure whether they are at R1. |
| "Changes" vs "steps" | Plural noun for the Key Line: "changes" when fixing an existing system, "steps" when building a new capability. |

## Concepts

### Introduction to Part Three: why problem-solving documents need extra machinery (pp. 119–120)
- For short documents, Minto expects the S-C-Q introduction to become automatic. Combined with the question/answer dialogue and the ordering and summarizing disciplines (Ch 6–7), the writer can work out the structure of the thinking fairly easily.
- Longer documents are harder. These are reports and presentations, which usually deliver the solution to a problem, and project plans or consulting proposals, which say how the problem will be solved. Typically there has been a long data-gathering phase. Several authors may work over days or weeks, and the writer is swamped with facts, data and ideas that must be sorted before the message is even known.
- Part Three is aimed at people who write such documents: management consultants, strategic analysts, market researchers and similar roles. Minto admits the material is long and complex. She says the approaches are well tested and in daily use worldwide.
- **Three common questions** drive problem-oriented documents. Which one applies depends on what the reader knows in advance:
  - *What should we do?* The solution is not known.
  - *Should we do it?* A solution has been suggested.
  - *How should we do it? / How will you do it?* The solution is known and accepted.
- The introduction defines the problem that produced the question. The pyramid then gives the "steps" or "reasons" (occasionally a deductive argument) that came out of analysing the problem and finding a solution.
- The thinking starts long before there is anything to communicate. The ideal sequence is the **four-stage process**: define the problem → structure the analysis → conduct the analysis and find the solution → form the pyramid.
- **Key to efficient consulting reports** has two parts. (a) Pin down the problem. (b) Design the data collection and analysis so the output converts readily into a pyramid. If stages 1–2 are set up well, stage 3 flows through to stage 4 with little friction. The pyramid is effectively shaped in advance ("pre-structured").
- Why this is hard:
  - the events behind a problem are often obscure, confused or misreported;
  - huge amounts of data exist, which tempts people to "go after everything";
  - many possible "solutions" offer themselves.
- Analytical frameworks reduce the confusion. Ch 8 gives a framework for defining problems. It serves first as a prelude to analysis and later as the template for the S-C-Q introduction. Ch 9 gives frameworks for conducting the analysis and for checking that the proposed solution ideas are valid.

### Chapter opening: the problem as a gap; Sequential Analysis (pp. 121–122)
- Saying "we have a problem" usually means noticing that some activity delivers results short of what you'd like. Some situation is producing an outcome you don't want, the **Undesired Result (R1)**, e.g., sales are declining. You want another result, the **Desired Result (R2)**, e.g., sales growing. The **solution** tells you how to get from R1 to R2.
- Base diagram: `Situation | R1 ↘ ? ↙ R2`.
- Framing the gap like this kicks off **Sequential Analysis**, an efficient method credited to B. Robert Holland (McKinsey, London, 1972). It works through five questions in a fixed logical order:
  1. *Existence:* is there a problem or opportunity, now or in prospect?
  2. *Location:* where is it?
  3. *Cause:* why is it there?
  4. *Options:* what could be done?
  5. *Choice:* which of those should be done?
- Mapping:

| Questions | Stage |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Define the problem |
| 3 | Structure the analysis (find causes) |
| 4–5 | Find the solution (best way to remove the problem or seize the opportunity) |

- **Communication mapping:** the answers to questions 1–2 become the document's introduction. The answers to the remaining questions become the points in the pyramid. The chapter therefore offers a structured method for problem definition, from which a proposal's or final report's introduction can be written with little extra effort.

### Problem-Definition Framework (p. 122)
- The R1/R2 gap does not appear out of nowhere. It comes from an existing situation and develops in response to particular circumstances, which may be simple or a complex web of cause and effect.
- Understanding how those circumstances developed is essential for two things: pinpointing what the gap actually is, and grasping why it matters.

### Laying out the Elements (pp. 122–124)
- **Simplified example.** A company has sold an in-demand product (industrial real estate, say) the same way for 30 years. Salesmen list prospects, write a script, then deliver it. Sales have grown about 10% a year. Going into the final quarter this year, projections show sales down 10% instead of up 10%. The company is shocked and wants to act quickly.
- **Exhibit 32 (p. 123) layout:**
  - *Situation.* Starting Point/Opening Scene = the selling process, drawn as three boxes in sequence: list the prospects → write the script → deliver the presentation. Disturbing Event = quarterly sales projected to be down 10%.
  - *R1* = threat to annual growth.
  - *R2* = continue 10% annual growth.
  - *The question between them* = how can we ensure continued growth? Candidate answers: expand the list, revitalize the script, offer the delivery on videotape.
- The Opening Scene contains an existing structure or process (here, the standard selling approach). That process yields, or is expected to yield, R2. Something happens or some action is taken (here, the sales projection is calculated), and it reveals a likely R1.
- The shortfall of actual against expected results constitutes the problem. Solving it means finding why the shortfall exists and what steps would close it. **Look for the causes among the Opening Scene's activities; that is usually where they sit.**
- The framework asks three questions:
  - *Situation* (Starting Point/Opening Scene + Disturbing Event): what is happening?
  - *R1:* which part of that is unwelcome?
  - *R2:* what result would be preferred?
- With those three answered, the definition is complete enough to name the Question and begin the search for a Solution. **Solutions typically consist of altering that Opening Scene structure or process.** In the example, the likely culprits are an out-of-date prospect list, a weak script, a poorly delivered pitch, or some combination.
- From here you structure the analysis (Ch 9). Diagnostic frameworks and logic trees break each area down completely to find the causes. The solution steps come from those frameworks; here they would probably be fixing the list, the script and/or the delivery.

### Converting to an Introduction (pp. 124–126)
- When it is time to write, convert the definition into an introduction. **Read the layout from left to right and then downward. The last thing the reader already knows always becomes the Complication that triggers the Question.**
- **Sales example as S-C-Q:**
  - S = we have used a tried-and-true approach for 30 years and grown 10% a year (Starting Point).
  - C = quarterly projections now show sales down 10% instead of up 10%, which bodes ill for year-end figures (Disturbing Event, R1, R2).
  - Q = how can we ensure continued growth?
  - Pyramid: "Eliminate inadequacies in execution" over three boxes: expand the list / revitalize the script / offer the delivery on videotape.
- **The simplest generic form** asks how to move from R1 to R2. S = an established process the reader is happy with (Situation). C = its output now falls short (R1, R2). Q = what now?
- **Most problems have a longer history:**
  - *Solution already found.* S = a problem arose (Situation, R1, R2). C = a fix has been devised. Q = is the fix correct? *or* how should it be put into effect? **Here the newly devised fix is itself the Complication.**
  - *Solution found but failing.* S = a problem arose and a fix was devised (Situation, R1, R2, Solution). C = the fix isn't delivering (R1-b). Q = what now? In the diagram, a dashed line under "Solution" leads to "Solution did not work" and a new R1/R2 "V" with "?".
  - *Triple layer.* The second solution fails as well (see the supermarket example below and Exhibit 33).
- **Exhibit 33 (p. 126), triple-layer problem.**
  - Background: a large packaged-foods maker, in the early days of supermarkets, wants to test new products on supermarket shelves for about a week before full launch, despite extensive prior testing. The supermarkets refuse, then accept a modest fee. Supermarkets consolidate into chains and the fee climbs to $20,000 a week. A committee can agree only on refusing to pay. The supermarkets then refuse to allow week-long test marketing at all.
  - Opening Scene: Manufacturer / New Product / Supermarket.
  - Disturbing Event: the wish for more thorough testing of new products before final rollout.
  - Layer a: R1 = supermarkets will not allow; R2 = test *in situ*; solution = pay fee.
  - Layer b: R1 = fee at $20,000; R2 = test *in situ* at a reasonable fee; solution = refuse to pay.
  - Layer c: R1 = supermarkets will not allow; "?"
- Because the history is laid out in order, it compresses into a few sentences, e.g., in a speech to the industry:
  - **Situation** carries everything the audience already knows: industry has paid fees to overcome reluctance; fee has risen to $20,000; we refused to pay. This covers the original scene plus both completed layers, a and b.
  - **Complication** = they now also refuse test marketing (R1-c).
  - **Question** = how should we respond?

### The general process (p. 127)
- Use the framework as the first step in problem solving *and* as the first step in building the pyramid that communicates the solution.
- It is also very useful for finding and fixing problems in documents someone else asks you to review.
- Either way, follow this process:
  1. Map out the problem's components.
  2. Establish the reader's position on a solution: none yet, one proposed, or one adopted?
  3. Settle which question the document must answer.
  4. Confirm that the S-C-Q mirrors the problem definition.
  5. Confirm that the top of the pyramid actually answers that question.

### Lay Out the Problem (p. 127)
- Four elements must be specified before the problem is defined well enough to look for a solution: Starting Point/Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, R2.
- Taken together they narrate how the problem unfolded, almost like a play, and treating them theatrically is a useful aid.

### The Starting Point/Opening Scene (pp. 127–128)
- **Theatre metaphor.** The curtain rises on a set showing a specific place at a specific moment; that is the Opening Scene. Then something happens that starts the action; that is the Disturbing Event.
- In problem definition, the curtain rises on the part of your (or the client's) company or industry where the problem began, at a specific point in space and time. It is usually a **structure or process you can easily visualize**:

| Typical Opening Scene structures | Typical Opening Scene processes |
|---|---|
| Organization charts | Sales or marketing activities |
| Computer configurations | Information systems |
| Plant/office locations | Administrative processes |
| Geographical markets | Distribution systems |
| | Manufacturing processes |

- **Pitch:** sketch the layout of the area for a reader with roughly the general knowledge of a typical *Fortune* or *Business Week* reader. Another test: picture yourself recounting the situation to an acquaintance. What would they have to "see" in order to follow?
- Examples (p. 128):
  - "A company that distributed household goods nationally from three warehouses" → the friend pictures three warehouse boxes feeding "household goods."
  - "A company made of several independently run businesses, each doing work where new image-processing technology may apply" → the friend pictures an org chart (parent box over five unit boxes) with an "image processing" bubble beside it.
- **At this point a minimal picture and a terse description are enough.** Fuller prose belongs to the later job of drafting the introduction.

### The Disturbing Event (p. 129)
- Nobody examines the structure or process until something upsets its normal working. The Disturbing Event is that upset. It may already have occurred, or it may be a possible or probable future development, soon or later. It destabilizes the otherwise steady Opening Scene and thereby sets off R1. In the image-processing example, the new technology's arrival plays that role.
- **Three kinds:**
  - **External.** A change that starts elsewhere in the environment the structure/process operates in: a new competitor, a switch to a new technology, a shift in government or customer policy.
  - **Internal.** A change the company starts itself: adding a business process, installing a new computer system, entering a new market, redirecting the product line.
  - **Recently Recognized.** Fresh signs that change is plainly or probably required: lagging performance in a product or process, below-par operating results, market research suggesting customer attitudes may be shifting.
- **Exception:** sometimes, especially when writing a consulting proposal, you lack the information to say exactly what made the reader recognize the problem. You should still be able to say what the reader is unhappy with in the structure/process. In that case **do not invent a Disturbing Event; go straight to R1.**

### R1 (Undesired Result) (pp. 129–130)
- R1 names what the reader is wrestling with now or will probably confront, *or an opportunity open to them*. Typically the Disturbing Event (external, internal or recently recognized) is what exposes it.
- In consulting, R1 is typically the stated reason for the engagement, although the client may not see its underlying causes.
- The Disturbing Event may reveal an opportunity that was previously unseen or unavailable. More often it will have done one of these:
  - harmed the company's processes or structures;
  - disrupted performance in some area;
  - forced, or ought to have forced, a rethink of products, processes or the business itself;
  - undermined, or ought to have undermined, core beliefs about the customer base, the market, rivals, the firm's distinctive strengths, its processes or its technology.
- There may be **more than one R1**.
- **State R1 as briefly as possible** in the diagram. Examples: can no longer serve the market; losing share; sales falling; margins declining; financial performance eroding; a forecast market opportunity can't be realized.

### R2 (Desired Result) (pp. 130–131)
- R2 names the output the reader would like in R1's place. If R1 is an opportunity, R2 is the ability to capture it.
- **Make R2 concrete and, where possible, numeric. The test is whether you could tell that it had been reached.** Without a defined end product there is no basis for picking among the solutions you will come up with.
- Phrase R2 in end-product terms that carry either a specific number or a specific end state. Examples: meet year-end growth goals; cut time to market by a third; allow supermarket testing at a reasonable cost; revise the system so it works properly; have enough capacity for projected demand.
- If R2 cannot be stated as a specific end product, or cannot be stated at all, write the general state you want to reach once the problem is solved. **Then make determining the specific R2 the first step of the problem solving.**
- **Purpose of the layout:** a crude but identifiable scaffold. It exposes gaps in your understanding and gives you a frame to wrap the introduction's words around.
- **The definitions will change.** Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1 and R2 may all be revised during problem solving. For example, data gathering may sharpen your sense of how big the external changes are, letting you restate R1 and R2. The *relationships* between the parts always hold.

### Look for the Question (pp. 131–132)
- With the parts laid out, look for the reader's question. What they ask hinges on **how much progress they had made on the problem before your analysis began**. Do they only want a route from R1 to R2? Or have they already settled on a route, which would make their question different?
- **A big error** is never pinning down, in your own mind, whether the reader has already done something about the problem. Once you see what action was taken and how it shifts the document's question, both the introduction and the downstream logic become far easier to write.
- **The seven problem situations** (where the reader stands relative to a solution):

| # | Frequency | Reader's position |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Most common | Has no route from R1 to R2 yet. |
| 2 | Most common | Has a route in mind but doubts it. |
| 3 | Most common | Is confident of the route but unsure how to carry it out. |
| 4 | Variation | Chose a route, carried it out, and it failed. |
| 5 | Variation | Holds several candidate routes and can't choose. |
| 6 | Possible, uncommon | Sees R1 but can't define R2 precisely enough to search for a solution. |
| 7 | Possible, uncommon | Has R2 in view but doesn't know whether they are actually at R1 (typical benchmarking study). |

- **Exhibit 34 (p. 132): mapping each situation to the introduction**

| # | Diagram | S | C | Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | R1 ↘ ? ↙ R2 | Situation | R1, R2 | How do we get from R1 to R2? |
| 2 / 3 | R1 ↘ Solution ↙ R2 | Situation, R1, R2 | Solution | Is this fix correct? *or* How do we put it in place? |
| 4 | R1 ↘ Solution ↙ R2, dashed line, "Solution did not work" ↘ ? ↙ | Situation, R1, R2, Solution | Solution did not work | What should we do? |
| 5 | R1 ↘ Alternatives A, B, C ↙ R2 | Situation, R1, R2 | We have alternative ways to solve the problem | Which is the best alternative? |
| 6 | R1 ↘ ? ↙ "?" (R2 unknown) | Situation, R1 | We know we must change but not what to aim for or how to get there | What should our objectives and strategy be? |
| 7 | "?" (R1 unknown) ↘ ? ↙ R2 | Situation, R2 | Not sure whether we are at R1 | Do we have a problem, and if so how should we respond? |

### Move to the Introduction (pp. 133–137)
- The framework mostly lays the elements out in the order the introduction uses them. Move left to right and down; the last thing the reader knows is always the Complication.
- The seven standard cases below are kept abstract to show the bare structure. Full-text versions of each introduction are in Appendix B (see `03b-introduction-patterns.md`).

**Case 1: What should we do? (p. 133)**
- S = we have approach X to selling to markets now. C = we expect much higher growth, face other problems, and fear X won't keep working. Q = how should we change?
- Pyramid: "Like this" → (How?) → Step One / Step Two / Step Three.
- Of all the cases this is the **easiest to analyse and to write**. Its S is invariably the current state of affairs, and its C is invariably "we're at R1 but want R2".
- The same shape serves for upgrading a system already in use. S = this is how the system runs now; C = it falls short of its purpose; Q = what would make it perform? **Key Line plural noun: "changes."**
- A slightly different shape serves for building something new. S = this is the task we need to carry out; C = we lack the means; Q = how do we acquire the capability? **Key Line plural noun: "steps."**

**Case 2 (p. 134); book heading: "Should we do what we are thinking of doing?"**
- Pattern: S = an approach spreading through the industry could hurt us. C = if it does, we must adapt. Q = should we start preparing?
- Pyramid: "Yes" → (How?) → Change A / Change B / Change C.
- Variations:
  - S = a problem or situation exists; C = an action is planned; Q = is that action correct?
  - S = action X is planned; C = X only makes sense if condition Y holds; Q = does Y hold?

**Case 3 (p. 134); book heading: "How should we do what we want to do?"**
- S = a city faces a problem. C = the remedy has been chosen. Q = how is it to be carried out?
- Pyramid: "Follow this process" → (How?) → Step One / Two / Three.
- Also used to explain how something *was* done:
  - S = there was a problem; C = doing X fixed it; Q = how was X done?
  - S = there is (or was) a goal; C = a system or process to reach it is being put in; Q = how does it operate?

**Case 4 (p. 135); book heading: "Our solution hasn't worked, what should we do?"**
- Pattern: S = a problem exists and several remedies have been tried. C = none has succeeded. Q = what now?
- Pyramid: "Try harder and be more thorough" → (How?) → Step One / Two / Three.
- Structurally this is **Case 1 again**, with the same "what should we do?" question. What distinguishes it is a history of two or three layers, which you must walk through before the question can be posed.

**Case 5: Which alternative should we choose? (p. 135)**
- S = the plan was to proceed by method X. C = method Y has been proposed as superior. Q = which should we pick?
- Pyramid: "Y is the better way" → (Why?) → Does A better / Does B better / Does C better.
- **Put alternatives in the Complication, always.** Normally they appear only if the reader already has them in mind, i.e., the reader raised them and wants them assessed.
- **Don't set up options merely to demolish them.** The anti-pattern: an opening of "there are three ways to solve this", then a Key Line of "A fails because…" → "B fails because…" → "so do C." C earns its place by solving the problem, not by being the last option standing. Appendix B covers generating and presenting alternatives in more depth.

**Case 6: What should our strategy be? (p. 136)**
- S = a minor player in a large market. C = true potential is unknown, but current performance is clearly far below it. Q = which strategy would close that distance?
- Pyramid (deductive Key Line): "Should aim to excel in niche A" → (Why?) → only a few really attractive ways to go → you are limited in how you can respond to any of them → biggest payoff from concentrating on niche A. The "How?" steps hang under the final box.
- When it arises: the client sees that a problem or opportunity demands action. But the territory is unfamiliar, or what the client knows is too thin, to define objectives or plan steps. Example: an industry in technological and market upheaval, where the client wants to use the churn to move out of stagnant segments into growing ones but can't yet tell which segments those are.
- The consultant's work:
  - analyse the industry and identify its key factors for success;
  - assess the client's strengths against them;
  - judge how effectively and profitably the client could compete with those strengths;
  - work out the wisest strategy.
- The document's top point states that strategy. The Key Line can list how to reach it, or (as in the example) argue for it deductively, with the how-to steps hanging under the final box.

**Case 7: Do we have a problem? (pp. 136–137)**
- S = major changes are happening as new market groupings emerge. C = the belief is that these changes bode ill for companies in this industry. Q = is that belief justified?
- Pyramid: "No, the changes will be great for the industry" → (Why?) → will allow A to emerge / will encourage B to develop / will prevent C from happening.
- This example reflects worries about a changing industry. The typical use, though, is a **benchmarking** study, where a client compares itself with rivals or with firms in other sectors that carry out the same activities.

### Real-Life Example (pp. 137–138)
- **The problem.** A retail distributor of household goods has three distribution centres (Worcester, Evansville, Las Vegas) plus rented space from a firm called DMSI. The warehouses were meant to serve 490 stores but sometimes struggle to serve the current 438, even with the rented space. With 4–5% annual growth and 198 new stores planned, the company expects to run out of capacity within two years.
- **Known options:** expand one or more existing warehouses, build a fourth or fifth, upgrade material handling, or keep relying on third parties. Each has a different effect on ROI.
- **What the company wants:** the strategy with the lowest capital outlay and operating cost that keeps current processing speeds and the same full-line strategy.
- **Exhibit 35 (p. 137), "Structure the problem":**
  - *Opening Scene:* boxes for Worcester, Evansville, Las Vegas and rented space; simple full-line distribution strategy; can in theory serve 490 stores but in fact needs DMSI to serve 438.
  - *Disturbing Event:* volume growing 4–5% a year; 198 more stores expected by end of next year.
  - *Layer a:* R1 = will run out of capacity in 2 years; R2 = have sufficient capacity to cope; options = expand present warehouses / build fourth or fifth / upgrade material handling / continue relying on third parties.
  - *Layer b (dashed line):* R1 = differing impacts on ROI; R2 = approach that ensures lowest capital outlay, lowest operating cost, same processing speeds, same full-line strategy; "?".
- This calls for **a variation on Case 5**: S = a problem exists; C = several possible fixes are on the table; Q = which one?
- **Exhibit 36 (p. 138), "Move from the problem to the pyramid":**
  - S: three centres set up for 490 stores can do only 438, and only with rented space; growth of 4–5% a year plus 198 new stores will exhaust capacity; you want to add enough capacity in time. It ends with: "But" a variety of ways exist, from expanding centres to building a fourth or fifth, and combinations.
  - C: the ROI impact differs by activity and timing; you want the approach with lowest capital outlay and operating cost that keeps processing speeds and the full-line strategy.
  - Q: what should the distribution strategy be?
  - Pyramid: "Add capacity incrementally, to avoid building a fourth warehouse as long as possible" → Modify Worcester and Evansville in 1997 / Implement "fast cycle" material-handling techniques / Continue selective third-party relationships / Expand Las Vegas in 2000 / Construct a new centre in Georgia or the Carolinas for start-up in 2002.

### Closing (p. 139)
- Minto acknowledges the framework is hard to take in on first reading. She still calls it extremely useful whenever you must explain a problem, orally or in writing, and an excellent guide to the introduction of a document recommending a solution.
- Between defining the problem and finding the solution comes the analysis itself: identifying the problem's causes and assessing possible courses of action. Here too the framework helps, by guiding you efficiently to the analyses needed (Ch 9, `08-structuring-the-analysis.md`).

## Rules
- **DEF-1**: For any long problem-oriented document, work in four stages (define the problem → structure the analysis → conduct it → form the pyramid), and design stages 1–2 so they pre-structure the pyramid. *Why:* it avoids drowning in data and makes the final write-up a translation, not a fresh struggle. *Check:* before data gathering or drafting, does a written problem definition and an analysis structure exist? (p. 120)
- **DEF-2**: Define a problem as a gap between an Undesired Result (R1) and a Desired Result (R2) arising from a specific Situation, not as a topic or area of concern. *Why:* a gap tells you what the solution must achieve; a topic does not. *Check:* can you state "the situation now yields R1; the reader wants R2"? (p. 121)
- **DEF-3**: Lay out all four elements (Starting Point/Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, R2) before looking for a solution. *Why:* the gap only makes sense in light of the history that produced it. *Check:* a framework layout exists with each element filled or explicitly marked unknown. (pp. 122, 127)
- **DEF-4**: Describe the Opening Scene as a concrete, easily visualized structure or process at a specific time and place, pitched at a general business reader (what a friend would need to "see"). *Why:* the reader must picture the arena before the problem makes sense, and the causes will be found in that arena. *Check:* can the Opening Scene be drawn as a few boxes (org chart, locations, process flow)? (pp. 127–128)
- **DEF-5**: Keep the Opening Scene sketch simple and its description short during definition; expand the prose only when writing the introduction. *Why:* the layout is scaffolding, not text. *Check:* the definition-stage scene is a sketch plus one or two lines. (p. 128)
- **DEF-6**: Identify the Disturbing Event as the actual or potential change that threatens the stable situation and surfaces R1, and classify it as external, internal or recently recognized. *Why:* it explains why the problem matters *now*. *Check:* is there a named event (or prospective event) linking the Opening Scene to R1? (p. 129)
- **DEF-7**: If you don't know what triggered recognition of the problem (common in proposals), do not invent a Disturbing Event; go directly to R1, which you can always identify as what the reader dislikes about the structure/process. *Why:* a made-up trigger misstates the history. *Check:* is any Disturbing Event supported by information actually received? (p. 129)
- **DEF-8**: State R1 as briefly as possible; list several R1s if the disturbance produced several. *Why:* brevity keeps the gap visible. *Check:* each R1 is a short phrase naming an undesired result. (p. 130)
- **DEF-9**: State R2 as specifically and quantifiably as possible, in end-product terms (a number or a definite end state). *Why:* without it you can't tell when the problem is solved or choose among candidate solutions. *Check:* could two people independently judge whether R2 has been met? (p. 130)
- **DEF-10**: If R2 can't be stated specifically (or at all), write the general desired state and make "determine the specific R2" the first step of the problem solving. *Why:* every later choice depends on R2. *Check:* a vague R2 is paired with an explicit first task to pin it down. (p. 130)
- **DEF-11**: Treat the definition as revisable scaffolding; update the elements as data arrives but keep the relationships between them. *Why:* understanding of external changes sharpens as you gather data. *Check:* after analysis, do the intro's R1/R2 match the latest understanding? (pp. 130–131)
- **DEF-12**: Look for the causes of the gap, and thus the solution, in the structure or activities of the Opening Scene. *Why:* the solution usually comes from changing what goes on in that structure/process. *Check:* does each candidate cause or remedy refer to an element of the Opening Scene? (p. 123)
- **DEF-13**: Establish explicitly whether the reader has already acted on the problem (suggested, chosen, implemented or abandoned a solution) before choosing the question. *Why:* Minto calls skipping this a big error; prior action changes the question. *Check:* the definition records the reader's position relative to a solution. (p. 131)
- **DEF-14**: Classify the reader into one of the seven problem situations and use the matching S-C-Q pattern (Exhibit 34). *Why:* each position produces a different question and therefore a different pyramid. *Check:* name the case number, and confirm the Q matches it. (pp. 131–132)
- **DEF-15**: Convert the framework to S-C-Q by reading it left to right and down. The Complication is the most recent development the reader already knows. *Why:* this keeps the introduction a story the reader agrees with, ending at the point that raises their question. *Check:* is the Complication the most recent development the reader knows of, and does it directly prompt the Q? (pp. 124, 126, 133)
- **DEF-16**: In a layered history, fold all earlier rounds (R1-a, R2-a, Solution-a, R1-b…) into the Situation; only the latest development is the Complication. *Why:* the earlier rounds are known background; the latest turn creates the question. *Check:* the C contains one new development; earlier failures sit in S. (pp. 124–126)
- **DEF-17**: Place alternatives in the Complication, and raise them only if the reader already knows them (the reader identified them and wants them weighed). *Why:* introducing unrequested options adds noise and invites a knock-down structure. *Check:* is every alternative mentioned traceable to the reader? (p. 135)
- **DEF-18**: Never justify a recommendation by knocking down rejected alternatives ("A fails, B fails, so C"); justify it by showing it solves the problem. *Why:* the reason to do C is that it closes the gap, not that others don't. *Check:* do the Key Line points argue *for* the chosen option against R2? (p. 135)
- **DEF-19**: Match the Key Line's plural noun to the question: "changes" when fixing a system already running, "steps" when creating a new capability. *Why:* the noun reflects whether you modify an existing structure or build one. *Check:* the top point's implied plural noun fits the S-C-Q pattern. (p. 133)
- **DEF-20**: Use the answers to Sequential Analysis questions 1–2 (is there a problem; where) for the introduction, and the answers to 3–5 (why; what could; what should) for the pyramid's points. *Why:* this is the hand-off from problem solving to communication. *Check:* the intro contains no causal analysis or recommendations; the pyramid does. (p. 122)
- **DEF-21**: When reviewing a document, confirm that the introduction mirrors the problem definition and that the pyramid's top answers the question the introduction raises. *Why:* the framework works as a diagnostic tool for others' documents too. *Check:* rebuild the framework from the draft; does its Q match the draft's top point? (p. 127)
- **DEF-22**: For a strategy question (Case 6), put the strategy itself at the top; the Key Line either gives steps to achieve it or a deductive argument for it, with the steps under the final box. *Why:* the reader lacks both the objective and the route, so the strategy must be stated and justified. *Check:* the top point is a strategy statement, not a list of analyses. (p. 136)

## Procedures

### P1. Define the problem (lay out the framework)
1. **Name the Opening Scene.** Identify the structure or process (org chart, locations, systems, sales/distribution/manufacturing process) where the problem began. Sketch it as a few boxes. Test it: would a general business reader, or a friend hearing the story, be able to "see" it? Keep it to a sketch plus a line or two. (DEF-4, DEF-5)
2. **Find the Disturbing Event.** Ask what happened, or may happen, to upset the stable scene. Classify it: external (competitor, technology, policy shift), internal (new process, system, market, product redirection) or recently recognized (lagging results, research signals).
   - *Decision:* if you don't know what triggered recognition, skip this step; do not invent one. (DEF-6, DEF-7)
3. **State R1.** Write the undesired result(s), or the opportunity, in as few words as possible. Note more than one if needed. (DEF-8)
4. **State R2.** Write the desired end product with a number or definite end state.
   - *Decision:* if you cannot, write the general desired state and record "first step: determine specific R2." (DEF-9, DEF-10)
5. **Trace the history below.** If a solution has already been tried or proposed, add it under the R1/R2 "V". If it failed or created a new problem, draw a dashed line and lay out the next layer (R1-b, R2-b, Solution-b…). Repeat until you reach the current open "?". (DEF-16)
6. **Locate likely cause areas.** List the elements or activities of the Opening Scene whose malfunction could produce R1 (e.g., list / script / delivery). These seed the diagnostic frameworks in `08-structuring-the-analysis.md`. (DEF-12)
7. **Mark the definition provisional.** Revisit after data gathering; keep the relationships, update the wording. (DEF-11)

### P2. Determine the reader's question
1. Ask: has the reader taken any action toward a solution? (DEF-13)
2. Walk the decision points:
   - No solution in mind, R1 and R2 both known → **Case 1** (How do we get from R1 to R2 / What should we do?).
   - A solution proposed but not confirmed → **Case 2** (Is the proposed fix correct? / Should we go ahead? / Does Y hold?).
   - A solution decided → **Case 3** (How do we implement it? / How did you do X? / How does it work?).
   - A solution implemented and failed (possibly more than once) → **Case 4** (What should we do?, after tracing the layers).
   - Several candidate solutions the reader has identified → **Case 5** (Which is best?).
   - R1 known but R2 not articulable → **Case 6** (What should our objectives and strategy be?).
   - R2 known, unsure whether at R1 → **Case 7** (Do we have a problem, and if so how should we respond?).
3. Write the Q in the reader's terms, using the Exhibit 34 row. (DEF-14)

### P3. Convert the framework to an introduction
1. Read the layout left to right and down: Opening Scene → Disturbing Event → R1/R2 → each solution layer.
2. Find the **last thing the reader already knows**. That is the Complication. (DEF-15)
3. Everything before it, including earlier failed solutions, goes into the Situation, written as familiar background. (DEF-16)
4. If alternatives are involved, check the reader identified them; put them in the Complication. (DEF-17)
5. Write the Question from P2.
6. Now expand the prose. The Opening Scene sketch becomes one or two sentences of Situation. (DEF-5)
7. Draft the top point as the answer to Q. Pick the Key Line's plural noun ("changes", "steps", "reasons") to fit the case. (DEF-19)
8. *Decision (Case 5):* make sure the Key Line argues why the chosen option meets R2, not why the others fail. (DEF-18)

### P4. Review someone else's problem-solving document (p. 127)
1. Reconstruct the four elements from the draft (Opening Scene, Disturbing Event, R1, R2, plus any solution layers).
2. Identify where the reader stands relative to a solution.
3. Determine what the question *should* be.
4. Compare with the draft: does the introduction reflect the reconstructed definition (right S, last-known C, right Q)?
5. Check that the pyramid's top point answers that Q. Report mismatches with the rule IDs above. (DEF-21)

### P5. End-to-end flow (Part Three)
1. Define the problem (P1–P2). The answers to "Is there a problem? Where?" become the introduction.
2. Structure the analysis from the Opening Scene: diagnostic frameworks and yes/no questions (`08-structuring-the-analysis.md`).
3. Conduct the analysis and find the solution: logic trees of possible actions, evaluated against R2.
4. Form the pyramid (P3 for the introduction; the frameworks and trees supply the Key Line). (DEF-1, DEF-20)

## Diagnostics
| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Analysts are gathering "everything" before anyone can state the problem | No problem definition; stages 1–2 skipped | DEF-1, DEF-2, DEF-3 |
| Intro asks "What should we do?" but the reader has already chosen a solution and wants implementation help (or vice versa) | Reader's position relative to a solution not established | DEF-13, DEF-14 |
| Recommendations can't be ranked; "success" is undefined | R2 vague ("improve performance") | DEF-9, DEF-10 |
| Introduction narrates each failed attempt as a separate complication | Layered history not folded into the Situation | DEF-15, DEF-16 |
| Key Line reads "A is no good… B is no good… therefore C" | Alternatives raised to be knocked down | DEF-17, DEF-18 |
| Options appear that the reader never mentioned | Unrequested alternatives | DEF-17 |
| Situation section is long and data-heavy | Opening Scene not kept to a visualizable sketch | DEF-4, DEF-5 |
| Proposal cites a dramatic trigger the writer can't substantiate | Manufactured Disturbing Event | DEF-7 |
| Solutions don't touch the process or structure where the problem arose | Causes not sought in the Opening Scene | DEF-12 |
| Top point doesn't answer the question the introduction poses | Intro/pyramid mismatch | DEF-21, DEF-14 |
| Introduction already contains causes or recommendations | Q3–5 material placed in the introduction | DEF-20 |
| "Steps" used for upgrading an existing system (or "changes" for a new capability) | Key Line noun doesn't match the situation | DEF-19 |
| Strategy document lists analyses at the top instead of a strategy | Case 6 mishandled | DEF-22 |

## Templates and patterns

**Problem Definition Framework (fill-in layout)**
```
SITUATION                                   R1                         R2
Starting Point / Opening Scene              <undesired result(s),      <desired end product:
  [box]──[box]──[box]   (structure/process)  as short as possible>       number or end state>
  <1–2 line description>                          \                    /
Disturbing Event                                   \                  /
  <external | internal | recently recognized>        <? | Solution-a>
                                            - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
                                            R1-b <solution failed / new issue>   R2-b <refined target>
                                                          \                  /
                                                               <? | Solution-b> ...
```

**Conversion rule**
```
Read left → right, then down.
Situation    = everything up to (not including) the last thing the reader knows
Complication = the last thing the reader knows
Question     = what that complication makes the reader ask (see seven cases)
```

**The seven S-C-Q patterns (Exhibit 34)**
```
1  S: Situation               C: R1, R2                          Q: How do we get from R1 to R2?
2  S: Situation, R1, R2       C: Solution                        Q: Is it the right solution?
3  S: Situation, R1, R2       C: Solution                        Q: How do we implement it?
4  S: Situation, R1, R2, Sol. C: Solution did not work           Q: What should we do?
5  S: Situation, R1, R2       C: Alternative ways exist          Q: Which is best?
6  S: Situation, R1           C: Must change; aim & route unclear Q: What should our objectives & strategy be?
7  S: Situation, R2           C: Unsure whether we are at R1     Q: Do we have a problem? If so, how respond?
```

**Pyramid skeletons per case (pp. 133–137)**
```
1 What should we do?        "Like this"                --How?--> Step 1 | Step 2 | Step 3
2 Should we do it?          "Yes"                      --How?--> Change A | Change B | Change C
3 How do we implement?      "Follow this process"      --How?--> Step 1 | Step 2 | Step 3
4 Solution failed           "Try harder, be thorough"  --How?--> Step 1 | Step 2 | Step 3
5 Which alternative?        "Y is the better way"      --Why?--> Does A better | Does B better | Does C better
6 What strategy?            "Aim to excel in niche A"  --Why?--> [few attractive ways] → [limited response] → [biggest bang in A]
                                                                                              └─How?─> steps
7 Do we have a problem?     "No, changes will be great"--Why?--> Permit A | Encourage B | Prevent C
```

**Variant S-C-Q frames**
```
Fix existing system:   S Here's how it works today   C It doesn't do what it should   Q How do we make it?   (Key Line: changes)
Build new capability:  S Activity we must perform     C We can't perform it           Q How do we create it?  (Key Line: steps)
Check a plan:          S Situation/problem            C We plan action X              Q Is it the right action?
Conditional plan:      S We plan X                    C Only if Y holds               Q Is Y the case?
Explain past action:   S We had a problem             C We solved it by X             Q How did you do X?
Explain a system:      S We have an objective         C Installing system/process     Q How does it work?
```

**Layered-history introduction frame**
```
Situation:    As you know, <original scene>; to deal with <R1-a> we <Solution-a>;
              then <R1-b>, so we <Solution-b>.            (S, R1-a, R2-a, Sol-a, R1-b, R2-b, Sol-b)
Complication: Now <R1-c>.                                 (latest development only)
Question:     How should we respond?
```

**R2 phrasing patterns**
```
<Meet | Reach> <metric> <target> by <date>
<Reduce | Increase> <metric> by <amount/fraction>
<Permit | Enable> <capability> at <constraint>
<System> functions properly / has capacity for <projected demand>
```

## Worked examples
**Exhibit 32 (pp. 122–124): sales projected down 10% after 30 years of 10% growth.**
- Before: a shock, with the company wanting quick action.
- Framework: Opening Scene = list prospects → write script → deliver presentation. Disturbing Event = quarterly projection down 10%. R1 = threat to annual growth. R2 = continue 10% growth. Q = how to ensure continued growth?
- After:
  ```
  S: tried-and-true approach, 30 yrs, +10%/yr   C: projection −10%, year-end at risk   Q: How ensure continued growth?
  Eliminate inadequacies in execution
    ├─ Expand the list
    ├─ Revitalize the script
    └─ Offer the delivery on videotape
  ```
- Lesson: the remedies map one-to-one onto the activities of the Opening Scene.

**Generic layered forms (pp. 124–125).**
- "We have a process we like / it isn't giving what we want / what should we do?"
- → "We had a problem / we came up with a solution / is it right, or how do we implement it?"
- → "We had a problem and a solution / the solution isn't working / what should we do?"
- Lesson: each prior solution moves into the Situation; the newest fact is the Complication.

**Exhibit 33 (pp. 125–126): food manufacturer vs supermarkets over in-store test marketing.**
- Before: a three-round history (fee demanded → fee became $20,000 → refusal to pay → test marketing barred).
- After:
  ```
  S: we've paid supermarkets a fee to test in situ; it has risen to $20,000/week; we refused to pay
     (scene + every element of layers a and b)
  C: they now refuse to let us test-market at all (R1-c)
  Q: How should we respond?
  ```
- Lesson: laying out the layers lets a complex history fit into a few introductory sentences.

**Opening Scene sketches (p. 128).**
- "Distributes household goods from three warehouses" → three boxes feeding a goods bubble.
- "Independently run businesses where image processing may apply" → org chart plus an "image processing" bubble. The technology's arrival is the Disturbing Event (p. 129).
- Lesson: the opening line should create a simple picture.

**Seven standard cases (pp. 133–137).** See the skeletons under Templates.
- Case 4 shows a failed solution leading to "try harder and be more thorough."
- Case 5 shows "Y is the better way," supported by what each option does better, never by knocking options down.
- Case 6 shows a deductive Key Line supporting a niche strategy.
- Case 7 is a benchmarking-style "No, the changes will be good for us."
- Lesson: the reader's position fixes the question, and the question fixes the pyramid's top point and Key Line logic (How? vs Why?).

**Exhibits 35–36 (pp. 137–138): household-goods distributor running out of warehouse capacity.**
- Before: three centres plus rented space; growth and new stores will exhaust capacity in about two years; four known options with different ROI.
- Framework: two layers, capacity then ROI criteria; Case 5 variation.
- After:
  ```
  S: 3 centres built for 490 stores serve only 438, using rented space; growth + 198 new stores → out of capacity;
     want capacity in time. But many ways exist (expand, build 4th/5th, combinations).
  C: ROI impact differs by activity & timing; want lowest capital & operating cost, same speeds, same full-line strategy
  Q: What should the distribution strategy be?
  Add capacity incrementally, to avoid building a fourth warehouse as long as possible
    ├─ Modify Worcester and Evansville in 1997
    ├─ Implement "fast cycle" material-handling techniques
    ├─ Continue selective third-party relationships
    ├─ Expand Las Vegas in 2000
    └─ Construct new centre in Georgia or the Carolinas for 2002 start-up
  ```
- Lesson: the second layer's R2 (cost and service constraints) supplies the criteria the recommendation must meet; the steps form a time-ordered implementation group.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **Scope.** The framework is meant for long, problem-oriented documents. For short documents, Minto expects the plain S-C-Q plus question/answer dialogue to suffice (p. 119).
- **R1 can be an opportunity.** In that case R2 is being able to exploit it (pp. 129–130).
- **The Disturbing Event can be prospective.** Something that could or probably will happen counts (p. 129). It may be skipped when unknown (p. 129).
- **Definitions are provisional.** Expect to restate R1/R2 as data arrives; only the relationships among elements are fixed (pp. 130–131).
- **Cases 2 and 3 share one diagram** (a solution under the V). What separates them is the reader's confidence: unsure → "Is it right?"; sure → "How do we implement?" (p. 132).
- **Case 4 is Case 1 with history.** The question is the same; the difference is the layers that must be traced first (p. 135).
- **Cases 6 and 7 are uncommon** (p. 131). Case 7 is the classic benchmarking setup (p. 137).
- **Key Line logic varies by case.** "How?" cases produce steps or changes. "Why?" cases produce reasons. Case 6 may use a deductive Key Line with the action steps under its final box (pp. 133–137).
- **Tension with Ch 5.** Case 6 uses a deductive Key Line, with the steps under the final box (p. 136). Ch 5 advises against deduction at the Key Line level (see DED-5 in `04-deduction-and-induction.md`), except when the reader can't grasp the actions without the reasoning first. Case 6, where the reader lacks both the objective and the route, is plausibly that exception, but Ch 8 doesn't say so explicitly.
- **Exhibit 36 vs the alternatives rule.** The rule says alternatives go in the Complication (p. 135). Yet the printed Exhibit 36 text ends its S block with a "But a variety of ways exist…" sentence listing the options, while C carries the ROI criteria. One reading: the "But…" sentence is effectively the start of the complication and is printed under S only for layout. Flagged as an ambiguity, not a rule change.
- **Date inconsistency in the real-life example.** The narrative puts the 198 new stores at year end and the capacity crunch two years out (p. 137). Exhibit 35 says "by end of next year". Exhibit 36 says 198 stores "by end 2002" and running out "by end 1999." Use the structure, not the dates.
- **Learning curve.** Minto warns the framework is hard to appreciate on first reading, but worth mastering because it serves both explanation and introduction-writing (p. 139).
- **Review use.** The same five-step process (lay out, locate reader, determine question, check intro, check pyramid) diagnoses other people's documents (p. 127).

> **Skill note:** A problem-definition helper can make Steps P1–P2 an interview: ask for the arena (Opening Scene), what changed, what's wrong, what "fixed" looks like as a number, and what has already been tried. The "last thing the reader knows" test is the single most useful check for introductions in problem documents.
