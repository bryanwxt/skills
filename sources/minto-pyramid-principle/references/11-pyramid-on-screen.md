# Reflecting the Pyramid on a Screen

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 11 "Reflecting the Pyramid on a Screen", book pp. 189–202. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `SCR`. The author credits most of the chapter's slide-design rules to Gene Zelazny (*Say It with Charts*; long-time Director of Visual Communications at McKinsey & Company, New York); the storyboarding approach is her own.

## When a skill needs this
- Turning a finished pyramid into a slide deck: deciding which points become text slides, which become exhibits, and in what order (storyboarding).
- Reviewing a deck for "visual recitation": slides that are lists, too wordy, captioned rather than stated, or meant to be read aloud.
- Writing chart/exhibit titles and choosing a chart form from the question the exhibit answers.
- Separating speaker script from on-screen text, including preparing a handout version.
- Checking legibility (type size vs. room distance) and slide density limits.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Visual presentation | A live talk supported by slides; a "show", not a report in slide form. |
| Visual recitation | Zelazny's term for slides full of text that the presenter reads aloud. |
| Show | The author's frame: a presentation needs a star, script, storyboard, excellent visuals, and attention to timing, pacing, suspense. |
| Text slide | A slide that uses words to show structure or emphasize key thought groups. |
| Exhibit slide | A chart, graph, table, or diagram showing relationships words alone can't make clear. |
| 90/10 ratio | Ideal mix: about 90% exhibits, 10% text slides. |
| What you say vs. what you show | Script (spoken, full, with transitions) vs. slide (stark, the point only). |
| Statement vs. caption | A slide/chart title that asserts the point ("Q3 margins fell for the first time in five years") vs. one that only names a topic ("Q3 margins"). |
| Rule of 32 | Farthest viewing distance in feet ÷ 32 = minimum letter height in inches (and the converse). |
| Putting the text slide to work | Zelazny's technique: design a text slide like an exhibit that uses words, showing relationships among the thoughts. |
| Build slide | A slide revealed in stages, piece by piece, as the speaker explains. |
| Five exhibit questions | Elements? / Amounts compared (to whole, to each other, over time)? / Change? / Distribution? / Co-relation? |
| Storyboard | A sheet turned sideways and divided into blank-slide frames, used to plan each slide's point and text-vs-graphic form before drawing. |

## Concepts

### Chapter opening: a presentation is a show (pp. 189–191)
- Given the choice, most people would rather present their pyramid orally than in prose. They tend to assume a visual presentation is just a report in slide form: turn the pyramid into tidy text slides, add a few exhibits, stand up and explain. The author says it isn't that easy.
- **Why it's harder:** a live audience, often uncomfortably seated and wishing it were elsewhere, reacts unpredictably and is open to every distraction. Your job therefore includes anticipating its reactions, holding its attention, and making it eager for your message; in short, entertaining it. That calls for artistry of the kind any entertainer needs.
- **A "Show" needs:** a leading performer, a script and a storyboard, first-rate visuals, and attention to intangibles such as timing, pacing, and suspense. These skills go well beyond "a report in slide form."
- **The typical bad slide (p. 190):** a "GUIDING PRINCIPLES" slide with a framing sentence and seven long numbered principles about a health-care supply chain redesign (customer satisfaction at acceptable cost; future orientation; product uniqueness; accountability; roles placed where most effective; consolidating common activities; keeping differentiated activities local). Faults named:
  - It is a **list**, not a set of related ideas summarized with an insight.
  - **Seven items is too many for anything.**
  - With 50–60 such slides, the presenter reads every word aloud and bores the audience to death, or worse, uses different words from those on screen and causes mass confusion.
- Zelazny calls this a "visual recitation", not a visual presentation. Its authors defend it on two grounds: it means nothing is forgotten, and a handout is provided at the end.
- Becoming an effective presentation "entertainer" takes real effort and formal coaching in speaking and in managing an audience (many firms run such courses). But **anyone designing a business presentation should at least be able to deliver this minimum:**
  - text slides limited to the key ideas, correctly grouped, each group summarized, all worded as tersely as possible;
  - backed up by easy-to-read exhibits (charts, tables, diagrams);
  - all of it following a carefully planned storyboard and script.
- **Two kinds of slides**, ideally about **90% exhibits to 10% text**. Their roles:
  1. make the presentation's structure plain (text slide);
  2. highlight key clusters of thought, e.g. conclusions, recommendations, next steps (text slide);
  3. demonstrate relationships that words alone can't readily make clear (exhibit).
- The chapter doesn't attempt full slide design or presentation delivery; it passes on Zelazny's rules for text and exhibit slides and the author's method for going from pyramid to storyboard/script.

### Designing Text Slides (pp. 191–195)
- **The star is you, the speaker, with your message.** The audience will always find you more interesting than anything projected. Slides are visual *aids*; their main job is to keep the presentation moving. So keep a sharp line between the words you speak and the material you project.

#### What You Say (pp. 192–193)
- Example (a structure-clarifying text slide, Jackson Foods, "CURRENT REALITY"): the **script** says in full sentences that the company has very high out-of-stock levels, that in its business failing to fill orders costs market share, and then, as three ¶ points, that this is partly due to manufacturing problems, that those are compounded by poorly managed supply chain processes, and that supply chain and manufacturing are out of step, so neither stock-outs nor key-customer service get fixed. The **slide** shows only: the label CURRENT REALITY, the headline "High out-of-stock levels", and three bullets of two to four words each, one per ¶ point (manufacturing, supply chain processes, and the alignment between them).
- **The best text slides are as stark and simple as possible.** Lead-ins and bridges between points never take up slide space (let alone whole slides); the speaker voices them.
- Consequence: anyone who missed the talk will find the slides alone unintelligible as a handout. A workaround some use: bind the slides with the script on the facing page, which serves both purposes, **but then write the script in outline form with transitions removed.**
- **Text slides are best confined to emphasizing the pyramid's major points**, roughly as in Exhibit 61:
  - Situation points (bulleted text slide)
  - Complication points (bulleted text slide)
  - Main Point and Key Line points (headline + numbered 1, 2, 3)
  - First Key Line point + its support points (1 as headline; a, b, c)
  - First support point: its sentence above a chart, or several charts (a + chart)
  - Second support point: its sentence above a chart (b + chart)
  - Third support point as a sentence plus graphic (c + chart)
  - Second Key Line point + its support points (2; a, b, c)
  - Next support point: sentence above a chart … and so on.

#### What You Show — seven guidelines (pp. 193–195)
1. **Present and support one idea at a time.** Sole exception: a slide that lists several points, such as a recap or a preview of points that later slides will expand.
2. **Use statements, not captions.** A word or two that merely names the topic leaves the audience free to guess wrong; a short statement removes any chance of misreading the point. (The book's pair is a two-word sales caption vs. the same words plus a verdict; an invented equivalent: "Q3 margins" vs. "Q3 margins fell for the first time in five years".)
3. **Keep the text brief.** Aim for at most ~6 lines, ~30 words, per slide; an idea needing more probably needs more slides.
4. **Use simple words and numbers.** Jargon, long words, and tangled phrasing draw the viewer's eye and mind away from the speaker. Round numbers: "$4.9 million" is easier to take in than "$4,876,987".
5. **Readable type size: the rule of 32.**
   - Know the farthest viewer's distance? Distance (feet) ÷ 32 = smallest legible letter height (inches). 16 ft ÷ 32 = 0.5 in.
   - Know the letter height? Height (inches) × 32 = farthest legible distance (feet). 0.75 in × 32 = 24 ft.
   - **Disagreement on illegible slides:** the author's one exception is a slide made unreadable on purpose to convey how tangled a situation is, and even then the presenter should say so, so nobody strains to read it. Zelazny disagrees: telling the audience not to read a slide is a cop-out; anything worth showing is worth making legible; illegibility is an inelegant way to show complexity; he says he can make such a slide legible about 98% of the time, and in the remaining cases the visual shouldn't be used.
6. **Design slides to be interesting to look at**: layout, typeface, and color raise interest. Zelazny's technique **"put the text slide to work"**: text slides all tend to look alike and a run of them bores; think of the text slide as a chart whose raw material is words rather than numbers, and design it to show the relationships among the thoughts (Exhibit 62).
7. **Use "build" slides** to heighten interest or handle complexity: reveal the slide's parts one at a time, explaining as you go, so the full slide never looks overwhelming. Exhibit 62 could be built: first the left circle, then the three middle circles, then the boxes.

### Designing Exhibit Slides (pp. 196–198)
- Text slides use the familiar medium of words; exhibit slides (charts, graphs, tables, diagrams) use a different medium, visual relationships, which convey large data sets and intricate relationships more effectively than prose.
- **Exhibits must be as simple and readable as possible.** The viewer can't stop to study them and work out what the elements mean. A cluttered, detailed, complicated chart makes you spend precious time explaining the chart instead of discussing its message.
- Exception: now and then a more intricate chart or diagram is acceptable if it gradually makes sense as the speaker talks it through, **but no more than one or two per presentation.**
- Exhibits usually either depict how a structure or process breaks into parts, or picture data as pies, bars, columns, curves, or dots. They generally respond to one of **five question types** (Exhibits 63–67):

| Question | Book's examples (described) and the form drawn |
|---|---|
| 1. What are the elements? (Exh. 63) | Organization chart titled with the benefit of a regional structure (easier delegation). Process flow of boxes and arrows titled with the claim that the company's supply chain is a standard one. |
| 2a. How do amounts compare to the whole? (Exh. 64) | Pie; title asserts that one region supplies close to half of sales. |
| 2b. …to each other? (Exh. 64) | Horizontal bars; title names the product line with the smallest profit. |
| 2c. …over time? (Exh. 64) | Vertical columns by year; title says costs fell every year except one. |
| 3. What has / how has it changed? (Exh. 65) | Two curves of amount against time; title contrasts flattening sales with rising costs. Paired columns per period; title says competitors have caught up. |
| 4. How are items distributed? (Exh. 66) | Frequency curve of order count by order size; title says most orders exceed a dollar threshold. Column histogram of order count by day; title says ordering peaks mid-month. |
| 5. How do items co-relate? (Exh. 67) | Paired (back-to-back) bars, cost on one side and overtime on the other; title says the two don't move together. Dot (scatter) chart of company size against orders placed; title says no relationship is visible. |

  > **Skill note:** The book's text names only the generic forms (pies, bars, columns, curves, dots); the pairing of each question with a specific form above is read from the exhibit drawings.

- **The trick:** decide the question the exhibit should answer, **state the answer as the chart's title**, then choose the chart form best suited to showing that point.
- **Title = message.** The title of a chart or diagram should state its message directly, as a full sentence or a phrase containing a verb. Benefits:
  - It lets you check that the chart's visual impression actually matches the message you intend.
  - It prevents confusion: left alone, different viewers fix on different relationships depending on their viewpoint, background, or interests; the title focuses them at once on the aspect you want. The book contrasts a topic title (profit share by region) with a statement (one named region earns nearly half the profit); an invented equivalent: "Headcount by function" vs. "Sales now employs a third of all staff".

### Storyboarding (pp. 199–202)
- Once you know the requirements for text and exhibit slides, design the whole presentation. **The author's procedure from pyramid to presentation:**
  1. **Write the introduction in full**, every word you'll say, in order. This ensures nothing is missing from the opening story and gives you a chance to confirm that this audience really has the question you plan to answer.
  2. **Take a blank storyboard** and head each empty frame with one point: the introduction points worth showing visually, plus the Key Line points and the points one level below the Key Line.
  3. **Rough out how you'll illustrate each point**, usually without real numbers: just indicate the kind of data and add notes, for yourself and the designer, on the relationship you want to show.
  4. **Write the spoken script that surrounds each slide**, so that the sequence reads as one continuous story.
  5. **Complete the designs** and hand them over for professional production.
  6. **Rehearse, rehearse, rehearse.**
- **A storyboard**, in its simplest form, is a landscape sheet split into boxes, one per blank slide. On it you write the specific points you expect to become slides and mark which should be text slides and which should be some kind of graphic.
- **Every slide is headed by a line (sentence or phrase) that states the point it illustrates.** This reminds both presenter and audience of the point, especially when a slide stays up for some time.
- Exhibit 68 (pyramid) and Exhibit 69 (first slides of the storyboard) illustrate; see Worked examples.
- **Limits and further reading (p. 202):** the chapter offers just the broad route from pyramid to slides, not the detailed planning and analysis that make a presentation compelling. The author recommends Antony Jay's *Effective Presentation* (London, 1970; published in the US as *The New Oratory*) for audience analysis, staging, presentation techniques, and rehearsal. Her favorite insight from it: the audience grants the presenter a favor by attending, not the reverse.

## Rules
- **SCR-1** — Treat a presentation as a show for a live audience, not a report in slide form: plan the script, storyboard, visuals, timing, and pacing. *Why:* a live audience is uncomfortable, distractible, and unpredictable; it must be held. *Check:* does a script and storyboard exist separately from the slides? (pp. 189–190)
- **SCR-2** — Never put a list on a slide where a group of related ideas summarized by an insight belongs, and never show seven items. *Why:* lists carry no point, and seven items overloads the audience. *Check:* every bulleted slide has a headline stating the insight its bullets support; count bullets. (p. 190)
- **SCR-3** — Don't read slide text aloud word for word, and don't say something different from what the slide says. *Why:* reading bores; diverging confuses. *Check:* compare script to slide; the script should expand and connect, not duplicate or contradict. (p. 190)
- **SCR-4** — Deliver at least the basic minimum: text slides holding only the key ideas, grouped, summarized, and brief; clear supporting exhibits; an underlying storyboard and script. *Why:* this is what holds a business audience's attention. *Check:* all three are present. (p. 191)
- **SCR-5** — Aim for about 90% exhibits and 10% text slides; use text slides only to clarify the presentation's structure or to emphasize key thought groups (conclusions, recommendations, next steps); use exhibits for relationships words can't show. *Why:* each type has a distinct job. *Check:* classify each slide by role; text slides with no structure/emphasis role are candidates for cutting or converting. (p. 191)
- **SCR-6** — Make yourself, not the slides, the star: put transitions and introductory remarks in what you say, not on the screen. *Why:* slides are aids whose job is to keep things moving. *Check:* slides contain no transitional or scene-setting sentences. (pp. 191–192)
- **SCR-7** — If the deck must also work as a handout, pair each slide with its script on the facing page, with the script written in outline form and transitions omitted. Don't bloat slides to make them self-explanatory. *Why:* stark slides are unintelligible to absentees. *Check:* handout need is met without adding text to slides. (p. 192)
- **SCR-8** — Confine text slides to the pyramid's major points, in the pattern Situation → Complication → Main Point + Key Line → each Key Line point with its supports → each support point as a sentence over a graphic. *Why:* slides should mirror the pyramid's top levels. *Check:* map each text slide to a pyramid box at or above one level below the Key Line. (pp. 192–193, Exh. 61)
- **SCR-9** — Present and support one idea per slide; the only exception is a slide enumerating a set of points (a summary, or a preview of points to come). *Why:* one idea at a time keeps the audience with you. *Check:* can the slide's point be stated in one sentence? (p. 193)
- **SCR-10** — Title every slide with a statement, not a caption. *Why:* a statement prevents the audience from inferring the wrong point. *Check:* does the title contain a verb and make a claim? (p. 194)
- **SCR-11** — Keep text slides to about 6 lines or ~30 words; split if more. *Why:* brevity keeps attention on the speaker. *Check:* count lines and words. (p. 194)
- **SCR-12** — Use simple words and simplified numbers (e.g. $4.9 million, not $4,876,987). *Why:* long words, jargon, and exact figures distract from the speaker. *Check:* scan for technical terms and unrounded numbers. (p. 194)
- **SCR-13** — Size type by the rule of 32: minimum letter height (in) = farthest viewer distance (ft) ÷ 32. *Why:* illegible text loses the audience. *Check:* compute for the room. (p. 194)
- **SCR-14** — Make every slide legible. If you deliberately show an unreadable slide to convey complexity (the author's lone exception), tell the audience not to read it; note that Zelazny rejects even this and says to redesign or drop it. *Why:* an unreadable slide makes the audience strain or disengage. *Check:* flag any slide below rule-of-32 size; require explicit justification. (p. 194)
- **SCR-15** — Make text slides visually interesting by "putting them to work": lay out the words to show the relationships among the ideas (e.g. a tree of circles and boxes) rather than as a uniform bullet list. *Why:* a run of look-alike text slides bores. *Check:* consecutive text slides that are all plain bullets are a candidate for redesign. (pp. 194–195, Exh. 62)
- **SCR-16** — Use build slides to reveal a complex slide in stages as you explain it. *Why:* it raises interest and keeps the full slide from overwhelming. *Check:* any dense diagram has a planned build sequence. (p. 195)
- **SCR-17** — Keep exhibits simple and readable; allow at most one or two more complex diagrams per presentation, and only ones that become clear as you speak. *Why:* viewers can't study a chart, and explaining a cluttered one wastes time. *Check:* can a viewer grasp each exhibit's message in seconds? Count complex exhibits. (p. 196)
- **SCR-18** — For each exhibit, first decide which question it answers (elements, comparison, change, distribution, co-relation), then state the answer as the title, then pick the chart form that best shows it. *Why:* form follows the message, not the data. *Check:* name the question; confirm the chart form suits it (see table). (p. 196, Exh. 63–67)
- **SCR-19** — Title every chart or diagram with its message as a sentence or a verb phrase, and check that the chart's visual impression matches that message. *Why:* it focuses all viewers on the same relationship and catches charts that contradict their claim. *Check:* topic-only titles (e.g. "Headcount by function") fail. (p. 198)
- **SCR-20** — Before making slides, write the introduction out in full, word for word. *Why:* it ensures the opening story is complete and that the question is valid for this audience. *Check:* a full-text introduction exists. (p. 199)
- **SCR-21** — Storyboard the introduction points you want to show, the Key Line points, and the points one level below the Key Line, each heading a blank slide frame, each marked text or graphic. *Why:* it fixes what each slide must say before any design work. *Check:* every Key Line point and its immediate supports appears on the storyboard. (p. 199)
- **SCR-22** — Rough out each visual with indicative data types and notes on the intended relationship before real numbers or final design. *Why:* it settles the message and form first. *Check:* storyboard frames have sketch plus relationship note. (p. 199)
- **SCR-23** — Write the spoken words for each slide so that the whole sequence reads as one story. *Why:* the slides alone don't make a narrative. *Check:* a script exists linking every slide. (p. 199)
- **SCR-24** — Put a point-stating sentence or phrase at the top of every slide. *Why:* it reminds speaker and audience of the point, especially when the slide stays up a while. *Check:* every slide's top line is a point, not a topic. (p. 199)
- **SCR-25** — Rehearse repeatedly before presenting. *Why:* the author's final step, emphasized threefold. *Check:* rehearsal time is planned. (p. 199)

## Procedures

### P1. Pyramid → storyboard → presentation (author's method, p. 199)
1. **Write the introduction in full** (every word, in order). Decision: does the question it raises truly fit this audience? If not, rework the introduction (and possibly the pyramid) before going on.
2. **Lay out blank storyboard frames** (paper sideways, divided into slide boxes).
3. **Head each frame with a point**: the introduction points worth showing (Situation, Complication), the Main Point, each Key Line point, and each point one level below the Key Line. Write each as a statement.
4. **Mark each frame text or graphic.** Decision rule (from SCR-5, SCR-18): structure or emphasis of a key group → text slide; a relationship among data or parts → exhibit; aim for mostly exhibits.
5. **For each graphic frame**: name its question (elements / comparison / change / distribution / co-relation), sketch the chart form without real numbers, note the data type and relationship for the designer.
6. **Script the spoken words around each slide** so the sequence tells a story; keep transitions and introductions in the script, not on slides.
7. **Finish designs and have them drawn**, applying the text-slide guidelines (SCR-9 to SCR-16) and exhibit rules (SCR-17 to SCR-19).
8. **Rehearse** several times.

### P2. Design a single text slide (pp. 193–195)
1. State the one idea as a sentence; make it the slide's top line (SCR-9, SCR-10, SCR-24).
2. Decide whether this is the enumeration exception (summary or preview list).
3. Add only the supporting points needed, as short phrases; total ≤ ~6 lines / ~30 words (SCR-11).
4. Simplify words and round numbers (SCR-12).
5. If the points have a structure (alternatives, causes, components), lay them out as a relationship diagram rather than plain bullets (SCR-15); if dense, plan a build (SCR-16).
6. Check the type size against the room (SCR-13).
7. Move all transitional/introductory wording into the script (SCR-6).

### P3. Design a single exhibit slide (pp. 196–198)
1. Decide the question the exhibit answers.
2. Write the answer as the title (sentence or verb phrase).
3. Choose the form: elements → organization chart or process flow; share of a whole → pie; items against each other → bars; amounts over time → columns; change → curves or paired columns over time; distribution → frequency curve or histogram; co-relation → paired bars or dot chart.
4. Strip clutter until a viewer can grasp it without study.
5. Check that the visual impression matches the title; if not, change the chart or the title.
6. Count complex exhibits across the deck (≤ 2).

### P4. Convert a "visual recitation" deck (diagnostic repair; pp. 190–195)
1. For each list slide, ask what single insight the items jointly support; make that the headline; regroup items (≤ ~5) under it, or split.
2. Move sentences that explain, introduce, or transition into the script.
3. Replace caption titles with statements.
4. Convert text describing data or relationships into exhibits until exhibits dominate.
5. If a handout is needed, produce slide + outline-script pairs rather than wordier slides.

## Diagnostics
| Symptom in a deck | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Slide titled "Guiding Principles" with seven long numbered sentences | List without an insight; too many items; visual recitation | State the insight; group to a few points; move detail to script (SCR-2, SCR-9, SCR-11) |
| Presenter reads every word from the slides | Slides carry the script | Strip slides to the point; speak the rest (SCR-3, SCR-6) |
| Presenter's words differ from the on-screen wording | Two competing messages | Align script to slide, or simplify slide (SCR-3) |
| "We put it all on the slides so we don't forget anything / for the handout" | Slides used as notes and document | Script for the speaker; slide + outline-script handout (SCR-7) |
| Mostly text slides, few charts | Exhibit/text balance inverted | Convert data and relationships to exhibits; aim ~90/10 (SCR-5) |
| Slide titles are bare topics ("Q3 margins", "Headcount by function") | Captions, not statements | Rewrite as claims with verbs (SCR-10, SCR-19) |
| More than ~6 lines or ~30 words on a slide | Overloaded slide | Split or cut (SCR-11) |
| Exact figures like $4,876,987 | Numbers too precise to absorb | Round (SCR-12) |
| Text unreadable from the back of the room | Type too small | Apply rule of 32 (SCR-13, SCR-14) |
| Long run of identical bullet slides | Monotony | Put text slides to work; use builds (SCR-15, SCR-16) |
| Dense chart that takes minutes to explain | Exhibit too complex | Simplify, or reserve as one of ≤2 complex visuals built up while speaking (SCR-17, SCR-16) |
| Chart form doesn't match the point (e.g. pie used to show change over time) | Form chosen before question | Name the question, then choose form (SCR-18) |
| Chart's title claims one thing but the eye sees another | Visual/message mismatch | Check impression against title; redesign (SCR-19) |
| Slides drafted before introduction is settled | No storyboard discipline | Write the intro in full; storyboard first (SCR-20, SCR-21) |
| Slides built straight from final data without rough sketches | Design before message | Rough out with notes on relationships first (SCR-22) |

## Templates and patterns

### Slide sequence mirroring the pyramid (Exhibit 61)
```
[Text]   Situation points             • • •
[Text]   Complication points          • • •
[Text]   Main Point + Key Line        <Main point>  1 __  2 __  3 __
[Text]   Key Line point 1             1 <statement>  a __  b __  c __
[Exhibit] Support 1a                  "a <statement>" over a graphic/set of graphics
[Exhibit] Support 1b                  "b <statement>" over a graphic
[Exhibit] Support 1c                  "c <statement>" plus graphic
[Text]   Key Line point 2             2 <statement>  a __  b __  c __
[Exhibit] Support 2a                  "a <statement>" over a graphic
  … and so on
```

### Script vs. slide pairing
```
SCRIPT (spoken; full sentences; transitions included)
  <Label>
  <Full-sentence statement of the situation/point and why it matters>.
  ¶ <Full sentence: first contributing point>
  ¶ <Full sentence: second contributing point>
  ¶ <Full sentence: third contributing point>

SLIDE (shown; stark)
  <LABEL>
  <Headline statement of the point>
    ▪ <2–5 word phrase>
    ▪ <2–5 word phrase>
    ▪ <2–5 word phrase>
```

### Text-slide checklist (Zelazny's guidelines as passed on by the author)
```
[ ] One idea (unless enumerating a summary/preview)
[ ] Title is a statement, not a caption
[ ] ≤ ~6 lines / ~30 words
[ ] Simple words; rounded numbers
[ ] Type height ≥ farthest distance (ft) / 32 inches
[ ] Visually interesting: relationships laid out, not plain bullets
[ ] Build sequence planned if complex
```

### Exhibit design frame
```
Question:  What are the elements? | How do amounts compare (whole / each other / over time)?
           | What has changed? | How are items distributed? | How do items co-relate?
Title:     <the answer, as a sentence or verb phrase>
Form:      org chart / flow | pie | bar | column | curve / paired columns | frequency curve /
           histogram | paired bars / dot chart
Check:     does what the eye sees match the title?
```

### Rule of 32
```
min letter height (in)   = farthest viewer distance (ft) / 32     e.g. 16 ft → 0.5 in
max legible distance (ft) = letter height (in) × 32                e.g. 0.75 in → 24 ft
```

### Storyboard frame
```
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│ #n  <point stated as sentence>│   ← top line = the slide's point
│ [TEXT] or [GRAPHIC]           │
│ sketch / bullets              │
│ note: data type; relationship │
└──────────────────────────────┘
Fill frames for: intro points to show · Main Point · each Key Line point · each point one level below
```

## Worked examples

**Bad slide (p. 190) — "Guiding Principles".** Before: a framing sentence ("these principles drove the design of a new supply chain vision") plus seven numbered, multi-clause principles. Diagnosis: a list, not grouped ideas summarized by an insight; seven is too many; 50–60 such slides read aloud make a "visual recitation". Lesson: slides must carry grouped, summarized insights, not documents.

**Script vs. slide (p. 192) — Jackson Foods "Current Reality".**
```
Script: problem statement (stock-outs very high) + why it matters (lost share)
   ¶ cause 1 (manufacturing), as a full sentence
   ¶ cause 2 (supply chain processes) compounding cause 1, as a full sentence
   ¶ cause 3 (misalignment of the two, blocking a fix and a customer focus), as a full sentence
Slide:  LABEL
        headline = the problem in ≤5 words
          ▪ cause 1 in 2–4 words
          ▪ cause 2 in 2–4 words
          ▪ cause 3 in 2–4 words
```
Lesson: the slide shows the skeleton; the speaker supplies explanation and connective tissue.

**Exhibit 61 (p. 193) — Slides map to the pyramid.** Nine-frame layout (see Templates): S and C as bullet slides, then Main Point + Key Line, then each Key Line point with a/b/c, then each support point as a sentence over a chart. Lesson: text slides handle the top of the pyramid; exhibits carry the support points.

**Exhibit 62 (p. 195) — A text slide put to work.** Title asserts that there are three main routes to higher machine efficiency.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **Author vs. Zelazny on illegible slides:** the author permits a deliberately unreadable slide to dramatize complexity, if announced; Zelazny says it's a cop-out, can almost always be made legible, and otherwise shouldn't be shown (p. 194). A skill should flag such slides and present both views.
- **One idea per slide has one exception:** enumerations (summaries, previews of points to come) (p. 193).
- **Complex exhibits are allowed sparingly:** one or two per presentation, if they become clear as the speaker develops them (p. 196).
- **Stark slides make poor handouts.** That is accepted, not fixed by adding words: use facing-page outline scripts (p. 192).
- **The 90/10 ratio is an ideal** ("ideally"), not a hard cap (p. 191).
- **The rules are a minimum.** Real presentation skill (public speaking, audience handling, staging, rehearsal) needs training and further reading (Jay); the chapter covers only the general pyramid-to-slides steps (pp. 191, 202).
- **Most of the slide rules are Zelazny's**, passed on by the author; the storyboarding method is hers (p. 191).
- **Statements over captions applies to both text slides and charts** (pp. 194, 198).
- **Storyboard depth:** introduction + Key Line + one level below; deeper detail lives in the script or later slides ("10–17 etc.") (p. 199).
