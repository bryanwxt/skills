# Reflecting the Pyramid in Prose

> Source: Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Ch. 12 "Reflecting the Pyramid in Prose", book pp. 203–209. Paraphrased reference; rule IDs use prefix `PRO`.

## When a skill needs this
- Rewriting dense, jargon-heavy business sentences into clear ones once the pyramid (the points and their order) is settled.
- Reviewing a draft sentence by sentence: can a reader "see" what it says? Which nouns carry the meaning, and what is their relationship?
- Diagnosing sentences where the reader has forgotten the start by the time the end arrives, or where the wrong thing is made the subject.
- Coaching a writer who is clear when talking but turns stiff and technical on paper.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning (one line) |
|---|---|
| Two steps of clear writing | Settle the point first; only then find words for it. |
| Dehydrated style | Compressed, jargon-laden business prose that writers mistakenly think commands respect. |
| Image (conceptual) | An abstract mental configuration that compresses many facts into one graspable shape. |
| Memory image | George Miller's term: the non-photographic picture a reader builds piece by piece while reading, which summarizes the passage and aids recall. |
| Skeletal image / skeletal structure | A sketchy arrangement of simple geometric forms plus arrows that captures only the structure of the relationships. |
| Create the image | Visualize the relationships your ideas involve (or, when rewriting, those hidden in a bad sentence). |
| Copy the image in words | Write a sentence that describes the image so the reader can rebuild it. |
| Find the nouns | The rewriting trick: isolate the relatively concrete nouns, then picture how they relate. |

## Concepts

### Chapter opening: why good thinkers write bad prose (pp. 203–205)
- **Two steps.** As stated at the start of the book, clear writing is (1) deciding the point, then (2) putting it into words. With the pyramid built and its groupings checked, you know exactly which points to make and in what order; only the wording remains.
- **In theory easy, in practice not.** You'd expect a business writer to turn their pyramided points into concise, graceful sentences and paragraphs carrying a lively message. Instead the typical sentence is long-winded and jargon-heavy, so paragraphs feel impenetrable and the subject endlessly dull. Three specimen sentences (all reworked later):
  1. A sales-force sentence of roughly 40 words: a chain of about seven prepositional phrases built on abstract nouns (improvement, cost-effectiveness, deployment, need, missions), with the only concrete things (sales force, stores, trading environment) buried in the middle and at the end.
  2. A planning sentence of similar length in the passive voice: a string of plan-type nouns (adjustments, preliminary plans, contingency-plan outlines, prioritized guides, discretionary spending) with no clear actor and no clear request.
  3. A two-sentence cash-flow passage: an abstract subject (current "needs") said to burden a system, the system said to be unready for strict accuracy, then a passive claim that improvement is possible through some unspecified information.
- **Who writes this:** capable, well-spoken people who are strong problem solvers and could explain the same ideas perfectly well aloud. On paper, though, they appear to think that a parched style and heavy technical vocabulary earn extra respect.
- **The author's verdict: that belief is nonsense.** Good ideas shouldn't be dressed in bad prose. Technical works can also be literary art (she cites William James, Freud, Whitehead, Russell, and Bronowski). Writing for specialists must of course use technical language, but overloading with jargon and a tortuous, cramped style is fashion, not necessity.
- **Objective:** prose that not only communicates the ideas clearly but gives the reader pleasure in absorbing them. Every writing book says this; it's hard. The technique that helps: **deliberately call up the mental pictures you relied on when the ideas first came to you.**
- **Why images (the author's belief):** abstract thinking runs on pictures, not words, because pictures are the more efficient medium. An image can synthesize a great mass of facts into one abstract configuration. Since a person can think about only about seven or eight items at once, this compression is enormously handy; without it your decisions could rest only on a handful of detailed facts. Put seven or eight such abstract images together and you can juggle a vast quantity of intricate detail in your head.
- **Illustration ("Relationships", p. 204):** three shaded bars A, B, C of decreasing length beside the statements "A is longer than B; B is longer than C; therefore A is longer than C". The relationships are grasped much faster from the bars than from the words.
- **Therefore:** to write clear sentences, first "see" what you're talking about, then copy that image into words. The reader rebuilds the image from your words, which lets them both grasp your thinking and enjoy the process.
- The chapter then shows (a) how readily images form when reading good prose and (b) how to find the images buried in bad prose so it can be rewritten.

### Create the Image (pp. 205–206)
- **Demonstration with good prose:** a short descriptive passage from Thoreau's *Walden* (in late March 1845 he borrows an axe, goes to the woods by Walden Pond near his intended house site, and cuts young white pines for timber; the hillside, the view of the pond and a small clearing, the pond's ice not yet melted, dark and waterlogged). Readers asked to watch their own minds find they build a picture, adding details as each phrase arrives.
- **What that picture is:** not a photograph but what George Miller (to whom the author credits the example) calls a **memory image**, which grows piecemeal as you read. The author describes her own as a run of shifting scenes: a dim, long-past day; an axe changing hands between two vague figures; a walk to the woods; pines being felled; a hillside appearing under them; finally a pause to look out over pond, clearing, and ice. Yours may differ; the point is that **you were building the scene yourself while reading.**
- **What the memory image does:**
  - It summarizes the information presented; you build it as part of understanding.
  - It helps you remember. Put the book down and you can't recite the passage, but you can read off from the image something roughly equivalent to it.
  - Memory research shows images improve recall, though recall is selective: some details drop out and others get embroidered, depending on the reader's feelings. Even so, the image serves as the reader's own phrase-by-phrase record of the passage and of what they took from it.
- **Every act of reading needs this image-building** if the material is to be understood and retained. Some passages are harder to visualize; very abstract ideas may be represented by skeletal structures rather than pictures. **But if the reader cannot picture the passage in some form, cannot actually "see" what it says, they have not understood it.**
- **Abstract prose can still be visual.** A passage from a document debating whether the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development should switch from fixed to floating lending rates argues: if the risk allowances built into the lending-rate spread prove too high, the excess income goes back to borrowers through a lower rate later; so fixed-rate lending would cost borrowers extra only if the Bank systematically overestimated risk and kept excess income more or less permanently, which seems unlikely. Words like "spread", "excess", and "reduction" let you see the relationships. You could draw it with about four lines and two arrows: a band labelled lending-rate spread with a wavy line of actual outcomes inside it; the top of the band marked "high risk allowances (excess income)"; an arrow from that down to "reduced lending rate". (The author added labels; you wouldn't need them for yourself.)
- **The image should be skeletal.** What's wanted is not a detailed, photo-like copy but just the outline of how the elements relate: usually one or more geometric forms (circle, straight line, oval, rectangle) arranged schematically or sketchily, plus something like an arrow for direction and interaction. Such sketches can look naive, yet great "visual thinkers" from Einstein on have described their conscious visual imagery as vague, hazy, and abstract.

### Copy the Image in Words (pp. 207–209)
- **Applying only these elementary image-making moves transforms the rewriting of bad prose.** Bad sentences fail to call up an image as you read, so the mind gropes for something solid to hold.
- **Method shown on the three specimens:**
  1. **Sales-force sentence.** Split into phrases: by the time "field sales-force deployment" arrives, the opening ("a primary area of potential improvement …") has slipped from memory, and the sentence continues for four more phrases. Ask: which **nouns** are relatively concrete? Sales force, store, changed trade environment. Picture them: a salesman approaching a store, both inside an oval labelled "new environment". The main relationship is salesman → store. The likely intended sense: **the sales force has to be redeployed to suit the new trading environment.**
  2. **Preplanned-adjustments sentence.** Nouns: preplanned adjustments; the alternative preliminary plans; the contingency-plan outlines and ranked guides (the author admits she can't tell what the last phrase means). Picture: a stack of alternative plans → arrow → preplanned adjustments → arrow → a contingency plan, drawn as a numbered list 1–5. The author apparently wants a contingency plan from the reader, so the sense becomes an instruction: **list, in priority order, the activities to be cut back should the plan need adjusting.**
  3. **Cash-flow sentence.** First, an immediate objection: the sentence blames the system for being unprepared, but it isn't really the system that fails the accuracy requirement. Applying the method anyway, the nouns are inaccurate cash-flow analyses, system, improvements, information. Picture: SYSTEM → inaccurate analyses (crossed out); NEW INFO → SYSTEM → accurate analyses. Insight: feeding in the right information yields accurate analyses, so the sense is: **given X kind of information, the system can deliver accurate cash-flow analyses.** Without the author, you can't say what X is, i.e. which neglected projection inputs the sentence had in mind.
- **The trick in one line:** find the nouns, look for the relationships between them, and see those as a visual image; then write the sentence that describes the image.
- **Summary (p. 209):** make yourself picture how your ideas relate to one another. With a clear mental image you can translate it directly into a clear sentence, which the reader can decode and absorb just as directly, with the bonus that the knowledge is then stored in memory as an image.
- **Why image storage matters:** reading proceeds word by word and we can hold only a few words in mind. By extracting the picture from the text, the reader takes in knowledge in large chunks (more efficient to process) and as a vivid impression (easier to recall).
- **Closing (p. 209):** the author quotes her kinsman Professor William Minto, from a more leisurely era. His image: a writer is like a commander filing a battalion through a gap so narrow only one soldier passes at a time, while the reader must re-form the troops on the other side; however large or complex the subject, that is the only way it can be communicated. Hence the writer owes the reader order and arrangement, the duties the old rhetoricians stressed toward those who honor us with their attention. She ends by urging the reader to do the same.

## Rules
- **PRO-1** — Settle the point and its order (the pyramid) before working on wording; treat wording as the second, separate step. *Why:* clear writing is deciding what to say, then saying it. *Check:* can the writer state the paragraph's point and its place in the pyramid before editing the sentence? (p. 203)
- **PRO-2** — Don't dress good ideas in dehydrated, jargon-laden prose; use technical terms only where specialist readers need them. *Why:* jargon overload and cramped style are fashion, not necessity, and make paragraphs impenetrable. *Check:* would the writer explain it this way aloud? If the spoken version is clearer, write that. (pp. 203–204)
- **PRO-3** — Aim for prose that gives the reader pleasure as well as clarity. *Why:* a reader who rebuilds the image enjoys it and remembers it. *Check:* reading the passage produces a picture, not a struggle. (p. 204)
- **PRO-4** — Before writing a sentence, visualize the relationship it expresses (the image you used in thinking up the idea). *Why:* conceptual thought is image-based; a sentence copied from an image is clear. *Check:* can the writer sketch the idea? (p. 204)
- **PRO-5** — Write the sentence as a copy of the image, so the reader can rebuild it. *Why:* the reader understands and stores the idea as an image. *Check:* give the sentence to a reader and ask them to draw it; the drawing should match the writer's sketch. (pp. 204, 209)
- **PRO-6** — Treat "can the reader see it?" as the test of understanding: if a passage can't be visualized in some form, even skeletally, the reader hasn't understood it. *Why:* comprehension and memory depend on constructing an image. *Check:* attempt a sketch of each key sentence; failure flags a rewrite. (p. 206)
- **PRO-7** — Keep images skeletal: a few geometric forms (circles, lines, ovals, rectangles) plus arrows for direction and interaction; not detailed pictures. *Why:* only the structure of relationships matters. *Check:* the sketch uses ≤ a handful of shapes and arrows (the bank example needs about four lines and two arrows). (p. 206)
- **PRO-8** — To rewrite an opaque sentence: break it into phrases, pick out the relatively concrete nouns, sketch how they relate, identify the main relationship, and write one plain sentence stating it. *Why:* the meaning is in the relationships among nouns, lost in strung-together phrases. *Check:* rewritten sentence names the key nouns and states one relationship among them. (pp. 207–208)
- **PRO-9** — While rewriting, check that the sentence attributes the action or failing to the right thing. *Why:* bad sentences often blame or credit the wrong noun (e.g. calling the system unprepared when the problem lies elsewhere). *Check:* ask of the subject, "is this really what does/lacks that?" (p. 208)
- **PRO-10** — When the image exposes a missing specific (e.g. what kind of information), get it from the author rather than inventing it; mark it as a placeholder. *Why:* the rewrite can only clarify what the author actually meant. *Check:* placeholders such as "X kind of information" are flagged for the author. (p. 208)
- **PRO-11** — Watch for sentences whose opening is forgotten before the main noun arrives. *Why:* reading is word by word with limited holding capacity. *Check:* if the reader must hold more than a phrase or two before learning what the sentence is about, restructure around the concrete nouns. (pp. 207, 209)
- **PRO-12** — Treat order and arrangement as a duty owed to the reader. *Why:* the reader receives ideas one at a time through a narrow channel and must reassemble them. *Check:* ideas arrive in an order the reader can rebuild (this ties the prose back to the pyramid). (p. 209)

## Procedures

### P1. Write a clear sentence from a pyramid point (pp. 203–205)
1. Take the point (already placed in the pyramid; PRO-1).
2. Ask: what does this look like? Recall or form the image you used when thinking it up: which things are involved, and how they relate (larger/smaller, causes, flows into, sits inside, replaces).
3. Sketch it skeletally if it isn't obvious: shapes for the things, arrows for direction/interaction (PRO-7).
4. Write a sentence that describes the sketch: the concrete things as nouns, the relationship as the verb.
5. Test: could a reader draw your sketch from the sentence? If not, revise (PRO-5, PRO-6).

### P2. Rewrite an opaque sentence ("find the nouns", pp. 207–208)
1. Break the sentence into its phrases, one per line, to see where the reader loses the thread.
2. List the nouns; circle the relatively concrete ones (people, things, places, documents, systems, outcomes). Ignore abstract filler ("area", "potential", "cost-effectiveness", "need").
3. Ask how those nouns relate; draw them (inside/outside, arrow from/to, before/after, crossed out vs. achieved).
4. Check attribution: is the subject really the thing that acts/fails? (PRO-9)
5. Identify the main relationship the sketch reveals; ignore secondary clutter.
6. Write one plain sentence expressing it, ideally with a concrete actor and an active verb.
7. If the sketch has a hole (a quantity, a kind of input, an order of steps), leave a marked placeholder and ask the author (PRO-10).
8. Compare with the original: does the rewrite say what the author evidently intended? If uncertain, present it as a probable meaning, hedged, as the author does.

### P3. Check a passage for "seeability" (pp. 205–206)
1. Read a paragraph once; close it.
2. Try to recall it as a picture or a skeletal diagram.
3. If you can read off its meaning from the picture, it passes.
4. If nothing forms, locate the sentences where the image breaks and apply P2.

## Diagnostics
| Symptom in a draft | Underlying problem | Fix (rule IDs) |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence strings five or more prepositional phrases ("area of improvement … of deployment … of missions … at levels …") | No image; the reader forgets the start before the end | Find the concrete nouns; restate the main relationship (PRO-8, PRO-11) |
| Abstract nouns dominate (improvement, effectiveness, adjustments, requirements) with no concrete actor | Writer hasn't pictured who does what to what | Picture the actors and objects; make them subject and object (PRO-4, PRO-8) |
| Writer explains it clearly aloud but the written version is stiff and technical | Belief that dry, jargon-heavy prose earns respect | Write it as you'd say it; keep jargon only for specialists (PRO-2) |
| Nobody can sketch what the paragraph says | Reader can't "see" it, so hasn't understood it | Sketch the intended relationships, then rewrite (PRO-6, PRO-5) |
| Sentence blames/credits the wrong thing ("the system is not prepared …") | Misattribution hiding the real relationship | Re-assign the action to the right noun (PRO-9) |
| Rewrite needs a specific the draft never gives (e.g. which "information" is missing) | Writer's thinking incomplete | Insert placeholder; query the author (PRO-10) |
| Elaborate, detailed mental picture needed to follow the text | Over-specific image | Reduce to skeletal shapes and arrows (PRO-7) |
| Wording polished but points in no clear order | Step one skipped | Return to the pyramid before rewording (PRO-1, PRO-12) |

## Templates and patterns

### Find-the-nouns worksheet
```
Original sentence:
  <phrase 1>
  <phrase 2>
  <phrase 3> …                         ← where does the reader lose the start?
Concrete nouns:   [A] [B] [C]
Sketch:           A ──► B   (inside C)    /  A ✗ → B   vs   D → A → B ✓
Attribution check: does A really do/lack what the sentence says?
Main relationship: <A does X to B because of C>
Rewrite:          <Subject A> <active verb> <B> <to/so as to/in response to> <C>.
Gaps for author:  <X = ?>
```

### Sentence frames distilled from the book's rewrites
```
We must <act on> <thing> to match <new condition>.               (sales-force rewrite)
<Outline/Specify> the order in which <items> will be <action> if <trigger>.   (contingency rewrite)
<System> can produce <desired output> if we feed it <specific input>.         (cash-flow rewrite)
```

### Skeletal image vocabulary
```
things / sets ........ circles, ovals, rectangles
quantities ........... lines or bars of different length
flow / cause / change  arrows
failure / rejection .. crossed-out box
before → after ....... two rows: old path (crossed) above new path
```

## Worked examples

**"Relationships" (p. 204) — image vs. words.** Three bars A > B > C next to "A longer than B; B longer than C; so A longer than C". Lesson: an image conveys a set of relationships faster than the equivalent sentences.

**Thoreau's *Walden* passage (p. 205) — a memory image forming.** A descriptive paragraph (axe, woods by the pond, young white pines, hillside, view of pond and clearing, unmelted dark ice). Readers build a growing, non-photographic picture phrase by phrase. Lesson: comprehension of good prose is image construction; the image later stands in for the text in memory.

**World Bank lending-rate passage (p. 206) — skeletal image of abstract ideas.** Argument: overstated risk allowances in the spread return to borrowers through later rate cuts; extra cost arises only if the Bank systematically and permanently overestimates risk, which is unlikely. Image: a band (the spread) with a wavy line of outcomes; top edge = high allowances/excess income; arrow down to reduced lending rate. Lesson: even abstract finance can be "seen" with about four lines and two arrows.

**Sales-force sentence (p. 207).**
Before: about seven chained phrases running from an "area of potential improvement", through the cost-effectiveness of sales-force deployment, to selling missions redefined for store and indirect levels, ending with trade-environment change as the driver. The reader loses the opening before reaching the sales force.
Nouns: sales force, store, changed trade environment → image: salesman → store, inside "new environment".
After (sense): the sales force needs redeploying to fit the new trading environment.
Lesson: the main relationship (salesman to store under new conditions) was buried under abstract wrappers.

**Preplanned-adjustments sentence (pp. 207–208).**
Nouns: preplanned adjustments, alternative preliminary plans, outlines of contingency plans/prioritized guides.
Image: alternative plans → preplanned adjustments → numbered contingency plan.
After (sense): set out, in priority order, which activities get cut back if the plan has to be adjusted.
Lesson: the image shows the author wants a prioritized contingency list; the rewrite asks for exactly that.

**Cash-flow sentence (p. 208).**
Objection first: the "system" is not really what fails the accuracy requirement.
Nouns: inaccurate analyses, system, improvements, information.
Image: system → inaccurate analyses (crossed out); new info → system → accurate analyses.
After (sense): feeding the system X kind of information would let it produce accurate cash-flow analyses (X unknown without the author).
Lesson: the image reveals the real causal claim and also exposes the missing specific.

## Nuances and exceptions
- **The image theory is presented as the author's personal conviction** (that all conceptual thinking happens in images), backed by appeal to visual thinkers and memory studies; present it as her rationale, not settled fact (pp. 204, 206).
- **Memory images are imperfect:** readers drop some details and embroider others according to their feelings, but the image is still the record that enables recall (p. 206).
- **Very abstract material** may be held as skeletal structures rather than pictures; that still counts as "seeing" (p. 206).
- **Technical language is legitimate for specialist readers;** the target is jargon overload and cramped style, not technical terms as such (p. 204).
- **Rewrites are interpretations.** The author hedges (prefacing rewrites with "perhaps" and "apparently") and admits she can't resolve some phrases without the writer (pp. 207–208). A skill should present rewrites as proposed readings and flag unresolved terms.
- **Bad prose isn't a sign of weak thinkers:** the specimens came from able, articulate problem-solvers (p. 204). The fix is technique, not intelligence.
- **The chapter assumes the pyramid is done.** The image technique works at sentence level; it doesn't replace structuring (p. 203).
  > **Skill note:** In two of the three specimens the rewrite also changes the sentence from a description into an action or instruction (redeploy the sales force; list the cutbacks in order). The book doesn't state this as a rule; treat it as a pattern visible in the examples.
- The book's "seven or eight items" limit on simultaneous thought (p. 204) is the same working-memory rationale used for grouping in Part One.
