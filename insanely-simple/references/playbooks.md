# Playbooks: applying the lens to real tasks

Each playbook gives inputs, steps and an output format. Element numbers refer to `principles.md`. Always run the **quality gate** first: is the underlying idea good? If not, say so (Element 1) before simplifying it.

---

## A. Simplify a message, doc, email, ad or landing page

**Use for:** "make this clearer / shorter / punchier", copy review, executive summaries.

1. **State the one thing** in one sentence a stranger would understand. If several things compete, ask which one matters most to *this* audience now (Element 3).
2. **Inventory every point.** Tag each one: *core* (the one thing), *proof* (directly supports it) or *other*. Cut every *other*. If several *core* points survive, find the theme that unites them and lead with that.
3. **Translate to human terms.** Turn specs into benefits and jargon into everyday words ("5 GB" → "1,000 songs in your pocket") (Element 8).
4. **Find the icon.** Is there one image, metaphor or example that carries the idea? Use it instead of three weaker ones (Element 5).
5. **Cut the preamble.** Open with the answer or the work, and put context after, only if needed (Element 7).
6. **Line-edit.** Cut every paragraph again, because it can always lose a few more words. Weigh small words ("a" vs "the") where the reader's takeaway changes (Element 6).
7. **Make the call to action simple.** One action and one destination (the home page, not a tracking URL).

**Output:** the rewrite, then a **Cut list** (removed item → reason), then *one* optional note on anything still competing for attention.

---

## B. Review a deck or presentation (or prepare to present to an executive)

1. Can the deck's point be said in three sentences? If yes, ask whether it needs to be a deck at all (Element 7).
2. Delete decorative visuals and keep only conceptual images (Element 5).
3. For a decision meeting: put the work or recommendation up front and skip the strategy recap the decider already knows. Expect to be interrupted, and plan to *discuss*, not perform.
4. For a public keynote: agenda → facts per topic → summary per topic → overall summary, repeating the key line. Rehearse a lot, but deliver conversationally.
5. Check the audience: is every invitee needed? (Element 2.)
6. Check honesty: is anything in there that you don't believe in? Remove it (Element 1).

**Output:** a keep/cut table per slide, plus a proposed 3-sentence spoken version.

---

## C. Rationalise a product lineup, pricing tiers, plans or editions

**Use for:** SKU sprawl, plan pages, feature-flag matrices, "should we add a Pro tier?"

1. **List every offering** with its target need and its real reason for existing (customer need, internal accounting, channel demand, competitor copy).
2. **Draw the smallest grid of genuinely distinct needs.** The 1998 example was consumer/pro × desktop/portable. Place each offering on it.
3. **Flag for removal:** overlaps in one cell, offerings that exist for internal reasons (e.g. "so finance can track the acquisition cost" → *put the software in the box*), offerings copied from a competitor's model, low-end "fillers" that dilute quality.
4. **Collapse to distinct models plus customisation.** Let customers configure within a model instead of choosing between near-identical models.
5. **Price for trust.** One clear price beats editions that feel like being squeezed (Leopard vs Vista).
6. **Test the explanation.** Could a sales rep (or the website) say in one sentence which option suits whom? If different reps would give different answers, it's still too complex.
7. **Name the internal cost** of the simplification (systems, accounting, logistics) and recommend absorbing it so the customer's experience stays simple.

**Output:** a before/after grid, a removal list with reasons, migration notes, and the one-sentence chooser for each remaining option.

## D. Name a product, feature, project, report or event

1. **Brief as constraints:** what it must convey (e.g. "it's a Mac", "it's for the internet"), what it must *not* suggest (e.g. toy, portable), and which family system it has to fit.
2. **Check against the existing naming architecture.** Brand marker + descriptive word beats invented words that tie back to nothing. Keep names stable across generations, with modifiers for information only.
3. **Generate a long list in-house.** The people who know the product and culture name it best. Shortlist about 5 and present each with its rationale.
4. **Prefer the obvious.** If the name makes instantly clear what category you're disrupting, that's a strength even if it undersells the scope.
5. **Settle the name before the plan.** Don't build launch materials with a blank where the name goes.
6. **Legal is an input.** Weigh trademark risk against the long-term value of the right name (Element 9). Don't settle for "the best we could get approved" without trying.
7. **Persist politely.** If a strong candidate is rejected, bring it back next to fresh options.

**Output:** a shortlist (≤5) with fit-to-constraints notes, a recommended pick with reasoning, and risks.

## E. Design a team, meeting or approval process

1. **Headcount test:** list each member and the decision or contribution only they provide. Remove anyone without one. Replace "more people for safety" with "the right people" (Element 2).
2. **Decider test:** name the ultimate decision maker. Are they in the briefing and at regular checkpoints? Do they see work unfiltered? If not, redesign, or delegate real authority to someone who can stay engaged.
3. **Layer test:** count the approval steps between idea and launch. For each, ask whether it improves the idea or only protects someone. Remove the protectors. Watch for "approval only earns the next meeting".
4. **Testing test:** for research or focus-group gates, is there a strong internal judge whose instinct has a track record? If yes, prefer that judge plus lightweight local sanity checks (e.g. ask colleagues on the ground in each market). If no, keep a minimal check, but never let research average the idea into blandness.
5. **Flexibility test:** can a better idea replace an approved one mid-production? If the process forbids it, fix the process.
6. **Format test:** replace standing formal reviews with short, informal, regular working sessions (Element 7). Swap periodic "report cards" for real-time candour (Element 1).
7. **Committee test:** if a cross-division committee exists because divisions disagree, the real fix is a leader who sets the shared vision and makes people align.

**Output:** a proposed roster with roles, decision rights, a cadence, the removed steps with reasons, and the risks of removing them.

## F. Set a project timeline

1. Find your domain's "perfect" duration from past projects: the point where less time hurt quality and more invited overthinking. (For the author's technology marketing it was about 3 months from briefing to live.)
2. Set the deadline slightly tighter than comfortable. The work needs a plan and not quite enough time.
3. Scope a brilliant 1.0 that's achievable on time. Park the cut features as the 2.0 backlog.
4. Make day one productive. No time-outs, and keep the team in visible motion with weekly or daily progress.
5. Flag the warning signs: a month of strategy before work, multi-round vendor selection, "we'll decide after more input".
6. Make an exception for craft at the moment of truth. Rehearse and polish launches thoroughly.

**Output:** milestones, the 1.0 scope, the 2.0 parking lot, and the explicit pressure points.

## G. Handle "it can't be done" and expert or legal advice

1. **Get it precise.** Exactly what blocks it, and where does the rule come from: physics, law, contract or habit?
2. **Separate impossible from improbable.** Ask what it *would* take: money, a different method, a different supplier, a senior call.
3. **Use your own common sense.** Does the claim survive comparison with what the same party does every day (e.g. a magazine produces a whole issue in days)?
4. **Shop it.** If one vendor or team can't, ask another.
5. **For expert risk advice,** write it out: the risk, its likelihood and cost, the strategic upside forgone, and the reversibility. Decide as the owner of the whole picture, not as the risk owner.
6. **Before relaying a "no" upward,** probe it yourself. Passing on a "no" that turns out to be wrong costs you credibility.
7. **Accept the good "no"s.** When the constraint exposes a flawed idea, drop the idea gracefully.

**Output:** the challenged constraint, the options found, and a recommendation with the risk trade-off spelled out.

## H. Champion an idea through an organisation

1. Rate the stakes. If the idea's survival is at risk, plan for overwhelming force, not a fair fight (Element 10).
2. Line up allies beforehand so you speak as a chorus.
3. Bring the strongest evidence, and nothing weak that could dilute it (Element 1).
4. Get senior air cover early to clear process logjams before they form.
5. Present your own work to the highest decider. Avoid proxies.
6. If the idea is rejected but you still believe in it, keep the debate alive. Make it real (e.g. shoot a sample, build a prototype, silk-screen the name onto a model) and re-present it next to new options.
7. Frame a focusing enemy (the problem, the status quo, a proxy competitor) if the audience needs one target.

**Output:** a stakeholder map, an evidence pack outline, a sequence of conversations, and the fallback re-presentation plan.

---

## I. Respond to a mistake or failed launch

1. Admit it quickly (internally first, then publicly as appropriate). Don't defend old problems.
2. Fix it or kill it quickly (puck mouse → new mouse, G4 Cube discontinued within a year).
3. Redirect attention to the next improvement.
4. Assess the brand bank balance: how much trust can absorb this withdrawal? Plan deposits (a strong product or campaign) to rebuild it.

**Output:** an acknowledgement message, the fix or kill decision, and the next deposit.

---

## J. Audit an organisation or team for Complexity

Score each item 0 (healthy) to 2 (severe) and cite evidence:

| Element | Probe |
|---|---|
| Brutal | Is feedback direct and first-hand? Is work shipped that people don't believe in? |
| Small | Average meeting size? Committees? Does the decider see work early and unfiltered? |
| Minimal | How many products or tiers, and can a rep explain them? How many points per message? |
| Motion | Time from brief to live? How long does work sit idle? Are decisions re-opened? |
| Iconic | Is there one image or symbol people associate with the offering? |
| Phrasal | Is there a naming system? Model numbers? Jargon in customer copy? |
| Casual | Ratio of deck production to thinking? Length of briefing documents? |
| Human | Specs or benefits? Metrics as the only judge? Focus-group vetoes? |
| Skeptic | Are expert "no"s challenged? Who owns the final trade-off? |
| War | Do idea owners present to deciders? Is there a clear competitive focus? |

Then note the **all-or-nothing check**: fixing only the worst element rarely holds, so propose a sequenced plan covering all of them, led by a named champion. Roll it out through participatory sessions, not mandates.

**Output:** the scorecard, the top 3 interventions, the champion, and a rollout plan.
