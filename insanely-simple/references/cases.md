# Case library

These are the book's stories, paraphrased and tagged by element. Use them as analogies when persuading. **Don't** present them as verified history beyond "per Segall's account". Format: **Name** [elements] — what happened → the lesson.

## Introduction
- **The Simple Stick: one product, one box** [3] — The packaging team designed boxes for two versions of one product. Jobs: combine them, one product, one box. The conversation took minutes. → The simplest option is often skipped because nobody questions the premise of the project.
- **Zune points** [3, 8] — Microsoft made people buy points in bundles at 80 points per dollar to purchase a 99-cent song. → Complexity can override brains and common sense even at smart companies.

## Ch 1 — Think Brutal
- **"TV good, print bad"** [1] — At their first meeting, Jobs told the author warmly that his Apple TV ads were great and his print ads were bad. → Blunt, malice-free honesty makes the relationship simple: you know where you stand.
- **The B team** [1] — Out of sympathy, the author kept a team's "good enough" ads in a Jobs review. Jobs spotted them instantly and asked whether the B team had made them. → Compromising standards costs credibility and makes your life more complicated.
- **NeXT woodcut ads and report cards** [1] — The agency showed NeXT deliberately "untechy" woodcut-style ads. Jobs rejected them. The next week he said everyone had known it was bad work and it must never happen again, then graded each agency person aloud (A, B+, F, A−). → Never show work you don't believe in. Explicit standing beats ambiguity.
- **"You're overhead"** [1, 2] — Jobs joked that an account manager was overhead. → He respected doers, not handlers, and was willing to kill spending, even a $1M+ shoot, when strategy shifted.
- **The Nike account director** [1] — Jobs told a candidate from a thriving account that his old job had been not to mess things up. → Maintaining success and rescuing a troubled brand are different jobs.
- **The rotating turret** [1] — Staff watched Jobs's attention "lock on" to whoever said something foolish. → A fact of life at Apple, but people survived it, and the hard reset next meeting meant one slip didn't define you.
- **Mac clones and *Pirates of Silicon Valley*** [1] — Jobs absorbed fierce criticism for killing the clones and shrugged off an unflattering TV movie. → Thick skin lets you act on conviction.

## Ch 2 — Think Small
- **Lorrie ejected** [2] — An unfamiliar attendee was politely dismissed at the start of a meeting because she wasn't necessary. → No mercy invitations. Enforce small groups, and explain why rather than being cruel.
- **Jay Chiat: "Go create something"** [2] — The agency founder threw the author out of a meeting to go do actual work. → Makers belong at the work, not in talk.
- **Mac team cap and Top 100** [2] — The Mac team was capped at about 100 people (add one, remove one) so Jobs could know everyone. An annual Top 100 offsite aligned leaders. → Size limits preserve a start-up culture at scale. "No committees."
- **Jobs saw work first** [2] — No one, not even the VP of marketing, could filter agency work before Jobs saw it. → Decider involvement without filtering preserves sparks others would kill.
- **Intel quarterly report cards** [1, 2] — Formal two-way relationship report cards. → A substitute for real-time honesty that big groups produce.
- **"We can't have an opinion until we know Intel's opinion"** [2, 1] — An account director blocked a unanimous team recommendation. → Large-group politics replaces honesty with calculation.
- **No testing / "That's something we can't do"** [2, 8] — A VP asked how Apple produced great work. Told "we test nothing", she said they couldn't do that and asked what else. → Institutionalised process stops companies from changing even when the answer is obvious.
- **Intel "zero-defect advertising"** [2, 3] — The CEO demanded ads with zero defects. → Treating risk as a defect drives toward mediocrity. Efficiency in fabs isn't efficiency in ideas.
- **Better idea mid-production** [2] — At Apple, a better idea found during the shoot could go straight back to Jobs. At Intel, once something was socialised, tested and approved, it was frozen. → A better idea is a better idea. Process must not outrank it.
- **Pixar asked Jobs to skip creative meetings** [2] — Jobs was "a genius who loved creativity" more than a creative genius. → You don't need creative skill to champion creativity, only the judgment to protect it from process.

## Ch 3 — Think Minimal
- **Ad-supported Mac OS 9** [3] — Jobs seriously considered a free OS with a 60-second startup ad and contextual ads (e.g. buy ink in the print dialog), then dropped it. → Focus means saying no to good-sounding ideas. Even champions are tempted.
- **The 1998 four-quadrant grid** [3, 5] — Jobs cut a sprawling lineup (Quadra, Performa, LC, …) to consumer/pro × desktop/portable. → One simple chart served as both strategy and roadmap, saved millions and inspired engineers.
- **Advice to Nike** [3] — Asked for advice, Jobs said Nike made beautiful products but also a lot of junk, and should drop the junk. → Cut the weak to elevate the strong.
- **HP 23+ and Dell 18 laptop models; mystery shopping** [3] — Dell's reps couldn't explain the differences and gave conflicting recommendations. → Too much choice destroys confident buying. Apple's two lines plus configuration won satisfaction rankings.
- **Dell's divisions compete with themselves** [3] — The Consumer and Commercial divisions each had their own P&L, line and goals, with near-duplicate laptops. → Organisational structure produces product complexity.
- **The Michael Dell fantasy** [3] — The author imagines Dell cutting 50 models to 6 in a televised address. → Minimising requires audacity and will upset people.
- **iMac seamless corners** [3] — Jobs proudly showed that three plastic pieces had become one, a detail no customer would notice. → Minimising every detail is a matter of pride.
- **iPad's "missing" features** [3] — Rivals added the ports and slots the critics demanded. Those tablets didn't sell. → The subtractions were the appeal.
- **Intel's focus-group pipeline** [3, 8] — Test three campaigns in several cities → produce the winner (plus a backup) → retest → revise → air → re-retest. Frame-by-frame memorability graphs. → Microanalysis consumes money and pushes work toward mediocrity.
- **Saturn "Sheet Metal"** [3, 9] — Goodby's people-as-cars ad bombed in focus groups. The client overruled the research and it succeeded. → Protect good ideas from research.
- **Paper balls** [3] — Lee Clow threw one paper ball (caught: a good ad), then five (none caught: a bad ad). Jobs agreed to a single-feature iMac spot. → One message lands and many don't.
- **Intel's four titles plus four end cards** [3] — Every stakeholder got their logo or message into the spot. → Simplicity can't be added in the final edit.
- **Leopard vs Vista / Windows 7** [3] — One $129 version for everyone, against four Vista editions at $200–400. Later a $30 Lion against Windows 7 at $200–300. → One clear option at a fair price builds trust. Tiers feel like being squeezed.

## Ch 4 — Think Motion
- **The agency review killed** [4, 2] — A 23-agency, months-long review was in progress. Jobs scrapped it, called Lee Clow, and Chiat had work in weeks. → Urgency and context justify cutting process. Bet on a small trusted group.
- **iPod and iPhone 1.0** [4] — Music only; no third-party apps at launch. → A realistic, brilliant 1.0 that ships on time, with the cut features seeding 2.0. Leadership renews each cycle.
- **Apple vs Dell brand campaigns** [4, 2] — Apple (1997): immediate start, CEO-led, small smart group, CEO-only approval, a month to Think different. Dell (2008): a committee of rival divisions (they'd been sent to counseling), every division must approve, a month only on strategy, and a closet of unused boards. → A fractured, leaderless group with no urgent mandate can't be simplified.
- **Microsoft's search for its values** [4, 8] — During the DOJ case an executive asked whether the company's values were written down anywhere. It took 18 months to codify them, and then a pollster repositioned the brand as "kids, puppies and small businesses". → A company that loses motion and identity hands its voice to outsiders. People end up presenting ideas they don't believe in.

## Ch 5 — Think Iconic
- **Jobs saddened by Apple's 1993 losses** [8] — Told about Apple's record loss, Jobs was quietly sad, not vengeful. → His attachment to the company's soul.
- **Think different** [5, 3] — Two words (by art director Craig Tanimoto) plus black-and-white portraits of heroes, a line that could have hung in the garage in 1976. Jobs's internal launch: marketing is about values. In a noisy world people will remember one thing, so be clear what it is (Nike and "Got Milk?" as models). → Show who you are through whom you honour. Values are constant.
- **The Think different media plan** [5, 3] — Only back covers, prime billboards, backlit bus shelters, building walls. One product focus per quarter. → Say one thing loudly. Placement is part of the icon.
- **iMac pays off the brand** [5] — After six months of Think different, the translucent iMac embodied it, and product ads became brand ads. Apple never needed a separate brand campaign again. → Products as the personification of the brand.
- **iPhone's one button** [5] — Three buttons for phone, web and iPod would have been "almost perfect". One button won. → Even a subconscious visual cue sells simplicity.
- **iDVD on the whiteboard** [5, 7] — Jobs ignored the team's mockups and drew one window: drag the video in, click "Burn". → The interface reduced to its essence.
- **The "iClod" iPod ad, then Silhouettes** [5, 8, 2] — A cringey "cool guy" dancing ad misfired. Silhouettes (no product shot, no faces) became iconic. Jobs first rejected it ("not Apple") and came around later. → Sell the emotion. Untested misfires are an acceptable cost. Keep debating ideas you believe in.
- **"The hard way vs the easy way" vs Mac vs PC** [5, 8] — Literal comparison ads didn't land. Personifying the platforms did. → Icons, not diagrams.
- **HAL and Y2K** [5, 10] — Macs had no Y2K issue (dates fine until the year 29,940). A Super Bowl spot slowly zoomed into HAL's red eye. Douglas Adams contributed a self-deprecating web headline. → One menacing image captures a complex problem.

## Ch 6 — Think Phrasal
- **MacMan → iMac** [6, 10] — Jobs liked "MacMan". His brief: it must say Mac, suggest the internet, and not sound like a toy or a portable. He rejected the first five names ("I hate them all"). The author re-presented "iMac" alongside new names. Jobs "didn't hate it this week", silk-screened it onto a model, polled people and adopted it. → Constraints, persistence and re-presentation. The "i" built a franchise.
- **The naming architecture** [6] — Mac*, i* + a descriptive word, stable names across generations, iPod touch/nano/shuffle. This contrasts with Dell's Inspiron/Vostro/OptiPlex and phones named Citrus, Fascinate and so on. → Consistency makes each name reinforce the brand. People use single words.
- **Mobilium → Centrino** [6, 9] — Intel built a mobile marketing plan with a blank space for the name. It dropped the clever "-ium" family name (Mobilium), probably over legal fear, and paid a naming firm for "Centrino" with a butterfly badge (which the chairman said looked like his prostate). → Name first. Don't let legal timidity kill a systematic name.
- **iPhone, the obvious name** [6] — It covered only one of three functions, but it was instantly clear. → Obviousness creates understanding and buzz. Usage expands the meaning.
- **Final Cut Studio: "put the software in the box"** [3, 6] — A manager planned a premium "Color" edition (copying Adobe's edition model) so the CFO could track the acquisition. Jobs: one product, period, and it was a mistake. → Don't lust after a competitor's marketing. Internal accounting is no reason for customer complexity.
- **apple.com and URLs** [6, 8] — Jobs rejected whizzy animation as "lazy" and refused campaign-specific URLs: people will go to apple.com, so put the link there. → Serve customers, not tracking.
- **The 1 a.m. copy calls** [6] — An hour of line-by-line edits of a 16-page insert, including "a" vs "the", with horse-trading ("you got the last one, I'll take this one"). → No unimportant details. Collegial debate.
- **The NeXT name** [6] — Jobs wanted to call it "Two". Designer Tom Suiter suggested "Next" after hearing Bill Gates use the word repeatedly in a speech. → Simultaneously autobiographical and visionary.

## Ch 7 — Think Casual
- **Hank's brand audit** [7, 8] — A new planner presented foam-core charts and demographic research (including asking kids in Harlem). Jobs slumped, heckled and cut it off: "let's look at some ads". He later phoned to say he wouldn't pay for it. → Slick and formal suggests fluff. Say in three sentences what you'd put on twenty slides.
- **"Just show me the ads!"** [7] — The author began a strategy recap with the ads face down. Jobs flipped them over himself and asked whether the author would be sitting beside him to explain them when he read the paper. → The work must stand alone. Setups feel manipulative.
- **Apple briefings vs Dell's 32-person day** [7, 2] — Apple: small, interactive, a 1–2 page brief, often from Jobs at the whiteboard. Dell: an all-day briefing for 32 people, Midwest focus groups, and in the end old ads with a holiday graphic. → Big productions serve the presenters, not the attendees.
- **Keynote structure** [7] — Agenda → facts → topic summaries → overall summary, with key lines repeated. Rehearsed for weeks, delivered like a conversation. → Formality only where it belongs, and simple even there.

## Ch 8 — Think Human
- **iMovie made Jobs cry / "Crying Man"** [8] — Jobs's home movie of his kids moved him. The agency's ad of a weeping father read as grief without context. A VP's family movie set to "Forever Young" ran instead, the first genuine Dylan recording used in an ad. → Emotion is powerful, but context matters.
- **iPod + iTunes** [8, 3] — Managing the library in iTunes rather than on the device was the insight competitors missed. → Moving complexity off the user's touchpoint is the human move.
- **Pixar's atrium** [8, 2] — Jobs designed the building so people would meet by chance, and saw innovation in ad hoc hallway meetings. → Process brings efficiency, people bring innovation.
- **The Crazy Ones voice-over** [8] — Jobs recorded a reluctant read (three takes) but chose Richard Dreyfuss so that he wouldn't distract from the message. He contributed the line about pushing the human race forward. → Ego takes second place to the message.
- **Jobs's three humbling events** [8] — Ousted in 1985, NeXT's long struggle, the cancer diagnosis. → They deepened his humanity and focus. Mortality strips away fear of embarrassment.
- **"1,000 songs in your pocket"** [8, 6] — Not storage specs. → Human-speak.
- **The Apple II brochure and the 100-uses foldout** [8] — "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication" (1977), and a foldout of a hundred things a personal computer could do. → Human voice from day one. A large volume of content can still carry a simple concept.
- **Mac vs PC (66 ads, 4 years)** [8, 10] — Aggressive but friendly, with the platforms played by people. Jobs rejected the scripts until the agency shot real samples over a weekend. → Competitive without arrogance. Make it real to win approval.
- **RIM PlayBook** [8, 3] — "Amateur hour is over" business positioning, yet ads showed games, and it shipped without native email. It shipped 200K units in the quarter Apple sold 9.25M iPads. → Inconsistent strategy versus selling to humankind.
- **Dell metrics vs Apple delight** [8] — Click-goal bonuses versus head-and-heart decisions. Faster horses, and "people don't know what they want until you show them". → Data in context.

## Ch 9 — Think Skeptic
- **Overruling the lawyers on the anti-Intel ad** [9] — Late legal objections to a benchmark ad. Jobs's one-line email dismissed the lawyers, the ad ran and nothing happened. → Advice, not orders.
- **iPhone vs Cisco** [9, 6] — Cisco held the iPhone trademark. Jobs launched without signing the agreement, the dispute settled quickly, and the name became priceless. → Weigh long-term value against short-term legal risk.
- **Willy Wonka millionth iMac** [9] — Jobs wanted a golden certificate in a box and to greet the winner in costume. Sweepstakes law ("no purchase necessary") killed it. → Sometimes the skeptical lawyer saves you.
- **Intel "Yes"** [9, 2] — A guest creative pitched owning the word "yes" to an over-expanded group. A German claimant was paid off cheaply, then legal killed the campaign anyway. → Complexity both birthed the campaign and killed it.
- **The Time insert in two weeks** [9] — The author relayed "impossible". Jobs called that nonsense and hinted at a rival agency. Begging and pleading got it done. → Probe every "no" before relaying it.
- **Dell Adamo** [9, 3] — An executive brought in ex-Motorola engineers to halve the schedule, then the $2,500 laptop died of online-only sales, mismatched fashion ads and other complexity. → Refusing "no" alone isn't simplicity. It's all-or-nothing.
- **Aluminium molds** [9, 1] — A vendor said a tiny Styrofoam insert melted and the box must change. Apple told the vendor to change its molds instead, and it did. → "Can't" usually means "costly". Details matter even unnoticed.
- **Apple Stores** [9, 8] — CompUSA's store-within-a-store failed (back corner, indifferent staff). Pundits predicted failure within two years. Premium materials, consistent design: 350+ stores, more revenue per square foot than Tiffany, more than half of visitors new to the Mac. → Control the experiences that define you, and ignore the naysayers.

## Ch 10 — Think War
- **Snail / Burning Bunny / Steamroller** [10] — An underdog openly invited an Intel lawsuit for publicity. Intel only posted counter-benchmarks, which kept Macs in the conversation. → An enemy focuses attention, and a proxy enemy attacks a whole category.
- **The Microsoft truce (1997)** [10] — Apple had to stop thinking that for it to win Microsoft had to lose: "the desktop war is over and we lost". Gates invested $150M and committed Office. Jobs later called the giant-Gates video backdrop his worst staging mistake. → Choose (and drop) enemies deliberately. Mind the imagery.
- **Michael Dell's bull's-eye** [10] — After Dell said Apple should shut down and give the money back, Jobs showed Dell's photo with a target over it. → An enemy that volunteers rallies the troops.
- **Mandela via Clinton** [10] — Mandela's office said no. Jobs asked the sitting US president to intercede, and the answer was "maybe after he leaves office". → Use every weapon. No holding back.
- **iPhone vs BlackBerry, the perfect storm** [10, 8] — A handheld track record (iPod/iTunes), frustrated phone customers, and an incumbent with deeply buried features. → Simplicity itself is the ultimate weapon.
- **Peeling the onion** [10] — Jobs said first solutions are complex and most people stop there. → Keep going past "it works" to the elegant version.

## Conclusion
- **The hockey puck mouse** [1, conclusion] — A round mouse you couldn't orient. The author told Jobs it insulted pro users. Jobs joked dismissively, then laughed and described the replacement already in progress. → Admit mistakes quickly and look forward.
- **The G4 Cube** — A MoMA-worthy design that couldn't hit a consumer price. Killed within a year. → A beautiful but wrong product gets fixed fast.
- **Antennagate and the brand bank** — Heavy press criticism, and customers stayed. → A healthy brand bank absorbs withdrawals.
- **The $80B cushion** — Jobs said cash reserves freed Apple to make imaginative leaps. → Bold simplicity is easier with insurance, so calibrate to your own.
