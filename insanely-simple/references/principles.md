# The Ten Elements of Simplicity, in depth

Each element has the same layout: **Claim**, **Why it works**, **Complexity smells**, **Moves**, **Evidence from the book**, **Limits**. Case names in *italics* are in `cases.md`.

Foundations that apply to all ten:
- **The undeniable truth:** given the choice, any sane person (any organism, the author jokes) picks the simpler path. As simplicity gets scarcer in a complicated world, it gets more valuable.
- **Simplicity = Brains + Common Sense.** Both are common, but Complexity regularly overrides them (*Zune points*).
- **Complexity is the default and never dies.** It moves into hierarchies, goals and colleagues, and even into champions of simplicity (Jobs nearly chose an ad-supported OS and the name "MacMan").
- **All-or-nothing.** Copying one Apple habit inside a complex culture rarely works (*Dell Adamo*, *Intel "can't stop testing"*).
- **It needs a champion** with both head and heart, willing to step on toes. The simpler way often costs more time, money and energy up front, and usually wins.

## 1. Think Brutal: honesty and standards that don't bend

**Claim.** Blunt is simple and meandering is complex. Say exactly what you think, and demand the same from others. Never present, approve or defend work you don't believe in 100%.

**Why it works.**
- Clarity keeps an organisation moving. When people don't have to decode feedback, all of the team's time goes to forward progress.
- Varnished or relayed truth ("the CEO hated it" when he had small fixable notes) sends teams back to rework the wrong things. Complexity gets in through the middleman's agenda.
- Unresolved standing ("where do I stand?") makes people look over their shoulders instead of ahead.
- Compromising on standards plants disappointment, extra work and more meetings. Worst of all, it leaves you defending an idea you never believed in. The one thing nobody can take from you is integrity.

**Complexity smells.**
- Feedback softened until it no longer says anything, or filtered through intermediaries.
- "Good enough" work kept in a review out of sympathy for the team that made it (*the B team*).
- Showing work you know is weak and hoping for the best (*NeXT woodcut ads*).
- Formal periodic "report cards" standing in for real-time candour (*Intel quarterly report cards*).
- Grudges carried from one meeting to the next.

**Moves.**
- Give direct, specific verdicts: "This doesn't work because X." Don't spend paragraphs on padding.
- Curate ruthlessly before anything goes up the chain: cut whatever you wouldn't be proud to ship.
- Do a **hard reset** between meetings. Judge each situation on its merits, and let people win back credibility by showing their worth.
- Tell people where they stand, good and bad, in real time.
- Grow a thick skin. Absorb what's useful in criticism and compartmentalise the rest (*Mac clones backlash*, *Pirates of Silicon Valley*).

**Evidence.** *First meeting: "TV good, print bad"*, *the B team*, *NeXT woodcut ads and the report-card meeting*, *"you're overhead"*, *Nike account director interview*, *the rotating turret*.

**Limits.** Being brutally honest is not the same as being brutal. The book separates them explicitly: Jobs was also funny, warm and charming. When you advise people, recommend candour without contempt.

## 2. Think Small: small groups of smart people

**Claim.** Start with small groups of smart people and keep them small. Everyone in the room is there for a reason, and there are no "mercy invitations". Keep the ultimate decision maker involved from the briefing onward.

**The two laws of small** (the author's tongue-in-cheek "science"):
1. The quality of a project's output is **inversely proportional** to the number of people involved.
2. The quality of a project's output **rises in direct proportion** to how involved the ultimate decision maker is.

**Why it works.**
- More people means harder briefings, more hand-holding, more review cycles and more diluted ideas. More brains only occasionally means better ideas.
- Adding people to a critical project signals that you don't trust the people already on it. If that's true, fix the team instead of padding it.
- Small groups move fast, get information fast and react to surprises. They also build durable relationships (*Apple–Chiat partnership*) because both sides know where they stand.
- A decision maker who sees work only at the end produces wasted weeks and mediocrity. Jobs insisted on seeing creative work *before* anyone filtered it, so that nobody guessed what he'd like and he could spot sparks others would have cut.
- Flat structure: when the question is simply "does the decider like it?", next steps are clear. In layered organisations, each approval only earns you the next meeting. Lower levels ask for more volume to cover themselves and avoid bold positions (*Intel layered approvals*).

**Complexity smells.**
- Spectators in meetings, and invitations sent "so they feel included".
- Committees (Jobs: Apple had zero, and was "the biggest start-up on the planet").
- "We can't have an opinion until we know the client's opinion" (*Euro/Intel email*).
- Divisions with separate P&Ls and goals that compete with each other (*Dell Consumer vs Commercial*).
- Process loved for its own sake: "zero-defect advertising", or framed "how to run a meeting" signs.
- An approved idea that can't be improved once in production because "the process is set".

**Moves.**
- For every attendee, ask: what decision or contribution needs this person? Remove anyone without one, and explain why so it doesn't feel brutal.
- Cap team size. Jobs's Mac-team rule was about 100 people, small enough to know everyone personally: to add one, remove one.
- Put the decider in the briefing and at regular checkpoints. If the decider truly can't engage, delegate *real* authority to someone who can.
- Strip review layers. Ask of each step: does it improve the idea, or only protect someone?
- Let better ideas override approved plans. "A better idea is a better idea."
- A tongue-in-cheek meeting rule: remove the least necessary person, walk out after 30 minutes, then do something productive to make up for it.

**Evidence.** *Lorrie ejected*, *Jay Chiat: "Go create something"*, *Mac team cap of 100 / Top 100*, *no ad testing at Apple*, *Intel zero-defect*, *Euro/Intel opinion email*, *Apple vs Dell brand campaign*.

**Limits.** "Small" is relative to the business, and *smart* is non-negotiable: a small group of the wrong people fails faster (*Dell's "small group of incompatible people"*). Be realistic about where the line sits.

## 3. Think Minimal: focus means saying no

**Claim.** Focus means saying no to the hundred other good ideas. Remove the extraneous from products, lineups, messages and processes. When in doubt, minimise.

**Why it works.**
- **Lineups:** too much choice stops being a benefit and becomes a liability. It slows decisions, sends buyers to experts and creates post-purchase angst. Distinct models with obvious differences, then customisation, beat 20+ overlapping SKUs (*HP/Dell laptops vs MacBook Air/Pro*). Fewer, premium products also concentrate R&D, marketing and quality.
- **Audiences:** sell to *people*, not segments. One message lets customers self-select (*Apple single P&L vs Dell divisions*).
- **Messages:** people catch one ball and drop five (*Lee Clow's paper balls*). Say one thing loudly rather than a hundred things quietly.
- **Processes:** every layer waters down the work. Minimal processes protect good ideas from people who would damage them.
- **Pricing and editions:** one version at a fair price builds trust. Multiple tiers at inflated prices feel like being squeezed (*Leopard vs Vista editions*).

**Complexity smells.**
- Product or tier proliferation that sales reps can't explain.
- Tiers created for internal accounting or to copy a competitor (*Final Cut Studio "Color" edition*).
- A message that has to satisfy every stakeholder: four feature titles plus four end cards (*Intel spot*).
- An ad-supported or monetised layer bolted onto a clean experience (*Mac OS 9 ad-supported idea*).
- Test → retest → re-retest loops.

**Moves.**
- Draw a **2×2** (or the smallest grid) of genuinely distinct needs and map every offering onto it. Kill or merge anything that overlaps (*the 1998 four-quadrant grid*).
- "Get rid of the crappy stuff and focus on the good stuff" (Jobs to Nike, paraphrased).
- For any message: pick the single most compelling point and present it compellingly. If you must deliver several, find a unifying theme and push it hard.
- One focus per period: Apple ran one product focus per quarter, on the home page and in nearly all ads.
- Be as proud of what you didn't do as of what you did.
- Minimise from the start. Simplicity can't rescue a cluttered piece in the final edit.

**Evidence.** *Mac OS 9 ads idea*, *1998 product grid*, *Nike advice*, *HP/Dell proliferation and mystery shopping*, *iMac seamless corners*, *iPad "missing features"*, *Intel focus-group pipeline*, *Saturn "Sheet Metal"*, *paper balls*, *Leopard vs Vista*, *Michael Dell fantasy*.

**Limits.** Expanding a lineup is fine. Making it *complicated* is not: every product needs a distinct, easily understood reason to exist. Minimising often takes audacity, because it upsets people.

## 4. Think Motion: urgency and the smart timeline

**Claim.** Simplicity never stands still. Plan for slightly less time than feels comfortable. Keep the team in constant motion. Aim realistically high, and ship a great 1.0 that points the way to 2.0.

**Why it works.**
- Too much time is the easiest way to ruin a project. It invites second-guessing, extra opinions, tests and new participants. Bernstein's line applies: great things need a plan and not quite enough time.
- In the author's experience, about **three months** from briefing to live work was the sweet spot for technology marketing. Less hurt quality and more invited overthinking. Every field has its own number, so find yours.
- Urgency justifies cutting process: Jobs scrapped a 23-agency review and hired Chiat in weeks.
- 1.0 discipline (the iPod was only a music player, the iPhone launched without third-party apps) keeps schedules honest. Cut features become the 2.0 starting point, so leadership renews each cycle.
- Leaders keep products emotional and benefit-led, which leaves competitors stuck in spec-sheet battles.

**Complexity smells.**
- Multi-round selection processes ("meet and greets" → 5 finalists → shoot-out).
- A month of strategy talk before any work starts (*Dell brand campaign*).
- Organisations that stop "doing things" and start debating, then spend 18 months codifying values (*Microsoft after the DOJ*).
- Brand positioning outsourced to non-marketers ("kids, puppies and small business").
- Projects with time-outs, where the team isn't consumed from day one.

**Moves.**
- Set deadlines with a bit of pressure built in. Distrust the "comfortable" timeline.
- Make day one count: brief, then start working immediately.
- Scope 1.0 to what you can do brilliantly on time. Neither overreach nor underreach.
- A company should know who it is before it needs to say it. Values don't need a document if the leader can brief from the heart, and a company that can't articulate itself has a deeper problem.

**Evidence.** *Agency review killed*, *Think different in a month vs Dell's closet of boards*, *Microsoft values search*, *iPod/iPhone 1.0*.

**Limits.** Motion isn't recklessness. Apple's launches were rehearsed exhaustively. Urgency applies to decisions and progress, not to skipping craft.

## 5. Think Iconic: one image that carries the idea

**Claim.** A single iconic image, symbol or design cue communicates faster and sticks longer than many words. Find the conceptual image that *captures the essence* of your idea. Don't decorate.

**Why it works.**
- Think different needed only a black-and-white portrait, the logo and two words, and readers took it in instantly. You show what you stand for through whom you admire.
- One button on the iPhone *says* simplicity before anyone touches it. Three buttons would have been "almost perfect", and "almost" isn't allowed.
- Iconic product imagery makes every product ad a brand ad, so a separate brand campaign becomes unnecessary (*iMac after Think different*).
- Iconifying an abstract enemy lets you attack without looking mean: Mac vs PC turned platforms into people, and HAL embodied Y2K.
- Media placement is part of being iconic: back covers, prime billboards and whole building walls, never in places that degrade the message.

**Complexity smells.**
- Slide decoration and stock photos that add nothing conceptual.
- Literal comparisons such as "the hard way vs the easy way" (logically right, emotionally flat).
- Forced "cool person" casting that alienates part of the audience (*the "iClod" iPod ad*).
- Multiple controls or entry points where one would do.

**Moves.**
- Ask: what single image, object or gesture *is* this idea? Can you reduce the interface to one window and one button (*iDVD whiteboard*)?
- Personify abstractions, competitors or problems into characters.
- Sell the emotion rather than the device when the device is only a means (*iPod Silhouettes*: no product shot, no faces, only joy).
- Place the icon only where it can shine.

**Evidence.** *Think different*, *iPhone one button*, *iDVD "one window, Burn"*, *iPod Silhouettes (Jobs first said "It's not Apple")*, *Mac vs PC*, *HAL Y2K spot*, *Think different media plan*.

**Limits.** Iconic work can feel "off brand" at first, and even Jobs initially rejected Silhouettes. Keep the debate going. Mistakes like "iClod" happen when you don't test, and Apple accepted that cost as cheaper than testing everything.

## 6. Think Phrasal: the power of words and names

**Claim.** Simplicity is at its most concentrated in words: product names, titles, headlines, URLs, single letters. The obvious, short, consistent choice usually wins.

**Why it works.**
- A name fits an essay's worth of meaning into a few characters. A good name tilts the odds toward success, and a bad one can hobble a product before launch.
- **Consistency** compounds: every Mac is a "Mac" and every consumer device is "i" + a descriptive word, so each new name reinforces the brand. Unrelated names (Inspiron, Vostro, OptiPlex) each have to fight their way into customers' vocabulary.
- **Singularity:** people refer to products by one word. Keep the name stable across generations ("iPhone") and use modifiers only for information.
- **Obviousness** is a feature. "iPhone" described only a third of the device, but nobody could mistake what was being disrupted, and use filled out the meaning.
- Simple URLs: send people to the home page and put the link there. Don't make customers do work so you can track them.

**Complexity smells.**
- Names that echo another company's identity, or that trigger misreadings ("MacMan" suggested both a toy and a portable).
- Alphanumeric model strings ("DVP SR200P/B").
- Names chosen because "this is the best we can get approved" before time ran out.
- Naming handed to an outside agency that doesn't know the culture, producing names "cool and legally safe" at the same time (Quadra, Performa).
- Marketing plans built with a blank where the name should go (*Intel "Mobilium" → "Centrino"*).
- Editions named to dodge a decision ("Platinum Edition").

**Moves.**
- Write the naming brief as constraints: it must say X, must not suggest Y, must fit the family system.
- Generate widely, then present a small shortlist. Keep re-presenting a strong option alongside new ones ("as long as you have new ideas, you can re-show the old one").
- Prefer the obvious descriptive word plus your brand marker.
- Settle the name before the marketing plan.
- Edit copy word by word ("a" vs "the"), because no detail is unimportant. Then cut again: every paragraph can lose a few more words.

**Evidence.** *MacMan → iMac*, *iPhone name and the Cisco trademark*, *Final Cut Studio "put the software in the box"*, *Mobilium/Centrino*, *NeXT name (from "Two")*, *the apple.com URL rule*, *late-night copy calls*, *iMovie name debated for weeks*.

**Limits.** "Obvious" can look *too easy* to a reviewer, the curse of simplicity. Explain the reasoning. Taking legal risk for a name (iPhone) is a judgment call, not a rule.

## 7. Think Casual: conversations, not productions

**Claim.** Shun the trappings of big companies. Use a table, a whiteboard and honest talk. Show the work instead of the preamble, and brief in one or two pages, not stacks of printouts.

**Why it works.**
- A slick deck makes an executive suspect you're padding thin facts, and the time spent wrapping the idea was time not spent thinking it through.
- Long setups feel manipulative ("Are you going to sit next to me and explain it when I read it in the paper?"). Work has to stand on its own, as it will in the world.
- Brevity reads as intelligence. More words make you seem less smart, not more.
- Formal presentations build walls. Ending with "Any questions?" doesn't create conversation, and relationships are built in debate.
- Good ideas often come out of informal briefings, where key phrases surface in conversation.

**Complexity smells.**
- Foam-core charts and demographic slides presented to a decider who wanted three sentences (*Hank's brand audit*).
- All-day briefings for 30+ people that end with "run last year's ads with a holiday graphic" (*Dell's 32-person meeting*).
- Ads presented face down with a strategy recap first.

**Moves.**
- Lead with the work or the answer. Give context only when asked.
- Let the decider brief people directly. A CEO at a whiteboard is the most efficient briefing there is.
- Keep a template, but make it a simple one: give the small group exactly what it needs to do top-quality work.
- Save formal choreography for public launches. Even there, keep slides simple: agenda, facts, summary, and repeat the key point.

**Evidence.** *Hank's brand audit*, *"Just show me the ads!"*, *Apple product briefings vs Dell/Intel/IBM megabriefings*, *Jobs's keynote structure*.

**Limits.** Casual doesn't mean unprepared. Jobs rehearsed keynotes for weeks. Informality is about the *format* of collaboration, not the effort behind it.

## 8. Think Human: speak to people, decide with head and heart

**Claim.** The most powerful simplicity connects to our humanity. Talk in human terms and benefits, not specs. Use data, but judge with head and heart. Don't design or approve by focus group.

**Why it works.**
- "1,000 songs in your pocket" instead of storage specs: people talk in benefits.
- Removing complexity is a human act. The iPod won because the music library lived in iTunes, not on the device. Other players tried to do everything on the device and became unusable.
- Leaps of about **10x** get noticed; incremental improvements don't. A new concept must be graspable in a second and usable immediately.
- Humanity is about **tone** (words plus images), not literally showing humans. Staged babies and puppies can feel *less* authentic.
- Competitive messaging can be aggressive without arrogance if it's good-natured (*Mac vs PC: 66 ads*).
- Sell to humankind and let people self-select. Business buyers are humans too (*RIM PlayBook "Amateur hour is over" vs iPad*).
- Analytics in context. Dell optimises clicks and bonuses, while Apple optimises delight and earns far more. People often don't know what they want until they see it, so a "faster horse" brief is a poor guide.

**Complexity smells.**
- Spec-sheet comparisons (megapixels, ports, MHz).
- "Make it more human" turned into "add stock photos of people".
- Ideas sanded down to offend no one, which strips their character.
- KPIs that override customer delight.
- Simplicity by proclamation: T-shirts, manifestos and slogans with no change to culture or incentives.

**Moves.**
- Rewrite every feature as what a person can now *do* or *feel*.
- Ask: is this an order-of-magnitude improvement, or does it create a new category? Can someone grasp it in one sentence?
- Collect all the data, then decide with judgment. Write down what the numbers can't capture.
- Reward acts of bravery that serve simplicity, and stay consistent long enough that the voice becomes culture.
- Admit and avoid arrogance: aggressive but friendly.

**Evidence.** *iMovie made Jobs cry / "Crying Man" misfire*, *iPod and iTunes insight*, *Apple II "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication" brochure and the 100-uses insert*, *Mac vs PC*, *RIM PlayBook*, *Pixar hallway collaboration*, *Jobs's three humbling events*.

**Limits.** Emotional ideas can backfire without context: "Crying Man" read as grief. And "no focus groups" assumes a strong internal judge. Apple's internal reviewers *were* that judge.

## 9. Think Skeptic: take advice, not orders

**Claim.** Treat expert opinion (legal, vendor, production, analyst) as input to weigh against your goals and common sense, not as a ruling. Expect the first answer to be "no", and probe it, because most "can't" means "it would take extra effort".

**Why it works.**
- Experts optimise their own risk domain and may not see the bigger picture. A leader weighs short-term cost against long-term benefit (*iPhone name: legal risk tiny next to the brand's value*).
- Most impossibilities are only improbabilities. When probed, vendors and partners find ways (*Time insert in two weeks*, *aluminium molds for a Styrofoam insert*).
- Relaying an unexamined "no" costs your credibility when someone else finds a way.
- Naysayers are often confidently wrong (*Apple Stores: "two years before they turn out the lights"*).
- Healthy skepticism cuts both ways: a lawyer's caution sometimes rightly kills a bad idea (*Willy Wonka millionth iMac*).

**Complexity smells.**
- Legal advice treated as a court order, or a campaign killed after a cheaply settled threat (*Intel "Yes"*).
- "That's not how we do things" offered as a reason.
- Redesigning the vision to suit a supplier's current equipment.
- Outsourcing a critical part of the customer experience to a party with no incentive to care (*CompUSA store-within-a-store*).

**Moves.**
- When you hear "no", ask: what exactly blocks this? What would it take? Who else could do it? Is this a law of physics or a habit?
- Weigh the expert's concern against the strategic upside, in writing: the risk, the cost if it happens, and the benefit forgone.
- If one vendor or person can't, try another.
- Control the experiences that define you. If a partner won't care, bring that piece in-house (*Apple Stores*).
- Accept useful "no"s gracefully.

**Evidence.** *"F— the lawyers" anti-Intel ad*, *iPhone vs Cisco*, *Willy Wonka*, *Intel "Yes" campaign killed*, *Time insert*, *Dell Adamo*, *Styrofoam molds*, *Apple Stores vs Gateway and CompUSA*.

**Limits.** Skepticism without the rest of simplicity fails. Adamo was built fast because an executive refused "no", then died of complex pricing, channel and messaging. Saying "never take no" is not a strategy.

## 10. Think War: champion ideas with overwhelming force

**Claim.** Simplicity is fragile and needs a fighter. When an idea's life is at stake, don't seek a fair fight: use every available weapon and overwhelming force, because a tie goes to Complexity. Picking an enemy creates focus.

**Why it works.**
- **An enemy focuses.** Attacking Intel (snail, burning bunny, steamroller) attacked the whole PC industry without naming a PC maker, and forced Macs into the conversation. Choose enemies deliberately and pick a proxy that stands for the whole category. Know when to drop one (Microsoft became a friend in 1997, when "for us to win, Microsoft has to lose" was the wrong frame).
- **Use every weapon.** Jobs asked a sitting US president to help recruit Mandela for Think different. It failed, but nothing was held back.
- **Represent your own work all the way to the top.** Intermediaries have less passion and will compromise or concede where you wouldn't.
- **Decisive wins set precedent**, and narrow ones invite another fight.
- **Simplicity is itself the ultimate weapon.** The iPhone was a "perfect storm": a proven handheld track record, frustrated customers in the category, and complex incumbents (BlackBerry menus).
- **Keep peeling the onion.** The first solutions to a problem are complex, and most people stop there. Living with the problem longer yields elegant ones.

**Complexity smells.**
- Sending a deputy to present your idea to the decider.
- Going into a key meeting without allies, evidence or executive air cover.
- Accepting the first workable solution as the final one.

**Moves.**
- Before a critical meeting: line up allies so you speak as a chorus, bring the decisive evidence, get a senior nod in advance, or bring in an outside voice for credibility.
- Present your own work to the highest level that will judge it.
- Frame a focusing enemy (a competitor, a problem or a status quo) when the audience needs one target.
- After a solution "works", do another pass and ask what else can go.
- Your strongest weapon is genuine passion for the idea.

**Evidence.** *Snail / Burning Bunny / Steamroller*, *Michael Dell bull's-eye*, *Microsoft truce 1997 and Mac vs PC 2006*, *Intel switch*, *Mandela via Clinton*, *iPhone vs BlackBerry*.

**Limits.** "War" is a metaphor for commitment, not permission for scorched-earth tactics against colleagues. Lawsuit bait (the snail ad) was a calculated bet by an underdog with little to lose.

## Closing principles (Conclusion)

- **Simplicity is always original.** It makes you the minority, which is why it differentiates.
- **Quality first.** A bad idea stays bad however simple it is. Simplicity does make recovery easier.
- **Admit mistakes quickly and move on.** The hockey puck mouse was replaced quickly, and the G4 Cube was killed within a year. Simplicity prefers looking forward to defending old problems.
- **Brand bank.** Good acts deposit trust and failures withdraw it. A high balance carries you through crises like Antennagate.
- **Simplicity isn't the goal. It's the guiding light** for reaching goals in every discipline.
- **Spread it by participation, not decree.** Run sessions where people contribute their own simplification ideas. A movement beats a mandate.
