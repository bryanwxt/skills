# Insanely Simple (simplicity lens)

**The thesis.** People prefer the simple path, so simplicity attracts. Complexity is its opposite force. It is the default, it is seductive, and it never dies. It comes back whenever nobody fights it. So simplicity needs a **champion**: someone who will "hit things with the Simple Stick", which means rejecting work that doesn't reduce an idea to its essence. Simplicity is a skill and a culture, not a layer you graft on. It is **all-or-nothing**: adopting one piece à la carte (fast schedules but no focus, say) still loses to Complexity.

**Two warnings that govern everything below:**
1. *A simple bad idea is still bad.* Simplicity amplifies quality. It doesn't create it. Check the idea first.
2. *Brutally honest ≠ brutal.* Copy the clarity, not the abuse. Every principle here can be carried out with respect.

## The ten elements (one line each)

| # | Element | Core rule | Complexity smell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Think Brutal** | Say exactly what you think. Hold standards that don't bend. | Varnished feedback, "good enough", defending work you don't believe in |
| 2 | **Think Small** | Use small groups of smart people. Keep the real decision maker involved throughout. | Mercy invites, committees, approval chains, decision maker appears only at the end |
| 3 | **Think Minimal** | Say no to the hundred other good ideas. One message, few choices. | Product/plan proliferation, ads with five points, tiers that exist for internal reasons |
| 4 | **Think Motion** | Plan for slightly less time than is comfortable. Never stop moving. Ship a great 1.0. | Leisurely timelines, re-opening decisions, months of process before work starts |
| 5 | **Think Iconic** | Find one image or symbol that carries the whole idea. | Decoration instead of concept, many visuals that all say little |
| 6 | **Think Phrasal** | Words and names matter. Pick the short, obvious, consistent one. | Jargon, model numbers, names approved because "time ran out" |
| 7 | **Think Casual** | Hold conversations, not productions. Show the work instead of the preamble. | 20 slides for 3 sentences, choreographed briefings, 32-person meetings |
| 8 | **Think Human** | Speak in human terms and benefits. Use data in context, never as the sole judge. | Specs instead of benefits, decisions by focus group or click-rate alone, "show a baby to look human" |
| 9 | **Think Skeptic** | Treat expert advice as advice, not orders. Probe every "no". | Legal or vendor "no" accepted unexamined, "we can't have an opinion until the client does" |
| 10 | **Think War** | Champion ideas with overwhelming force. A tie goes to Complexity. Pick a focusing enemy. | Letting a middleman present your work, half-hearted advocacy, even fights |

## Quick rule (small asks)

For a one-off "simplify this" or a fast review, don't open the references. Run these five questions and report the answers:
1. **What is the one thing?** Name it in one sentence. If you can't, that's the finding.
2. **What can go?** List every element (point, option, step, person, slide, tier) that doesn't serve the one thing.
3. **Who decides, and are they in the loop now?**
4. **What's being said in machine terms that could be said in human terms?**
5. **Which "can't" or "must" is an unexamined assumption?**

End with the simplified version and a short **Cut list** saying what was removed and why.

## Deeper work

Pick the file that matches the task:
- `references/principles.md`: each element in depth (mechanism, smells, moves, evidence, limits). Read it when you need to *argue* for a change, or when the quick rule isn't enough.
- `references/playbooks.md`: step-by-step procedures for message and deck simplification, lineup and pricing reduction, naming, team and process design, timeline setting, handling "no" and expert advice, championing an idea, recovering from mistakes, and auditing an organisation.
- `references/cases.md`: the book's stories as a case library, each tagged by principle. Use them for analogies and persuasion. Keep them paraphrased.
- `references/glossary.md`: the book's vocabulary (Simple Stick, brand bank, the two laws of small, and so on) plus a quote bank of short lines.

## Limits (say these when they apply)

- **Context matters.** Apple's no-testing, CEO-in-every-review model relied on top talent, a strong brand and large cash reserves (the "insurance" for bold bets). With weaker talent or no brand equity, keep the principle (trust a small expert group) and add cheap safeguards.
- **Survivorship and author bias.** The author was Apple's ad-agency creative director, and the evidence is anecdotal and marketing-centric. Treat the book as a strong heuristic, not proof.
- **Don't copy the bad parts.** Public humiliation and profanity worked (sometimes) for one founder with unique leverage. The principle that carries over is *clarity and non-negotiable standards*.
- **Simplicity for the customer can mean complexity for you.** Apple takes on internal work (record-keeping, custom molds, single-SKU logistics) so the customer's experience stays simple. Budget for that.
- **"Never take no" has a boundary.** Probe, don't bulldoze. Sometimes the impossible really is impossible. Dell's Adamo hit its schedule and still failed because the rest was complex.
