# Performance presentation standards (Chapter 6, Appendices C–E)

> **Dated material.** This reflects GIPS as of 2004: the 1999 standards, the AIMR sponsor, the Investment Performance Council (IPC), and the forthcoming "Gold GIPS" (effective 2006). **For current requirements use `gips-2020.md`.** Treat dates and specific requirements here as history and rationale. The EIPC attribution questions and disclosures below are still a useful review checklist.

## Why standards exist
In the mid-1980s US pension funds saw mostly "above-average" track records. Managers cherry-picked time periods, representative accounts and calculation methods. This led to the AIMR-PPS (1987) and, in the UK, the NAPF guidelines (1992). The two were sometimes contradictory, which drove the case for one global standard: GIPS, published in 1999. It's voluntary, based on **full disclosure and fair representation**, and backed by industry self-regulation.

**For clients:** comparable, trustworthy track records. Non-compliance may signal weak ethics or controls.

**For managers:** a marketing advantage (and in the US, a disadvantage if you're *not* compliant), an "international passport" and level playing field, professionalism, risk control (investigating composite outliers), and efficiency and data quality.

## Core mechanics (as described in the book)
- **Firm-wide claim:** the *firm* claims compliance, not a composite or a single presentation. All presentations must then be fair, so give every prospect a compliant presentation.
- **Composites:** every actual, fee-paying, discretionary portfolio is in at least one composite defined by strategy. This includes lost and terminated accounts, up to their last full period. Portfolios move between composites only on documented guideline changes, and the history stays with the old composite.
  - Composite returns are asset-weighted (beginning-of-period weights, or with cash flows). Equal weighting would let small, easily manipulated portfolios dominate.
  - Narrow or broad definitions: narrow is harder to administer but shows tighter dispersion. Bacon advises **starting narrow**, since merging later is easy. The composite creation date must be disclosed.
- **History:** at least 5 years, growing to 10 years. Annual returns. (Bacon recommends quarterly as well.)
- **Calculation:** time-weighted total return, geometrically linked, with monthly valuation (from 2001). The 2004 text anticipated valuation at each external cash flow from 2010.
  - True TWR and linked Modified Dietz are acceptable. The analyst's test, index substitution and regression are **not**. IRR is acceptable only for VC and private equity.
  - The firm must have a policy for large flows (e.g. ≥10% of assets triggers revaluation; the recommendation cites flows that distort the return by ±0.2%).
- **Five sections:** input data, calculation methodology, composite construction, disclosures, presentation and reporting. Each has requirements (mandatory) and recommendations.

### Selected requirements from the 1999 text (Appendix E)
- **Input data:** market values (not cost); trade-date accounting (2005); accrual accounting for fixed income, and for dividends from the ex-date (2005). Recommended: the same FX source for composite and benchmark.
- **Calculation:** total return including cash; returns after trading expenses; accrued income in both numerator and denominator; a minimum asset level, if set, applied consistently. Recommended: net of non-reclaimable withholding tax; consistent treatment of flows.
- **Composites:** carve-outs excluding cash can't form standalone composites. Cash must be allocated and the method disclosed. From 2005, carve-outs had to have their own cash. Simulated or model results can't be linked to actual results.
- **Disclosures:** firm definition and total firm assets; that a list of composites is available; settlement-date valuation if used; minimum asset level; currency; leverage and derivatives; gross or net of fees; withholding-tax treatment; percentage of assets outside the benchmark's countries; FX source inconsistencies; percentage of non-fee-paying portfolios; local-law conflicts; non-compliant pre-2000 periods; carve-out cash allocation.
- **Presentation:**
  - 5–10 years of annual returns
  - number of portfolios, composite assets and percentage of firm assets
  - a dispersion measure
  - the compliance statement
  - the creation date
  - benchmark returns for the same periods (or an explanation of why there are none), plus benchmark changes with dates and reasons, and the build and rebalancing rules for custom benchmarks
  - **no annualising of periods under one year**
  - prior-firm performance linked only if records and assets transfer and the process is unchanged
  - one year to bring acquired non-compliant assets into compliance

  Recommended: gross-of-fee returns, cumulative returns, equal-weighted means and medians, volatility, and risk measures (σ, TE, β, duration).

## Verification
- Done by an independent third party, **firm-wide** (no "verified composite" claims). It tests (a) composite construction across the whole firm, and (b) whether processes and procedures are designed to calculate and present compliant performance.
- At least one year. Bacon recommends annual verification and bringing the verifier in early (he sees no conflict in pre-compliance consulting). Costs range from thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars.
- Claiming compliance without verification is high-risk, both reputationally and with regulators.
- Required procedures include:
  - knowing the firm, GIPS and local rules
  - obtaining written policies (discretion, composite definitions, inclusion and exclusion timing, accruals, valuation, TWR method, flow timing, composite method, taxes, off-benchmark securities, leverage and derivatives)
  - testing the firm definition, composite completeness, discretionary classification, sampled account inclusion and exclusion timing, return recalculation, asset weighting and linking, dispersion, disclosures and record-keeping
  - obtaining a representation letter
- A separate, deeper **performance examination** of one composite is optional.

## Guidance-statement topics (as of 2004)
- **Definition of firm:** meaningful, rational and fair. It can't be drawn narrowly to exclude poor products. The broadest definition is recommended, but several "firms" within one organisation are allowed (e.g. by region) while working towards compliance. The "assets in one base currency" option ended in 2005.
- **Carve-outs:** Bacon advises avoiding them. Problems:
  - cash allocation (cash drags returns)
  - concentration risk
  - inseparable currency overlay
  - allocation bets taken relative to the *parent* benchmark
  - exaggerated bet sizes
  - administrative difficulty
- **Portability:** the record belongs to the firm. Linking prior-firm performance requires that substantially all decision makers moved, the team and process stayed intact, records support the numbers, substantially all the composite's assets moved, and this is disclosed. It's typically met only in mergers. Otherwise, show the prior record as supplemental information.
- **Supplemental information:** for example attribution, ex ante risk of a representative account, or risk-adjusted returns. It must follow the spirit of GIPS, not contradict the compliant presentation, be labelled supplemental (it isn't verified), and never be used to get around the requirements.

## Achieving and maintaining compliance (Bacon's advice)
- Senior management buy-in, a steering committee chaired by the sponsor, a sound plan with contingency, and firm-wide education. Client-facing staff are the usual failure point.
- Allow at least a year for a complex business. Six months is aggressive.
- Common struggles:
  - **Firm definition:** fee income helps prove every account was found.
  - **Composite definitions:** hard to change once set, so start narrow.
  - **Missing data:** especially for closed accounts.
- To maintain compliance: name an owner for presentation integrity, review quarterly, and verify annually. The touchstone is a **fair and honest representation of the firm's track record**.

## EIPC: 22 questions to ask of any attribution report (Appendix C, 2002)
1. Does the model follow the manager's decision process?
2. Is the benchmark appropriate and the official one?
3. Did the benchmark or style change during the period? (Attribute against the benchmark in force at the time.)
4. Did the model change? Why?
5. Is there an unexplained residual?
6. If so, is it shown, ignored, or allocated? (Ignoring it isn't good practice.)
7. Is interaction calculated?
8. If so, is it shown, ignored, allocated, and applied consistently over time?
9. Is the model arithmetic or geometric?
10. Is a smoothing or linking algorithm used? Which one?
11. Is it buy-and-hold or transaction-based?
12. How are weights defined? (Weighted returns must sum to the portfolio return.)
13. Is it genuinely multi-currency? Which FX rates? (Only attribute currency if there's a currency process. Use forward or hedged returns, because local returns can't be achieved.)
14. How are off-benchmark allocations treated?
15. Where do transaction costs sit? (Charge allocation-driven costs to allocation. Consider measuring the dealing desk.)
16. Gross or net of fees? (Net vs an unadjusted benchmark puts fees in selection.)
17. Is cash included, and is there a cash benchmark? (Cash is often a large bet.)
18. Is there gearing or leverage? Is it on an all-cash basis?
19. Are derivatives included, and how? (Use economic exposure if that matches the process.)
20. Is the data from the manager's records? Does it reconcile to the official return?
21. Which return methodology is used? (Large internal or external flows distort the effects. Higher frequency is better.)
22. If the base isn't a benchmark (e.g. a composite, model, carve-out or peer group), why?

## EIPC guidance on attribution presentation (Appendix D, 2004)
**Guiding principles:** return and risk attribution must follow the decision process and reflect the investment style, and must use an appropriate benchmark specified in advance (investable, transparent, measurable). If you can't meet these, keep the results internal and don't show them to clients or prospects.

**Required disclosures for return attribution** (where applicable):
- **Investment process:** the object of analysis (portfolio, representative account, model, composite) and why; the main decision factors; benchmark composition and rebalancing, past benchmarks and change dates; treatment of off-benchmark investments; the rationale if there's no benchmark.
- **Model:** a description (name and source if published; the revisions if it's a variant; a broad description if proprietary); the effects produced; model changes and their impact; arithmetic or geometric excess; the period and why it was chosen; calculation frequency; the linking method (available on request) and any smoothing; buy-and-hold vs transaction-based; treatment of interaction and residuals; derivatives; leverage; FX treatment relative to the currency strategy; cash and cash benchmark (strategic vs income cash); transaction costs and fees.
- **Input data:** portfolio and segment return methodology and frequency; gross or net treatment of fees, custody, taxes and costs; benchmark methodology, adjustments and source; actual or all-cash basis for leverage; price, FX source and timing differences; withholding-tax treatment of income.

**Risk attribution adds:**
- the risk measures and decomposition factors
- ex post and ex ante analysis, **reconciled with each other** to test the model
- the method behind ex ante estimates, and turnover and stability assumptions
- the reporting date
- the same benchmark and period as the return attribution, or a stated reason for differing

Firms claiming GIPS compliance are encouraged to follow this guidance. Attribution may be shown as supplemental information.
