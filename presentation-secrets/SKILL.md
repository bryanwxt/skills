# Presentation Secrets

**The thesis.** Most presenters *deliver information*. Jobs *created an experience*: a three-act play with a villain, a hero, a supporting cast, props, and one unforgettable moment, rehearsed until it looked effortless. None of it is innate charisma. It's a learnable craft in three acts: **create the story → deliver the experience → refine and rehearse.** The book divides these into 18 "scenes".

**The one question underneath everything:** *Why should I care?* Every headline, slide, number and demo has to answer it for this audience. Sell the dream and the benefit, not the widget.

## The 18 scenes at a glance

| Act | Scene | Core move |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Create the story** | 1 Plan in analog | Script and sketch on paper first, then build slides (most of the prep time goes on story, not slides) |
| | 2 Answer the one question | "Why should I care?" Benefit before technology |
| | 3 Messianic purpose | Lead with genuine passion and a mission to make lives better |
| | 4 Twitter-like headline | One ≤140-character line: specific, with a personal benefit, repeated everywhere |
| | 5 Road map | Rule of three: preview the points, deliver them, recap them |
| | 6 Antagonist | Establish the problem or pain ("Why do we need this?") before the solution |
| | 7 Conquering hero | Reveal the solution as the cure, in plain benefits |
| | *Intermission 1* | Change pace about every 10 minutes (video, demo, guest) |
| **2. Deliver the experience** | 8 Inner Zen | Visual slides, one idea each, no bullets, white space |
| | 9 Dress up numbers | Make every figure specific, relevant and contextual, often through analogy |
| | 10 Zippy words | Simple, concrete, emotional language. Kill jargon |
| | 11 Share the stage | Partners, customers, employees, reviews. Give credit |
| | 12 Props and demos | Short, simple, sweet, swift, substantial. Something for eye, ear and hand |
| | 13 The showstopper | One scripted, rehearsed moment that brings the headline to life |
| **3. Refine and rehearse** | 14 Stage presence | Eye contact, open posture, gestures, and vocal variety (pitch, pause, volume, pace) |
| | 15 Look effortless | Many hours of deliberate rehearsal, video review, bucket method for Q&A |
| | 16 Costume | Dress a little better than the audience, appropriate to the culture |
| | 17 Toss the script | Slides as prompter, one theme per slide, minimal notes |
| | 18 Have fun | Infotainment. Never apologise for glitches. Smile and move on |
| *Encore* | One more thing | An occasional closing surprise; end on the repeated headline |

## Choose the mode

- **Quick** (one-off: "punch up this pitch", "write a launch line", "review my slides"): use the quick rule below and don't open the references.
- **Build** (a new presentation or pitch end to end): follow Playbook A in `references/playbooks.md`. It runs through the three acts with a checkpoint after the story and before any slides are made.
- **Coach** (rehearsal, delivery, nerves, Q&A): Playbooks H–J.
- **Review** (critique an existing deck or talk): Playbook K's rubric.

## Quick rule

Apply these six checks and report what changed:
1. **Headline:** is there one sentence of 140 characters or fewer that says what it is and why the listener should care? Write it.
2. **Villain → hero:** is the problem established before the solution?
3. **Three:** are the points grouped into three (four at most), previewed and recapped?
4. **Human numbers:** is every number translated into something the audience knows ("1,000 songs in your pocket", not "5 GB")?
5. **Visual:** can each slide be one image plus a few words? Is there a demo, prop, or moment to show rather than tell?
6. **Plain words:** remove jargon and buzzwords, and replace abstractions with concrete, emotional language.

## Limits (say these when they apply)

- **Some of the book's science is shaky.** Treat these as rules of thumb, not facts. The 10-minute attention rule is anecdotal. Visual/auditory/kinesthetic learning styles aren't supported as a way to match teaching to learners, though variety still helps. The Mehrabian "93% non-verbal" figure is a misreading of studies about emotional ambiguity. "10,000 hours" is an oversimplification. Richard Mayer's multimedia principles are the well-supported part.
- **"No bullets, ever" is for spoken slides.** Documents, read-ahead decks, handouts and emailed decks need text. The book itself says bullets are fine in material people will read.
- **Hype needs a "there" there.** Superlatives ("insanely great", "amazing") work only when the claim is true and suits the speaker's brand. A financial adviser shouldn't promise a revolution.
- **Context changes the format.** Jobs ran 90-minute launch events with a captive fan audience. Board updates, decision memos and teaching sessions borrow the techniques (headline, three, numbers, visuals, rehearsal) but not the theatre.
- **Don't imitate Jobs's persona.** Copy the method, not the mannerisms, wardrobe or attacks on competitors. Gestures and enthusiasm must be authentic.
