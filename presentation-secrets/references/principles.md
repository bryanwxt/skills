# The 18 scenes, in depth

Each scene has the same layout: **Claim**, **Why**, **Techniques**, **Anti-patterns**, **Examples** (details in `cases.md`), **Caveats**. Foundations that apply throughout:
- **Presentation as theatre.** It has conflict, resolution, villains, heroes, a supporting cast, props and a climax, and it is staged and rehearsed.
- **It's about them, not you.** The audience asks one question: why should I care?
- **What are you really selling?** Not routers but human connection (Cisco). Not coffee but a "third place" (Starbucks). Not computers but tools that unleash human potential (Apple).
- **No naturals.** Jobs got better over 25+ years by working at it.
- **Software-agnostic.** Keynote vs PowerPoint is irrelevant. Story and delivery decide the outcome.

# ACT 1 — Create the story

## Scene 1. Plan in analog

**Claim.** Storyboard the plot on paper or a whiteboard before opening slide software. Write → sketch → produce.

**Why.** Slide templates push you toward titles and bullets, the opposite of what you need. Designers spend most of their prep time thinking, researching and sketching. Nancy Duarte's guideline was up to about 90 hours for a one-hour, 30-slide talk, with only about a third of that spent building slides. If you get the story right, the slides are easy. The napkin test asks whether the idea is simple enough to sketch on a napkin, like Southwest's three-city triangle or Cranium's "everyone gets to shine".

**Techniques.** Script first in prose, then sketch the visuals, then produce. Check the **nine elements** (glossary): headline, passion statement, three key messages, metaphors/analogies, demos, partners, customer evidence/endorsements, video clips, and props/flip charts/show-and-tell. Follow **Aristotle's persuasive outline**: arouse interest → pose the problem → offer the solution → describe the benefits → call to action ("Now go out and buy one"). Jobs's 2×2 product grid on a whiteboard (consumer/pro × desktop/portable) shows visual thinking in action.

**Anti-patterns.** Opening PowerPoint first. Agonising over fonts and bullets during planning. Treating the software as the main character instead of the speaker.

**Caveats.** The hour counts are a designer's ideal. Scale them to the stakes, but keep the ratio: story before slides.

## Scene 2. Answer the one question that matters most

**Claim.** The audience is silently asking "Why should I care?" Answer it early, often and clearly, and explain the *why* before the *how*.

**Why.** The brain is lazy and conserves energy. If listeners have to work out the benefit themselves, they leave. Jobs connected the dots with "what that means is…".

**Techniques.**
- Start from the customer experience and work back to the technology (Jobs, WWDC 1997, paraphrased).
- State the benefit plainly: makes money, saves money, makes a task easier or more enjoyable.
- Translate features through an analogy, as in the Core 2 Duo sales script: two brains in your computer means you can download music while running a virus scan without slowing down.
- Make the one thing consistent across the talk, website, press release and marketing.
- Repeat it at least twice.

**Anti-patterns.** Jargon openers ("premier developer of intelligent semiconductor IP solutions…"). "Look at us" press releases about appointments, logos and awards that no reader cares about. The fix for the chip-IP CEO was to ask everyone to hold up their phones and say "our software builds the chips in these, which is why your phone will get smaller and last longer".

**Examples.** The iMac 1998 framing (internet "simply and fast"). The Intel switch ("we can't build the products we envision with the PowerPC road map"). Benefit lines for Keynote, iPod nano, Time Capsule, iPhone 3G and Genius.

## Scene 3. Develop a messianic sense of purpose

**Claim.** Charisma comes from passion for a mission: changing the world, or the customer's life, for the better. It doesn't come from money.

**Why.** Passion is contagious and gives you the persistence to stick with something. Jobs said he never did it for the money. Great leaders "rally people to a better future" (Buckingham). Built to Last found that purpose beyond profit inspires people.

**Techniques.**
- Write a **passion statement**: "I'm excited about this because it ___." Share it openly.
- Find what you're really passionate about. It's usually the improvement in customers' lives, not the product (Schultz barely mentions coffee).
- Paint a vivid future and invite people to help build it (the Sculley pitch: sugared water versus a chance to change the world).

**Anti-patterns.** Mission statements by committee. Hiding emotion because it feels unprofessional.

**Caveats.** The book says that if you don't love your work you won't inspire, and suggests considering a change. That's strong advice. For an agent, the practical move is to help the speaker find the genuine source of their enthusiasm within the topic.

## Scene 4. Create Twitter-like headlines

**Claim.** Write the one-sentence headline yourself, before anything else. Keep it to 140 characters or fewer, make it specific and memorable with a personal benefit, and repeat it in every channel.

**Why.** Audiences and the press look for a category and a one-line description, so do the work for them. Journalists then reuse it word for word ("the world's thinnest notebook" turned up in about 30,000 search results). Investors ask for a one-liner. Google pitched Sequoia with "access to the world's information in one click". One investor said that if you can't say what you do in ten words or less, he isn't interested.

**Techniques.**
- Use subject-verb-object form: "Today Apple reinvents the phone."
- Test it: is it concise, specific (1,000 songs), and does it carry a personal benefit (in your pocket)?
- Make the slide text mirror the spoken headline.
- Pattern: headline → one-sentence expansion → demo or details → repeat the headline. Jobs said "reinvent the phone" five times in one keynote.
- Make it consistent across keynote, slide, interviews, home page, press release and ads (the MacBook Air table).
- Use USA Today headlines as models.

**Examples.** "1,000 songs in your pocket". "The world's thinnest notebook". "Twice as fast at half the price". "The industry's greenest notebooks". "iMac: the excitement of the Internet, the simplicity of Macintosh". "Keynote: for when your presentation really counts". Outside Apple: Cisco "changes the way we live, work, play and learn". Starbucks "a third place between work and home". Gates "a PC on every desk, in every home".

## Scene 5. Draw a road map (rule of three)

**Claim.** Give a verbal agenda, usually in three parts. Outline the points, cover each one, summarise each one, then recap.

**Why.** Short-term memory holds about three or four items. Three is a deep pattern in stories, comedy and rhetoric (three bears, three musketeers, "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness"). The book cites the US Marines organising in threes. Reporters notice when you promise three points and deliver two, and they take notes.

**Techniques.**
- List every possible point, then categorise until you have three big messages. Under each, add stories, facts, examples, analogies and endorsements.
- No agenda slide is needed. Say the road map out loud, and give each item a minimal slide (just "1", "2", "3", "4").
- Build intensity across the three (first transition "good", second "even bigger", third "bigger still").
- Use threes inside sentences too (Obama, JFK via Sorensen: "write for the ear").
- Media prep: headline, then three ideas, then supporting material for each, and repeat the headline at least twice.

**Examples.** The three iPhone "products" that turn out to be one device. Mac's three transitions. The three-legged stool (Mac, music, iPhone). Four topics at Macworld 2008. Stanford's "three stories". DuPont asking each employee for three cash-saving actions. Jimmy V's "laugh, think, cry".

**Caveats.** Four is acceptable, and Jobs sometimes used four. Never go much past that.

## Scene 6. Introduce the antagonist

**Claim.** Every great story needs a villain: a competitor, the status quo, or a customer pain. Establish the problem before revealing the solution.

**Why.** The brain wants meaning before details: "Will it eat me?" before "How many teeth does it have?" (Medina). Investors want the problem set up first, which "creates a cup to pour the coffee into". A shared enemy unites an audience, much as religions do (Lindstrom).

**Techniques.**
- Ask the rhetorical question: "Why do we need this?" or "Why do we need our own browser?"
- Describe the current state of the industry and its failings concretely. Build up the pain and make it tangible.
- Raise objections to your own solution and answer them ("A stylus? No. Who wants a stylus?").
- **Four-question elevator pitch:** what do you do? What problem do you solve? How are you different? Why should I care? One sentence each.

**Examples.** The 1984 ad and "Big Blue". Smartphones "not so smart and not so easy to use". Safari vs IE on speed and innovation. TravelMuse: booking is the last 5% of trip planning, and the other 95% is the real work. LanguageLine. Al Gore's CO₂ graph and lift.

## Scene 7. Reveal the conquering hero

**Claim.** After the villain, reveal your product or idea as the hero that frees the audience. The hero's job is to make lives better, not just to beat the enemy. State its benefits plainly.

**Techniques.**
- Lay out the landscape and its trade-offs. The iPod example ran through CD player $5/song, flash $10/song, MP3 CD $1/song and hard drive 30¢/song, then said "that's where we want to be" and introduced the iPod.
- Underline the hero's unique quality ("your entire music library fits in your pocket, never possible before").
- Do it fast if needed: in the iPhone app ads, the problem and solution take 30 seconds ("Solving life's dilemmas one app at a time").
- Sell the experience, not the specs ("why you'll love a Mac" rather than a spec list).
- Passion for a wrong you want to right gives you the perseverance (Jobs, Smithsonian interview).

**Examples.** The 1983 Big Blue history speech. Mac vs PC (Microsoft's "I'm a PC" response had no villain and less punch). MacBook Pro solving the "G5 won't fit in a PowerBook" problem, with a gag photo of the pope as the "higher authority" they'd consulted.

**Caveats.** Villain framing can read as smug (some found Mac vs PC smug). Keep it good-natured, and make the villain the *problem* more often than a named rival.

## Intermission 1. The ten-minute rule

Audiences tune out after about ten minutes (Medina's classroom observation). Insert a change of pace (video, demo, guest speaker or story) at roughly that interval. Jobs played the iPod Silhouettes ad at minute ten of Macworld 2007, as a break between the music act and the Apple TV act. *Caveat: the number is folk wisdom. The point that variety sustains attention holds up.*

# ACT 2 — Deliver the experience

## Scene 8. Channel their inner Zen

**Claim.** Slides should be simple, visual and free of bullets: one theme per slide, lots of white space, and pictures over text. The slide supports the speaker. It isn't a document.

**Why.**
- **Picture superiority effect:** about 10% recall after 72 hours for oral information alone, rising to about 65% with a picture added (Medina).
- **Mayer's multimedia principles:** words + pictures beat words alone. Put words next to the relevant parts of pictures (contiguity). Narrate rather than put text on screen (split attention). Leave out extraneous words and pictures (coherence). Use arrows or highlights to direct the eye (signalling).
- The average slide has about 40 words. Jobs's first four slides at Macworld 2008 had seven words in total.
- Bullets tell the audience "write this down", which pulls attention away from you. Text that duplicates your speech hurts (Reynolds: "slideuments").
- Kanso (Zen simplicity): "eliminate the unnecessary so the necessary may speak" (Hofmann).

**Techniques.**
- One idea per slide, and make the on-slide words match the spoken words ("Music" while saying "let's talk about music").
- Use photos instead of feature lists. The iPhone 3G took eleven slides, ten of them photos, rather than one bullet slide.
- Use line drawings and arrows for processes (the Genius cloud diagram).
- Show the striking picture alone (MacBook Air on an envelope).
- Make product claims short ("industry's greenest notebooks" instead of a list of compliance details).
- Write in plain English throughout. Learn from Plain English Campaign before/afters and USA Today Snapshots.
- If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough (attributed to Einstein). Shell's CEO said leaders should be able to explain where they're going in two minutes.

**Anti-patterns.** Title + bullet + sub-bullet + sub-sub-bullet. Mixed fonts. Spec dumps. Filling every centimetre.

**Caveats.** Visual slides demand *more* speaker preparation, because the slide can't be your crutch. Send detail separately (Apple sent people to its website for specs).

## Scene 9. Dress up your numbers

**Claim.** Numbers mean nothing without context. Make them **specific, relevant and contextual**, and use analogies to make them vivid.

**Techniques.**
- Translate units into user terms: 5 GB → 1,000 songs. 30 GB → 7,500 songs, 25,000 photos or 75 hours of video.
- Break totals down: 4M iPhones ÷ 200 days = 20,000 a day. Then compare to the market: second place behind RIM, and equal to the next three competitors combined.
- Reframe with a comparison: 5% market share is more than BMW or Mercedes have in cars.
- Show the number happening: a 3G vs EDGE page loading side by side, 21 s vs 59 s.
- Use analogies of scale: a petaflop = a 1.5-mile stack of laptops. Transistor count → Ithaca vs Europe shrunk to Ithaca's size. The $700B bailout = 3.5B iPhones, or $2,300 for every American. Blood pressure 220/140 → explain what it means for the patient ("you could drop dead at any minute").
- Use few numbers, all in support of the theme.

**Anti-patterns.** Spewing figures with no context. Assuming the audience shares your excitement about a spec.

## Scene 10. Use "amazingly zippy" words

**Claim.** Use simple, concrete, emotional words. Speak on stage the way you'd talk at the watercooler. Kill jargon.

**Why.** Readability analysis (Seattle P-I) showed Jobs's keynotes at about 10–14 words per sentence with a fog index of about 5.5–6.8, against Gates's 18–22 words and 9.4–10.7. Jargon hides meaning and signals insecurity ("Insecure managers create complexity", Jack Welch). Suze Orman: simplicity is respect for the listener, and complexity often comes from fear of seeming unimportant.

**Techniques.**
- Use the three qualities: **simple** (few syllables, no jargon), **concrete** (specific, tangible), **emotional** (vivid adjectives).
- Use analogies to familiar things: Apple TV as a "DVD player for the 21st century", iPod shuffle "smaller than a pack of gum", iPod "the size of a deck of cards". Once an analogy works, keep using it.
- Use superlatives *authentically*: "amazing", "gorgeous", "screamer", "buttons you'll want to lick". Hype is only hype when there's no "there" there.
- Run text through a readability tool and edit, edit, edit.
- Pitch the way Pogue wants pitches: one short paragraph saying what it is and does (a laptop that survives six-foot drops, dunking and 300° heat).

**Anti-patterns.** "Best-of-breed", "synergy", "consumer-centric", "place-based out-of-home digital network" (which meant video screens in delis). Mission statements written by committee.

**Caveats.** Fit your enthusiasm to your field. A regulated or conservative context needs measured but still plain and emotional language (the mutual fund example).

## Scene 11. Share the stage

**Claim.** A great presentation is a symphony, not a one-man show. Bring on partners, experts, customers and employees, and praise people publicly.

**Why.** The brain craves variety, and speechwriters kept political speeches to about 20 minutes. Third parties add credibility, and word of mouth is the strongest influence on purchases. Knowing what you don't know adds authority (Jony Ive explaining the unibody).

**Techniques.**
- Have partner CEOs on stage (Otellini in a bunny suit, Fox's Gianopulos), or appearing by video or webcam (Madonna, Gates in 1997).
- Show customer testimonials, ideally in person, otherwise on video of two minutes or less, otherwise as a quote.
- Read short quotes from respected reviewers (Mossberg, Pogue, Baig, and "PC Magazine" for an ironic laugh).
- Give credit: ask the team to stand, thank their families, thank customers.
- Recruit beta customers who will vouch for you, and give them a reason (access, input, visibility).
- Self-deprecating fun works too (Noah Wyle as "Steve").

**Anti-patterns.** Hogging the spotlight. A guest who hasn't rehearsed (see Scene 14, Stan Sigman).

## Scene 12. Stage your presentation with props

**Claim.** Demos and props turn claims into experiences. Use them in every presentation and plan them in from the start.

**Kawasaki's five qualities of a demo:** **short**, **simple** (one or two messages), **sweet** (the hottest, most differentiating feature, passing the "so what?" test), **swift** (no step longer than about 15 seconds), **substantial** (solves a real problem).

**Techniques.**
- Focus on one feature, like a movie trailer. Safari vs IE7 page loads took about 3 minutes in total. The GarageBand podcast studio was built in four steps.
- Sit at stage right, angled so you can still see the audience, and explain each action as you take it.
- Pass physical objects around (unibody frames), then stay silent and let the product speak.
- Have fun and commit to it: the Starbucks prank call, Photo Booth "teenage effects".
- Bring in someone who speaks to the target audience (John Mayer for GarageBand, teens demoing a teen website).
- Hide a surprise in the demo ("this system I've been using… is running on Intel").
- Give the audience something to *do*, use volunteers, and use your own unique skills (Montemagno).
- Online: polls, annotation, handing over mouse control.
- Have a "chief demonstration officer" sidekick if useful (Cisco's Jim Grubb).

**Caveats.** The book's visual/auditory/kinesthetic framing (≈40% visual and so on) isn't solid science. Keep the practical takeaway, which is to mix seeing, hearing and doing.

## Scene 13. Reveal a "holy shit" moment

**Claim.** Script one emotionally charged moment that brings the headline to life. People forget what you said but remember how you made them feel (attributed to Maya Angelou).

**Why.** Emotionally charged events trigger dopamine, which acts as a "remember this" Post-it note (Medina). The moment becomes the photo, the headline and the story people retell.

**Techniques.**
- Find the *one theme* the audience should recall without notes, then find a way to make it physical or dramatic: the MacBook Air out of an envelope, the iPod out of a pocket, the Mac speaking for itself out of a bag.
- Build up to it instead of opening with it. Layer the reveal: "three products… are you getting it? One device."
- Plan it across channels in advance (press images, ads, website).
- A moment doesn't need a product. A personal story works (the organic farmer who can now hug his kids straight from the field). So does unexpected news (dropping "interim" from the CEO title).
- Rehearse it until it's crisp. A failed big moment is worse than none.

## Intermission 2. Schiller learns from the best

At Macworld 2009, Phil Schiller replaced Jobs and used the toolkit successfully: a theme up front ("Today is all about the Mac"), a road map ("3 new things"), dressed-up numbers (3.4M store visitors a week = 100 Macworlds), demos, sharing the stage (engineers and a battery video), visual slides, and a "one more thing" (DRM-free iTunes) saved for last. **Lesson: the method is transferable.**

# ACT 3 — Refine and rehearse

## Scene 14. Master stage presence

**Claim.** How you say it matters as much as what you say. Delivery should match the strength of the content.

**Body language.**
- **Eye contact:** glance at the slide, then return to the audience. Hide notes. Visual slides make this possible.
- **Open posture:** no lectern and no crossed arms. During demos, sit side-on to the computer.
- **Gestures:** purposeful and frequent, never hands stuck at your sides or in your pockets. McNeill's research ties gesture to clear thinking. Stay authentic and don't copy mannerisms.

**Voice.**
- **Inflection:** raise and lower pitch on key words.
- **Pauses:** the most dramatic tool. Pause before and after the reveal and let the room breathe.
- **Volume:** lower it to build up to the big line, then raise it (or the reverse: whisper the key line).
- **Rate:** normal pace for demos, slower for headlines.
- Mark up a script with emphasis, pauses and volume (the book's iPhone and iPod delivery tables).

**Anti-patterns.** "Mr. Note Card": AT&T's Stan Sigman reading a monotone script with his hands in his pockets, six minutes that felt like thirty. Ben Stein's "Bueller?" monotone.

**Caveats.** The book leans on Mehrabian ("words are least important"), which overstates it. Delivery strongly colours *how a message is received*. It doesn't replace content.

## Scene 15. Make it look effortless

**Claim.** Effortless delivery comes from relentless, deliberate rehearsal. Jobs rehearsed for days, and teams spent hundreds of hours on a five-minute demo.

**Techniques.**
- Rehearse every slide, demo, light cue and transition, with full dress rehearsals.
- Use **deliberate practice** (Ericsson): specific goals, feedback, iteration.
- **Video review:** record the first five minutes, watch it with someone honest, and check eye contact, body language, filler words, vocal variety and energy.
- **Energy scale:** most people rate themselves 3–6 out of 10. Rehearse "over the top", which usually lands about right.
- **Filler words:** ask for feedback, have someone tap a glass at every "um", watch the recording.
- **Bucket method for tough Q&A:** list the likely questions, group them into about 7 categories, write the best answer for each category (one that works however the question is phrased), listen for the trigger word, then answer with eye contact. Kissinger: "What questions do you have for my answers?"
- **Contingency drills:** rehearse what happens if the clicker or computer fails. The CEO who kept going without slides succeeded because he'd practised.
- **Nerves:** prepare to the extreme, arrive early, test audio and lights, and shift the focus from "me" to "we" (what it means for them).

**Anti-patterns.** Spending $25,000 on slick slides and none on rehearsal, then losing your place in front of the audience.

**Caveats.** "10,000 hours" and "no naturals" are motivational oversimplifications. The actionable part is: practise far more than you think you need to.

## Scene 16. Wear the appropriate costume

**Claim.** Dress a little better than everyone else, appropriate to the culture. Dress like the leader you want to become.

**Notes.** Jobs wore suits and a bow tie in 1984, a Brioni suit to the bank for NeXT, and a mock turtleneck once he *was* the brand. Don't copy the uniform unless you've earned it. If you dress like a rebel, dress like a well-off one.

## Scene 17. Toss the script

**Claim.** Talk to the audience, not your notes. Visual slides act as your teleprompter, with one theme per slide.

**The five steps:**
1. Write the script in full sentences in the speaker notes (4–5 sentences per slide).
2. Highlight the key word in each sentence and rehearse.
3. Delete everything except the key words and rehearse again.
4. Memorise the *one idea* per slide.
5. Rehearse with no notes, using the visual as the prompt.

You'll have rehearsed each slide at least four times.

**When notes are a must** (step-by-step demos): use 3–4 large-type key words per section, discreetly placed. Jobs's iPhone Maps demo notes were five lines, including the "scripted" Starbucks gag. Joel Osteen glances at notes on a lectern but never stands behind it.

## Scene 18. Have fun

**Claim.** Presentations are **infotainment**. Enjoy them, and the audience will too. Nothing goes "wrong" unless you let it derail you.

**Techniques.**
- Commit to the humour (the OS 9 funeral with a casket and eulogy, the "Hell froze over" slide for iTunes on Windows, "reports of my death are greatly exaggerated").
- When something fails: acknowledge it lightly, smile, maybe tell a story while it's fixed (Jobs's TV-jammer anecdote during the clicker failure), explain what they would have seen, and move on ("It's pretty awesome when it works").
- **Never apologise** or draw attention to a glitch nobody noticed.
- Keep the enthusiasm even when the demo fails.

## Encore. One more thing

Occasionally save a final surprise for the end, but not every time, or it stops surprising. The Stanford speech shows the whole method without slides: a road map of "three stories", passion, humour about a grave subject, anaphora (repeating the start of sentences: "Don't… Don't… Don't…"), and a closing headline repeated three times ("Stay hungry, stay foolish"). **The final lesson: believe in yourself and your story.**
