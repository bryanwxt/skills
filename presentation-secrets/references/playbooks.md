# Playbooks

Scene numbers refer to `principles.md`. Ask only for inputs you can't infer: **audience**, **goal/action**, **time slot**, **setting** (live stage, meeting room, video call, or a deck that will be read), and **what's genuinely new or exciting**.

---

## A. Build a presentation end to end

1. **Frame (Scenes 2–3).** Write down: the audience, what they care about, the one action you want, and *what you're really selling* (the life improvement, not the product). Write the passion statement: "I'm excited about this because ___."
2. **Headline (Scene 4).** Use Playbook B.
3. **Story spine (Scenes 5–7, Aristotle).** Hook → antagonist (the problem/pain, made tangible) → hero (the solution) → three key messages with their benefits → call to action. Fill in the **nine elements** checklist (glossary) and mark which you'll use.
4. **Checkpoint: show the story in text** (headline, road map, villain/hero, three messages, the showstopper idea) and get agreement before building any slides. *Story before slides* (Scene 1).
5. **Pacing (Intermission 1).** Plan a change of pace about every 10 minutes: demo, video (≤2–3 min), guest, story or activity.
6. **Slides (Scene 8).** Use Playbook D: one theme per slide, visuals, words that match the speech.
7. **Numbers (Scene 9).** Run every figure through Playbook E.
8. **Words (Scene 10).** Do a jargon sweep and add one sticky analogy per complex idea.
9. **Cast (Scene 11).** Line up customer or partner voices, review quotes, and thanks to the team.
10. **Demo (Scene 12) and showstopper (Scene 13).** Use Playbooks F and G.
11. **Rehearse (Scenes 14–17).** Use Playbook H, and Playbook I for Q&A.
12. **Close.** Repeat the headline, give the call to action, and add an optional "one more thing".

**Output:** a one-page story brief, a slide-by-slide script table (*Spoken words | Slide visual | Delivery note*), and a rehearsal plan.

---

## B. Write the headline and messaging

1. Draft 5–10 candidates. Each must be 140 characters or fewer, in subject-verb-object form, specific (a number, a superlative or a concrete image), and carry a personal benefit.
2. Score each one: concise? specific? benefit to the listener? memorable when spoken aloud? Could a journalist paste it into an article as-is?
3. Pick one. Write the one-sentence expansion ("What it does is…").
4. Write the **four-question elevator pitch**: what do you do / what problem do you solve / how are you different / why should I care.
5. Write the three key messages, each with its supporting devices (story, fact, example, analogy, endorsement).
6. Check consistency: the same headline should appear on the slide, in the talk (2–5 times), the press release, the website hero, and the email subject.

**Output:** the headline, expansion, elevator pitch, three messages, and a channel consistency table.

---

## C. Short pitch (investor, demo day, sales, 3–10 min)

1. Category and scale (one line). 2. Problem: "the cup for the coffee", made vivid. 3. Solution as hero, plus a headline. 4. Demo or proof (one feature, under 2 min). 5. Three reasons it wins. 6. Evidence: customers, numbers in context. 7. Ask.

Cut everything else. If the idea doesn't fit in about 6–8 minutes, refine the idea (DEMO and TechCrunch logic).

---

## D. Turn a text-heavy deck into visual slides

For each slide:
1. Find its **one idea**. If there are several, split the slide.
2. Move the text into speaker notes (full sentences).
3. Pick a visual that *is* the idea: a photo, a simple diagram with arrows, one big number, or a few words.
4. Keep the on-screen words to a handful that echo what you'll say.
5. Add signalling (an arrow or highlight) for anything detailed, and keep plenty of white space.
6. A feature list becomes a sequence of slides, one photo per feature.

Keep a separate leave-behind document with the detail, where bullets are fine.

**Output:** a before/after table per slide (*Old content | One idea | New visual | Words on slide | Notes*).

---

## E. Dress up a number

For each figure, answer:
- **Specific:** use exact, concrete figures (7,500 songs, not "thousands").
- **Relevant:** what unit does this audience live in (songs, lattes, salaries, seconds waiting)?
- **Contextual:** compare against the market, a rival, the past, or a familiar benchmark (BMW's share).
- **Break it down or scale it:** per day, per person, or "stacked laptops 1.5 miles high".
- **Show it:** can it be demonstrated side by side (page-load race)?
- **So what:** state the consequence in plain words (the doctor's "you could drop dead").

Then cut any number that doesn't support the theme.

## F. Design a demo

1. Choose the *one* feature that's hottest, most differentiating, and solves a real problem.
2. Script it in 15-second beats, saying each action out loud ("I just push here, and boom").
3. Add one moment of fun or surprise, and one silent beat to let the result land.
4. Decide who demos: you, an expert, or someone from the target audience.
5. Plan the staging: sit side-on and keep eye contact. Keep 3–5 key-word notes out of sight.
6. Prepare a fallback: a recorded video, a second device, and a line to say if it fails.
7. Check against Kawasaki: short, simple, sweet, swift, substantial.

---

## G. Script the showstopper

1. State the one theme the audience must remember tomorrow.
2. Brainstorm ways to make it physical, visual, surprising or personal (an object from an envelope or pocket, a side-by-side race, a guest walk-on, a personal story, a twist reveal).
3. Write the build-up: previous milestones → rising expectation → the twist ("three things… one device").
4. Line up the other channels (photo op, slide, press line).
5. Rehearse the timing, lights, props and fallback until it's crisp.

---

## H. Rehearsal plan

| Pass | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | The five steps to tossing the script (full notes → key words → one idea per slide → no notes) |
| 5 | Record the first 5 minutes and review eye contact, posture, gestures, fillers, vocal variety and energy (aim for 7–9/10) |
| 6 | Mark up the script with emphasis, pauses, volume and pace for the headline and reveal |
| 7 | Rehearse demos and transitions with the tech, and drill a failure (clicker dead, demo frozen) |
| 8 | Full dress rehearsal at the venue: lights, audio, clothing (a notch above the audience, fitting the culture) |

For guests on stage: insist they rehearse too. No reading from cards.

---

## I. Prepare for tough Q&A (bucket method)

1. List every likely question, especially hostile ones.
2. Group them into buckets. There are usually about 7 (price, competition, risk, timeline, and so on).
3. Write one strong answer per bucket that works however the question is worded and leads back to your message.
4. Note the trigger words that map a question to its bucket.
5. Practise with someone firing reworded questions. Answer with eye contact, confidently, and briefly.

---

## J. Recover from a glitch (live)

Acknowledge it lightly, without apology ("Looks like it's not serving photos on that one"), and smile. Fill the time if needed (a short story, or what they would have seen), hand the fix to someone else, and move on with full enthusiasm. If nobody noticed, say nothing.
